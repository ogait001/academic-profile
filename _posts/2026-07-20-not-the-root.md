## I. The Pulpit Move

There is a sermon preached in some version almost everywhere, and it goes like this: Satan is the root. He is the source of the addiction, the affair, the rage, the theft. Confess that a battle is underway, resist the enemy, and the sin retreats with him. There is real pastoral comfort in this framing — it externalizes shame, it names an opponent, it gives the sufferer someone other than himself to fight. And there is real biblical warrant behind parts of it: scripture does not deny an adversary who tempts, accuses, and blinds.

But comfort and accuracy are not the same test, and this essay argues that the pulpit has quietly let one stand in for the other. Satan is not the root. He is, at most, the tempter — and the difference between root and tempter is not a fine point of vocabulary. It is the difference between a theology that can still ask a person to confess and one that has already excused him before he opens his mouth.

<br>

## II. Eve's Own Verbs

Go back to the text that supposedly settles the matter in the tempter's favor. Eve, confronted, says the serpent deceived her, and she ate. Read carefully: she names the deception and she names her own action in the same breath, and the second half is not absorbed into the first. She does not say the serpent made her eat. She says he deceived her, and she ate — two clauses, two subjects, one after the other, neither one canceling the other.

The verse just before it is more revealing still. She saw that the tree was good for food, and that it was a delight to the eyes, and that the tree was to be desired to make one wise; she took of its fruit and ate. Four verbs — saw, took, ate, gave — and every one of them hers. The serpent supplies a sentence. Eve supplies the seeing, the wanting, the reaching, the eating. If the text wanted to say the serpent overrode her will, it had the vocabulary to say so. It says something narrower and more exacting: he spoke, and she moved.

This is the distinction the classical tradition guarded with care. The serpent supplies the suggestion; concupiscence supplies the attraction; the will supplies the consent. James states the same sequence almost as an outline: each person is tempted when he is lured and enticed by his own desire, and then desire, having conceived, gives birth to sin. The tempter and the desire are real, but neither is yet the sin; the sin waits on the third term. And that middle term — the disordered wanting on which the serpent's sentence catches — is not how the will was made. Christianity does not claim the will was created bent. It claims it became bent, and that the bending is now inherited rather than chosen fresh by each person. The tree is not the cause of the hunger; it is only what the hunger, already present, reaches toward.

## III. David and the Roof

Turn to the story this series has already used once, because it bears repeating with the text in view. David rises from his bed, walks on the roof of his house, and sees a woman bathing. Nothing supernatural intervenes at that moment. No serpent, no whisper is recorded. He inquires who she is. He sends for her. She comes to him. He lies with her. When the news of the pregnancy reaches him, he does not repent — he arranges for her husband's death, first by summons, then, when that fails, by a letter carried by the husband's own hand ordering his own exposure at the fiercest point of the battle.

Count the deliberate acts in that sequence: the look that lingers past the first glance, the inquiry, the summons, the act itself, the attempted cover-up, the murder disguised as military necessity. Not one of these requires demonic assistance to explain. Each one is exactly the kind of decision an unremarkable, unafflicted will is capable of producing on its own, given enough power and enough silence. The text does not need Satan to make David's arithmetic add up, and neither, honestly, does experience.

<br>

## IV. What the Locked Door Does Not Change

Consider two scenes side by side. One is public: a shooter walks into a crowded place and the whole world watches the sin happen in the open, and no one doubts, in that moment, whose will produced it. The other is private: a man behind a locked door, alone with a screen, forming a desire that no camera will ever record. The pulpit's instinct is often to treat these as different categories — the first as evil, the second as merely weak, or merely tempted, or merely human.

*Against You Alone* already named the deeper standard: the sin's primary offense is not against the witnesses, present or absent. David wrote, against you, you only, have I sinned — not against Uriah first, not against Bathsheba first, though the wrong against them was real, but against the God before whom the roof and the locked door are equally lit. A teaching that speaks fluently about visible atrocity and goes quiet about invisible lust has not found a doctrinal distinction. It has found an audience it is more comfortable confronting.

<br>

## V. Possession Requires Abdication

The strongest form of the pulpit's claim reaches for the extreme case: possession. If a man's body can be occupied and spoken through by something not himself, does that not prove the root, in its fullest form, is external after all?

*The Serpent, the Self, and the Collapse of the "I"* already worked through this more carefully than a sermon usually allows. The man called Legion did not wake up one morning invaded without cause; the text situates him already among the tombs, already cutting himself, already outside the community that might have held him at the center. Occupation, on this account, is not force applied against a standing will — it is what moves into a will that has already vacated its own house. The abandonment precedes the opposition. Even the most extreme case in scripture does not describe a fortress stormed; it describes a house left unguarded, and then unoccupied by its rightful tenant, room by room, until something else moved in.

Nothing here denies that extraordinary forms of demonic oppression exist. The claim is narrower: scripture never presents ordinary human sin as requiring extraordinary demonic causation. The exceptional case remains exceptional precisely because it is not the account of how the affair, the theft, or the rage usually comes to be.

This is not a smaller claim than the pulpit's. It is a harder one. A stormed fortress can console itself — it did everything it could. A house left unguarded cannot.

<br>

## VI. Why the Root Is a Comfortable Place to Stop

It is worth asking honestly why the root-teaching preaches so well. An external enemy is easier to hate than an internal will, and hating the enemy feels like a kind of victory even when nothing in the man himself has changed. A congregation told to bind the spirit of lust leaves feeling armed. A congregation told the lust was never anyone's but its own leaves feeling exposed. The first sermon is more comfortable to preach and more comfortable to hear. It is not, on that account, more true.

There is a cost to the comfortable version, and it is not abstract. If Satan is the root, then confession becomes a report on enemy activity rather than an admission of personal direction — and a report on enemy activity does not require the word I. *Against You Alone* already identified the one sin that arrives at Judgement unbowed, still certain, still composed: pride, the will that will not confess because it has never located anything in itself to confess. A theology that quietly relocates the will's guilt onto an external tempter, sermon after sermon, is training that same posture into people who think they are doing the opposite of pride. The result looks like spiritual warfare. It functions like an alibi.

<br>

## VII. What the Tempter Still Is

None of this requires denying that a tempter exists, or that his whisper carries real force, or that scripture takes him seriously enough to name him the accuser, the deceiver, the one who prowls. *Not Gepetto* already drew the line this essay depends on: the tempter supplies the occasion; the will supplies the turning. Occasion is not cause. The tempter can propose; he cannot perform the act of willing for another. He can hold the fruit in the sentence and make it shine, but the reach is never his to make. A man can be genuinely tempted — genuinely, forcefully, even supernaturally tempted — and the sentence can still end with him.

The serpent's curve, as this series has described it elsewhere, loops and circles but never crosses the center; it has no present tense, no I am of its own. It can only offer a trajectory. Whether anyone steps onto that trajectory happens at the crossing point, and the crossing point belongs to the will standing there, not to the voice that pointed toward it.

Christ Himself put the matter where this essay has tried to keep it. Asked what defiles a person, He did not answer from without but from within: out of the heart of man come the evil thoughts, the murders, the adulteries, the coveting, the envy, the pride. He does not say these things come from Satan. He says they come from within — which makes Mark 7 something close to the New Testament's own reading of Genesis 3. The mouth of the tempter is not named among the sources; the heart is.

This is why Christianity has always distinguished temptation from sin itself. Christ was tempted in every respect as we are, yet without sin. Temptation therefore cannot itself be guilt. The decisive moment is neither the whisper nor the desire but the consent of the will. Only there does temptation become sin, and only there can repentance begin — which means the person tormented by the thought he did not want, and did not take, has not yet sinned in being tormented. The tempter has reached him. He has not yet answered.

<br>

## Coda

Eve did not say the serpent made her eat. David did not summon a demon to the roof of his house. The man among the tombs was not ambushed in a fortress but abandoned in a house he had already left unguarded. In every case scripture troubles itself to distinguish the whisper from the deed, the occasion from the choice — a distinction the comfortable sermon collapses and the honest one has to keep. Sin presupposes will. Take away every tempter that ever lived and the human capacity to reach for what was forbidden would still be standing at the crossing point, waiting, as it always has, for someone to say yes.

That yes is never wrung from the will alone. Grace does not replace freedom; it heals it — reaching the crossing point the tempter can only point toward, and steadying the very faculty that has to answer. The root of human sin was never on your belly, moving through the grass. It was always at the center, where the I am either answers or abdicates — and where, by grace, it can also be made able to answer at last.
