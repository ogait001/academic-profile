## I. A Question That Refuses Its Own Grammar

Retribuo means to pay back, to restore in kind, to render an equivalent for what was received. The verb presupposes an economy: two parties, a transfer in one direction, and a return transfer that answers it, if not in the same coin then in coin of comparable weight. The Psalmist's question borrows that grammar deliberately -- pro omnibus quae retribuit mihi, for everything He has rendered to me -- and in doing so states, before it can be answered, the condition that makes it unanswerable. An economy of return requires that the second party have something of its own to offer, something that did not already belong to the first. David saw this before the Psalm restates it, in the prayer over the offerings for the Temple: Tua sunt omnia, et quae de manu tua accepimus, dedimus tibi -- all things are Yours, and what we have given You, we have only given from Your own hand (1 Chronicles 29:14). Nothing brought to the altar was ever not God's first.

The gift returns to its owner disguised as an offering.

This is not a difficulty peculiar to Temple economics. It is the general shape of the problem the tetralogy spent four essays working out with respect to knowledge: a relation in which one party grounds the other's very capacity to act cannot be evened out by any act of the grounded party, because the grounded party's every resource, including the resource it would use to act, is already an item on the other side of the ledger. Retribuo, taken strictly, asks the creature to find something outside the circle of what it has received, and the circle has no outside. The question, read as a request for an object, has no answer. It was never going to have one.

And yet the Psalm does not stop at the question. It answers it, in the very next breath, with four verbs -- accipiam, invocabo, reddam, ambulabo -- I will take, I will call upon, I will render, I will walk. Every one is first person and future tense; not one denotes a thing changing hands. And even before the individual verbs are weighed, the Psalm quietly introduces the feature that will prove decisive: every act of rendering is qualified by presence -- coram, before the face of. This is the hinge the rest of this essay turns on. Whatever the Psalmist finds to render, he does not find it among the objects that were disqualified in verse one of his own question. He finds it in a different grammatical category altogether: not a noun handed across, but a mode of remaining in front of the giver once the giving is acknowledged to be one-sided and permanent. The tetralogy called this mode a Stand. The Psalm, three thousand years earlier, called it reddam coram -- I will render, in front of.

## II. Coram: What Standing Before Adds to Rendering

Coram appears twice in three verses, and its repetition is not decoration. Vota mea Domino reddam coram omni populo ejus -- I will pay my vows to the Lord before all His people. Ambulabo coram Domino in regione vivorum -- I will walk before the Lord in the land of the living. In both cases the verb of payment or motion is qualified not by an amount or a manner but by a location relative to a witness: coram, before the face of. Even where the Psalm is at its most transactional in vocabulary -- reddam, I will render, the verb closest to retribuo itself -- it refuses to let the rendering stand alone. The vow is not paid; it is paid in front of someone. Presence is not the leftover once the payment is made. It is written into the grammar of the payment as its condition.

This is worth pausing on, because it inverts an intuition that comes naturally to anyone raised on the language of debt. We tend to think of witness as incidental to a transaction -- the payment would still be a payment if no one watched it happen. The Psalm's grammar says otherwise: the vow that is reddam coram is a different act than a vow merely discharged in private, not because the amount changes but because a vow paid in the absence of any witness has not yet become the kind of act that can answer quid retribuam, since it has not yet cost the one paying it the one thing a private discharge costs nothing: the risk of being seen while it happens. This is precisely the location the tetralogy's final essay called the Crossing Point, and precisely the act it called the Stand -- the will's own occupation of that location at the moment something is asked of it, sustained long enough to be seen occupying it. Coram is the interior grammar of stabant. The women at the cross were, without using the word, keeping the Psalmist's vow: not paying a debt, but standing where the debt could be seen not to be paid, and staying anyway. Their presence altered nothing in the visible outcome of the crucifixion, yet Scripture preserves it because fidelity itself had become the act.

## III. The Cup That Is Received and Still Called an Answer

The Psalm's next move is stranger than it first appears. Asked what he will render, the Psalmist answers: Calicem salutaris accipiam -- I will take up the cup of salvation. The verb is accipiam, I will receive; grammatically it is the same posture as the seed on the path, the same posture the tetralogy's fourth essay treated as the site where presence can fail before it starts. And yet here reception is offered as the very content of the answer to a question about giving. This reversal is deliberate. It is the Psalm noticing something the tetralogy's own argument requires but did not, in its earlier essays, have occasion to name: that reception is not uniformly passive. A cup taken up by someone standing at the Crossing Point, occupying it, willing to be seen taking it, is a different act from a cup that merely arrives at an unoccupied hearing and is carried off before anyone was there to receive it. The difference is not in the cup. It is in whether anyone was rendering their presence at the moment of taking it.

The Latin Church did not let this stay a literary observation; it built it into the Mass. In the older Roman Rite, the priest prays this exact verse -- Quid retribuam Domino pro omnibus quae retribuit mihi? Calicem salutaris accipiam, et nomen Domini invocabo -- immediately before drinking from the chalice at Communion, at the one moment in the liturgy when what is received is understood to be, in the fullest sense available to Catholic theology, God Himself. The prayer is not spoken over an ordinary cup, and it is not asking what object might match a debt. It is asking the question at the exact instant when the only conceivable answer is: to receive, and to be present while receiving, since nothing else on offer could possibly be adequate to what is being given. The chalice does not let the communicant off the hook of quid retribuam. It relocates the render into the act of drinking itself, done in front of the altar, in front of the assembly, coram omni populo restated in bread and wine. Receiving becomes an answer the instant someone stays to be seen receiving it.

## IV. Ambulabo: The Vow Extended Into Duration

Stabant juxta crucem -- they stood beside the cross -- is a perfect tense fixed to a single, unrepeatable hour. Ambulabo coram Domino -- I will walk before the Lord -- is future, iterative, and has no hour built into it at all. This difference is not an accident of two different texts drawing on two different moments in salvation history. It is the same discovery in two grammatical tenses, and together they say something neither says alone: that the Stand is not confined to crises. Calvary supplied the tetralogy with its proof that presence can be held at maximum cost, against everything the parable of the sower named as fatal. But a life is not one afternoon at the foot of a cross. It is, overwhelmingly, ordinary hours in which nothing is being asked of anyone at the pitch the Gospel records -- and the Psalm's answer to quid retribuam is pitched exactly there, in the register of a life rather than an hour. Ambulabo is a verb of walking, not standing: motion sustained across days that do not individually announce themselves as tribulation, in a land -- regio vivorum, the land of the living -- that is simply the ordinary world, not Golgotha.

The Fathers often described the Christian life not as possession but as perpetual approach. The Eastern tradition has a name for the shape this takes when it is examined closely: Gregory of Nyssa's epektasis, the soul's perpetual reaching forward into a God whose infinity means there is no point of arrival at which the reaching could stop and rest. Gregory reads this not as frustration but as the very structure of blessedness for a finite creature before an infinite God -- presence, for such a creature, cannot be a state achieved and then held; it can only be a direction continuously taken. Ambulabo names exactly this: not I will arrive, not I will have stood, but I will keep walking, before Him, in the land where walking is still possible. The tetralogy's Stand was defined against a moment -- the Now, in which reception either condenses or is lost. Ambulabo takes that same structure and multiplies it across a life, so that what the fourth essay called a Stand becomes, extended through time, something closer to a habit of the will: not one occupied Crossing Point but the willingness to occupy the next one, and the next, for as long as there is a next one to occupy.

<br>

## V. The Land of the Living

Regio vivorum is doing more work in this Psalm than a scenic backdrop. The land of the living is named because its opposite is available and was, for the Psalmist praying this line, recently near -- the Psalm that contains it, taken as a whole, is a testimony of having been drawn back from death, its later verses naming the near escape outright: pretiosa in conspectu Domini mors sanctorum ejus, precious in the sight of the Lord is the death of His saints, said by someone who has just finished describing his own death as a thing barely avoided. Death is not merely an ending in this vocabulary; it is the one condition under which coram becomes structurally impossible, because presence requires a someone continuous enough across time to be before another, and death is exactly the dissolution of that continuity. This is why presence is, among the things a creature might render, the one gift whose grammar requires a body and a duration rather than merely a will. An angel is present to God constitutively, in a single unbroken act; only a creature bound to time can render a presence achieved across days instead of simply being true.

What looks like the deficiency of finitude -- that a human being cannot simply be present the way an angel or God is, but must instead keep becoming present, hour by hour, choice by choice -- turns out, on this reading, to be the very shape of the render. God does not lack duration and therefore cannot offer a gift shaped like duration. Only the creature, walking in the land of the living, can hand back to God the one thing built from the very limitation that seemed, in the tetralogy's earlier essays, to separate it from Him.

## VI. A Caveat About the Word "Render"

It would misstate everything argued so far to let reddam and ambulabo sound like a debt finally paid off, as though the creature had, after all, located a currency equal to the Giver's and closed the account the first line of the Psalm opened. That reading would undo the very point the opening verse was making. Quid retribuam remains, after this whole essay, a question without a proportionate answer, because presence does not settle a debt in the way an object would; it does not reduce what is owed by any calculable amount. What it does is refuse the transactional frame altogether while still standing inside the relationship the frame was trying, badly, to describe. This is not a technicality. The tradition has always insisted that even the will's own perseverance in presence is itself sustained by the grace of the one before whom it stands -- that the Stand at Calvary and the walking of ambulabo are not self-funded achievements set over against God's unearned grounding, but the creature's cooperation with a gift that includes, among everything else it gives, the capacity to keep giving presence back -- the same caveat the tetralogy entered about the Stand at Calvary, entered here again about the walking that extends it. Quid retribuam is answered, but not repaid. The Psalmist is not discharging a debt with a currency he finally found. He is doing the only thing left available to someone who has understood that no currency exists: staying, in front of the One from whom even the staying is, in the last analysis, received.

<br>

## VII. What Is Rendered

The tetralogy closed by naming a presence that could have left, and did not, as the one thing a creature has that God does not need. This essay set that closing sentence next to a much older one and found them answering the same question from opposite ends of the corpus: Quid retribuam Domino pro omnibus quae retribuit mihi? has no object for its answer, and was never going to. What it has instead is a cup taken up in front of a people, a vow paid where it can be seen not to close any account, and a walking that does not end at the border of any single Now but is renewed across an entire land of the living, for as long as there is ground left to walk on. None of this balances the ledger the question opened. All of it answers the question anyway, because the Psalmist, like the women at the cross, discovered that the render God cannot need and will not refuse is not a payment at all. It is a presence that could have left, and instead keeps walking, before Him, today, and tomorrow, and however many days remain in the land of the living.

*Vota mea Domino reddam coram omni populo ejus.*

*I will pay my vows to the Lord in the presence of all His people -- not because the debt is closed by the paying, but because presence, offered where it could have been withheld, was never asking to close it. God never asked for repayment because repayment was never possible. What He received instead was what only a creature could give: a presence that remained when it was free to depart.*
