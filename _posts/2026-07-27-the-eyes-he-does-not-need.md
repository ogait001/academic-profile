## I. The Question behind the Question

Ask how God sees, and the question already contains its own confusion, because see smuggles an organ into the sentence before any argument has earned it. Eyes are not a metaphor for knowledge in general; they are a particular solution to a particular problem, the problem of a finite subject who has no access to what lies outside itself except through a receiving surface tuned to some register of the world. Light strikes a retina. A signal travels. A brain constructs an image that stands in for a reality it did not itself contain. At every step of that chain, the knower and the known remain two things, and the whole apparatus exists to bridge a gap that would otherwise be absolute.

This is what I have elsewhere called externalization: the process by which an interior -- a self, a fact, an intention -- throws off traces that can cross to another interior only by leaving the first. A word spoken is an externalization. A gesture, a face, a sentence written on a page -- all externalizations. None of them is the interior itself. All of them are what the interior can afford to release without ceasing to have a remainder that was never released. This is the structure that makes the Infinite Interior infinite: no externalization, and no sum of externalizations, exhausts it. That is also what makes finite knowing partial by construction. I know another person only through what crosses; the Wall stands exactly where the crossing stops.

So when the question is put plainly -- if God has no physical eyes, how can He see, know, perceive our interior? -- it is really two questions wearing one grammar. The first is whether God needs an organ. The second, buried underneath, is whether God is subject to the Wall at all. The two questions have the same answer, and the answer is no, but it is worth separating them, because the second is the one that matters and the first is only its symptom.

## II. Two Modes of Knowing

Eyes belong to externalization because the knower who needs eyes is a knower on the far side of a gap. I know you because I receive what you release. That sentence is true of every finite subject toward every other finite subject, without exception, and no amount of attentiveness closes the gap it describes -- it only narrows what crosses. Friendship, marriage, decades of shared life: all of it increases the bandwidth of externalization. None of it removes the Wall. This is not a deficiency to be regretted. It is what it is to be one interior standing outside another, and the essay on the Turing Test argued at length that a transcript, however long, inherits this limit rather than escaping it: a machine that produces every externalization a person could produce would still stand on the far side of the same Wall, because the Wall is not a shortage of evidence. It is the structure of finite knowing itself.

God's knowing is not this, and the reason is not that His instruments are better. It is that He does not know through instruments at all. *God knows you because your very being is present to Him.* That is not a claim that He watches more closely, or that His attention misses nothing, or that His bandwidth is infinite where ours is narrow -- all of those pictures still place Him on the far side of a gap, only a smaller one. The claim is stronger and stranger: there is no gap to close. Your being does not have to reach God, the way it has to reach me, because it was never outside Him in the way it is outside me. He does not receive your externalizations. He is not withheld from your interior the way I am withheld from it. The Wall, I have argued, is the epistemic limit between finite subjects. For God, there is no wall -- not because He broke through it from His side, with a knowledge powerful enough to breach any barrier, but because He was never on the outside of it to begin with. A wall separates two things that stand apart. Nothing stands apart from the God in whom, as another tradition puts it without needing this vocabulary, we live and move and have our being.

One clarification must be entered here, because the sentence just written invites a misreading that would undo it. To say that nothing stands apart from God, that your being is present to Him without a gap, is not to say that your being is a part of God, or a region within Him, or the same being as His. Presence here is not identity. The creature is not God and is not contained in God as a piece is contained in a whole. What is denied is only the epistemic separation -- the Wall -- that stands between any two finite interiors; what is not denied, and indeed is required, is the far deeper distinction between the being that is given and the being that gives it. The creature depends on God for its very actuality, and dependence of that kind is the most asymmetric relation there is, nothing like the mutual exteriority of two created things and nothing like the identity of one thing with itself. That God is not outside your interior is a statement about the absence of a wall, not about the presence of a shared substance. The rest of this essay assumes that distinction and never spends it.

This is why eyes are the wrong image and not merely an imprecise one. An eye is built for a subject who is elsewhere relative to what it sees. God is not elsewhere relative to any interior. The absence of eyes is not a limitation on divine knowledge; it is a grammatical clue that divine knowledge belongs to a different family of acts than seeing does.

## III. Condensation without a Future Tense

The framework already has a name for how a state gives rise to its successor: condensation, the gathering of inheritance -- what the previous moment contributes -- together with reception -- what is newly given at the Now -- into a single new actuality. Every condensation is a settled fact about a creature; every condensation belongs, once it occurs, to the past. This raises an obvious temptation, one I gave in to when I first put the question this way: if condensations are how a creature's history accumulates, and God knows the whole history, does He not also know the condensations that have not yet happened -- the future condensations?

The phrase should be retracted, and not merely softened. A condensation, on the terms this topology itself sets, is the trace left by a reception that has already crossed into actuality. To speak of a condensation that has not yet occurred for the creature is to use the word outside the only conditions under which it has a referent. It borrows the settledness of the past and attaches it to something not yet settled, which is exactly the confusion this framework exists to dissolve rather than reproduce.

What can be said instead is this: God eternally knows every reception, and therefore, as each reception enters time and becomes a condensation, He knows every condensation as it is. Nothing is added to His knowledge when Tuesday arrives that was withheld from Him on Monday -- but not because Tuesday's condensation was already sitting somewhere ahead of Monday, waiting its turn, already formed as a "future condensation" for Him to inspect in advance. It was not yet formed at all. What was already His, on Monday, was Tuesday's reception, eternally present to Him, without that reception yet being, for the creature, the settled trace that only condensation produces.

The temporal distinction is not erased; it is relocated. The creature's condensations remain strictly ordered, past before future, because condensation is the mechanism of the creature's becoming and becoming is sequential by its very nature. God's knowledge of every reception is not ordered that way, because His knowing is not a becoming at all. Every reception is eternally present to God; every condensation is eternally present to the creature's own identity, at the moment, and only at the moment, it occurs. Divine eternity is not a longer timeline that contains the creature's shorter one, with future condensations already sitting further down the track. It is not a timeline.

This is also why the classical formula -- God knows the future because He sees all of time laid out before Him -- undersells what this topology is claiming. That formula still pictures a completed timeline, a filmstrip fully shot, with God positioned to view every frame at once. It rescues divine knowledge from ignorance of the future but leaves time itself as the kind of thing that could, in principle, be looked at from outside like a landscape. The claim on offer here is different in kind. There is no future timeline for God to inspect, because a timeline is what condensation produces from inside sequence, and God does not stand outside sequence looking in. The future is not a completed corridor lying in front of the creature that God has already walked to the end of. It is the horizon of receptions not yet actualized for the creature -- and eternally actual, without a not yet, for God.

## IV. What Is, Has Already Been

Read this way, a line I wrote earlier -- that as soon as receptions come to the Now, they are already condensations -- stops sounding paradoxical and starts sounding almost tautological. Reception and condensation are not separated by a delay that God's superior perception manages to close. They are separated by the Crossing Point itself, which is not a duration. The instant a reception occurs for a creature, it is simultaneously a condensation of that creature; there is no interval in which it is one and not yet the other. Nothing needs to travel from reception to condensation, because they are not two events joined by a gap. They are two names for the same occurrence, read from two angles: reception, insofar as something new is given; condensation, insofar as that given thing has now settled into what the creature is.

This is the structure Ecclesiastes names without any of this apparatus: that which hath been is now, and that which is to be hath already been. Read as a claim about history repeating, the verse is a commonplace, and not a very interesting one -- of course patterns recur. Read through condensation, it says something more exact. It does not mean events repeat. It means every reception, the instant it enters time, belongs already to eternity -- so that what is now, considered from the standpoint of the God for whom it is eternally received, has in a sense always been His; and what will be, considered from that same standpoint, already is His, before it is the creature's. The creature experiences the gap between is and will be as the substance of time itself, the felt difference between what has happened and what is still open. God does not experience that gap, not because He has already looked ahead and filed the answer away, but because for Him there is no ahead to look toward. What the creature calls waiting, God's mode of being does not contain at all.

## V. The Wrong Word is "At Once"

Everything so far has been aimed at dislodging one picture: that God knows by receiving what crosses to Him from outside. Once that picture goes, the natural next thought is that divine knowledge is finite knowledge with its limits removed -- the same act we perform, scaled up without ceiling. And there is one hidden assumption in that thought that has not yet been named, because it does not announce itself as an error. It announces itself as praise.

We instinctively imagine God's knowledge as a single attention expanded without limit, a gaze wide enough to hold everything at once. But "at once" does not abandon the model of finite cognition; it magnifies it. It keeps the very structure the previous sections dismantled -- a knower set over against a field of objects -- and merely makes the field infinite and the knower tireless. The correction that follows is therefore of a piece with the ones before it, not a new topic: it is the last place the finite model hides after externalization, the Wall, and the future tense have all been taken from it.

Suppose God knows billions of interiors -- perhaps the whole of every interior that has ever crossed into actuality. The natural way to express the scale of that is to say He knows them at once, meaning simultaneously, meaning in a single comprehensive act spread across an enormous field of objects.

But "at once" still describes a quantity of attention, however vast, distributed across a quantity of objects, however many. It pictures God as processing billions of streams in parallel -- an infinite bandwidth rather than a narrow one, but still a bandwidth, still an act of attention that must be sufficient to its objects, still, in the end, a very much larger version of the same kind of knowing an eye performs. That picture keeps God inside the family of subjects who know by reception and stretches the family to its limit. It does not step outside the family.

The alternative is not that God attends to billions of interiors simultaneously, but that He is not managing streams at all. Every person's Crossing Point is immediately present to Him, not because His attention has been divided finely enough to reach that far, but because eternity is not divided into finite acts of attention in the first place. He does not distribute Himself among persons the way a finite knower must divide focus among objects; He is wholly present to each without any diminishment of His presence to any other, which is not a description of extraordinary multitasking but a claim that multitasking -- attention parcelled out across competing claims -- is not the kind of act divine knowledge is. A being who knows by reception, however capacious, will always need a word like "at once" to explain how the reception keeps up with its objects. A being for whom eternity simply is the dimension in which every Crossing Point is present does not need that word, because there was no keeping up to be done.

## VI. A Different Question

The usual question about omniscience asks how God can know a future that has not happened yet, and most answers to it -- foreknowledge as extraordinarily good prediction, foreknowledge as viewing from outside time, foreknowledge as somehow compatible with a still-open will -- spend their energy trying to fit an act of seeing into a case where the object of seeing does not yet, by the terms of the question, exist to be seen. This essay has tried to show that the question is asking for the wrong thing before any answer is given, because it assumes God knows the way we do and only wonders how He manages the harder case.

The better question is not how God knows the future. It is what divine knowledge is, once eternity is understood as something other than an especially long or especially early vantage point within succession. On these terms, God does not have eyes because He does not need externalization; He does not know future condensations because condensation is a creature's word for its own becoming, and God does not become; He does not know billions of interiors at once because "at once" still measures an act against a field of objects, and His presence to each interior is not measured against anything. What remains is not a smaller claim than the traditional one, that God foreknows all things. It is a different claim, resting on different ground: that God's knowledge was never a case of seeing the future from very far away, because He was never far from any Now to begin with.

If divine knowledge is neither observation, nor reception, nor attention distributed across a field of objects, then every finite model of knowing has failed, and failed not at its edges but at its center. What has been removed is not a defect in the models but the models themselves: the eye, the channel, the bandwidth, the timeline, the vast parallel attention. Each was a way of picturing a knower set over against what he knows, and each has turned out to smuggle back the very separation the divine case does not contain.

The finite grammar is now exhausted. Whatever divine knowledge is, it does not belong to the family of reception, and no enlargement of that family will ever reach it. The question that remains is not how God sees, or how He keeps up, or how He reaches across. It is what divine knowledge is, when nothing finite is left to say it with. That question this essay does not answer.
