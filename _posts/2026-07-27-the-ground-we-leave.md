## I. The Relation Had Two Sides

Every argument so far in this corpus has run in one direction. Under the Fig Tree showed that a man's most private Now was never hidden from the one who holds it open. The Eyes He Does Not Need dismantled every model that would make that holding a kind of watching. The Ground That Knows gave the positive identity underneath both: to ground the Now is to know it, one act named twice, no second act of attention required. Each essay, in its own way, was an argument about God -- about the unconditioned side of a relation between an infinite Ground and a finite creature standing, or so it was quietly assumed, upon it.

That assumption is worth stating plainly, because the whole of this essay is what happens when it is dropped. The first three essays needed the creature only as the site where God's presence lands; they did not ask whether the creature is present to receive what lands there. Grounding, on their account, is unconditional -- God does not wait to see whether a person will cooperate before He holds that person's Now in being. But knowing that the Ground never withdraws tells you nothing about whether the ground is bearing anything. A field can be perfectly watered and still be barren, if nothing in the field is doing what a field must do to turn water into grain. The three essays gave the water. They did not ask about the field.

Christ's own teaching supplies the question this corpus has been circling without naming. Seeing they see not; and hearing they hear not, neither do they understand. If divine presence to the creature is as total as the previous essays argued, this verse should be impossible. Nothing is withheld from anyone; the light in which all things are seen, per the Psalm this series has already leaned on, is not rationed. And yet people hear and do not understand -- not because the word withheld itself, but for a reason that has to be located somewhere other than in God's giving, since God's giving, on this topology's own terms, does not have degrees. The reason has to be found on the creature's side of the relation. This essay is the search for it.

## II. What the Path Cannot Hold

The parable that supplies the search is the sower's, and its first failure is the sharpest, because it is the fastest. When any one heareth the word of the kingdom, and understandeth it not, then cometh the wicked one, and catcheth away that which was sown in his heart. This is he which received seed by the way side. Mark's version is, if anything, more severe: the seed is sown, and immediately Satan comes and takes it away.

That word immediately is doing more work than a first reading gives it credit for. It removes an interval this framework has elsewhere insisted is decisive. Under the Fig Tree located the whole of moral life in the space between a thought's arrival and the will's answer to it -- narrow, but never absent, because it is exactly wide enough for consent or refusal to occur. On the path, that space does not open. There is no moment in which the hearer weighs the word and turns it down; the taking happens too fast for refusal, which means it is not describing a decision at all. Refusal would at least require someone standing at the Crossing Point to do the refusing. What the path describes is stranger: a hearing with no one home to receive it. The word is delivered -- the ear does its work, exactly as the eye does its work when someone looks straight at a miracle and sees nothing worth remembering. What fails is not the delivery and not, strictly, the will's judgment. What fails is that the will was never there to judge.

This is why seeing they see not is not a paradox to be softened into ordinary human limitation, some vague claim that people miss things. It is a precise description of a structural possibility this corpus has not yet had reason to name: that the senses can do their entire job, delivering the word exactly as the chain in Under the Fig Tree said they would, and the delivery can still arrive at a Crossing Point no one is occupying. The Wall, in the first three essays, was the limit between two finite interiors -- the reason I cannot know you the way God knows you. What the path exposes is something else, smaller and stranger: not a limit between one interior and another, but an interior vacancy -- the Crossing Point left unoccupied, so that the word crosses the threshold of hearing and is taken before any will is there to meet it.

## III. The Stand

A name is needed for what was missing on the path, and the vocabulary already assembled in this corpus supplies almost all of it. The Crossing Point is a location -- the place in a person where the loop of memory and the loop of anticipation meet, where reception either becomes condensation or does not. That location does not move and is never absent; it is simply where the Now of a temporal being is. But a location being there is not the same as anyone being at it. What the sower's first failure exposes is a further thing, distinct from the Crossing Point itself, which this essay will call the Stand: the will's own occupation of the Crossing Point at the moment reception arrives, sustained long enough for the arriving thing to settle into what the creature is.

The distinction matters because it locates a difference the three prior essays did not need and this one cannot do without. God's presence to the creature's Now is, on this topology's own settled account, constitutive -- it is not a further act added to grounding, and it cannot be withdrawn without the Now itself ceasing to be held in being at all. The creature's presence to that same Now is not like this. It is not constitutive of anything; it is closer to a stance, taken or not taken, sustained or abandoned, and its absence does not annihilate the Now -- the Now goes on being held by God whether or not the creature is standing in it. This is the asymmetry the whole essay turns on. On God's side, presence to the creature simply is what grounding is; there is no question of God failing to take His Stand, because His relation to the Now was never a stand to begin with, only a giving. On the creature's side, presence to its own Now is exactly the sort of thing that can be taken or missed, because the creature is not the Now's ground. It is only ever standing, or failing to stand, in a place someone else is holding open for it.

So the path's failure can now be stated without metaphor. God grounds the Now in which the word is sown; that much is never in question, and nothing about the sower's parable suggests otherwise. What the path lacks is the Stand -- the hearer's own will, present and occupying the Crossing Point at the instant the word arrives, so that arrival can become something kept. Without it, the word passes through a Now that is real, and grounded, and known by God down to its least particular, and still comes to nothing for the creature, because the one place a reception must be met to become a condensation was left standing empty.

<br>

## IV. Two Further Failures

The parable does not stop at the path, and the two failures that follow refine the Stand rather than repeat it. He that received the seed into stony places, the same is he that heareth the word, and anon with joy receiveth it; yet hath he not root in himself, but dureth for a while: for when tribulation or persecution ariseth because of the word, by and by he is offended. Here the Stand is not simply missing, as it was on the path. It is taken -- the joy is real, the reception genuine -- and then abandoned. What the rocky ground exposes is that the Stand is not a single instant but a duration, and a duration is precisely the kind of thing that can be started and not finished. The seed springs up quickly for the same reason it withers quickly: nothing beneath the joy was holding the ground, so nothing beneath the joy was equipped to hold the will there once holding cost something.

The thorns name a third failure, closer to the ordinary case than either of the first two: he that received seed among the thorns is he that heareth the word; and the care of this world, and the deceitfulness of riches, choke the word. Nothing snatches this seed away, and nothing kills it outright. It is simply crowded -- and crowding is the one word this corpus already has for what the loops of memory and anticipation do when they are not held in check by a will occupying the Now between them. The thorns are what Under the Fig Tree called the chain: care, the passions' response to a threatened future; riches' deceitfulness, the anticipation-loop mistaking a good for more than it is. Neither is an intruder from outside the mind. Both are the ordinary furniture of a temporal creature's interior life, and the failure they cause is not dramatic. It is a Stand divided rather than a Stand abandoned -- presence given partially, to several things at once, until none of them receives enough of it to condense into fruit.

Only the last soil receives what the others could not, and Matthew is careful about the verb he adds to hearing: he that heareth the word, and understandeth it; which also beareth fruit. Understanding, on this account, is not a sharper act of intellect than hearing is. It is hearing plus a Stand sustained across the whole distance between reception and fruit -- the same distance this corpus has elsewhere measured in the parable of the virgins, where oil was named as the accumulated orientation of a will that kept turning the right way across a lifetime of unwitnessed crossings. Fruit is what condensation looks like when the Stand that let it happen was not a single instant but a life's worth of instants, held.

<br>

## V. Stabat

Everything so far has described failure, because failure is what the parable supplies and this essay needed the parable to locate the missing term. But the corpus has already learned, in its earlier movements, that a purely negative account is a debt, and the debt is paid by finding the place where the missing thing is not absent but achieved. For the Stand, that place is Calvary.

By the moment John records, presence has already failed at every register the parable named. Peter, offered no persecution graver than a servant girl's question, takes the offense the rocky-ground hearer takes at real tribulation, and three times. The eleven scatter into exactly the crowdedness the thorns describe, care for their own safety choking out, for a night, everything they had been taught. The Gospel does not soften this; it simply narrates it and moves on, the way a field is described as it is rather than as it should have been. Against that scattering, one sentence is set with a verb that will not be read past: Now there stood by the cross of Jesus his mother, and his mother's sister, Mary the wife of Cleophas, and Mary Magdalene.

Stood. Not was present, not happened to be near, not arrived once grief made action impossible -- the Gospel had every plainer verb available and did not choose one. What is being reported is not a location but a Stand in the exact sense this essay has been building toward: a will occupying an unbearable Now and not leaving it, at the one moment in the whole narrative when tribulation is not a future possibility, as it is in the parable's warning, but the entire content of the present. Mark's rocky-ground hearer is offended when tribulation arises because of the word. At the foot of the cross, the word himself is the tribulation, in the plainest sense the mother of that word could suffer, and the Stand holds anyway. This is not the absence of what felled the other hearers. It is their exact conditions, met by the one response the parable never gets to show, because the parable is a warning and Calvary is an answer to it.

Nothing about this Stand is comfortable, and nothing about it is easy, which is the point. The parable's failures are all, in their different ways, a Stand that cost too little to be worth keeping -- snatched because nothing was risked in defending it, withered because nothing was rooted beneath the joy, choked because it was never the only thing being attended to. The Stand at the cross is the inversion of all three at once: nothing is snatched, because someone is standing there to be robbed of nothing; nothing withers, because there was no quick joy to begin with, only grief absorbed and not fled; nothing is choked, because for the length of that Now, nothing else was permitted to compete for the ground.

## VI. A Caveat About Grace

It would misstate what has been argued to let the Stand sound like an achievement the creature produces out of its own unaided strength, a kind of moral athletics set over against God's effortless grounding. That reading would also undo the very asymmetry this essay depends on. If the Stand were purely the creature's own contribution, generated from resources entirely its own, then presence would after all be earned on both sides of the relation, only unevenly -- which is precisely the picture the first three essays spent their length dismantling with respect to knowledge, and there is no reason to let it back in with respect to will.

The tradition this corpus keeps returning to has never described perseverance as self-sufficient. What sustains a Stand across tribulation, on the classical account, is not a store of native willpower larger than Peter's happened to be, but grace meeting a will that does not withdraw its own cooperation -- two causes, unequal in kind, acting in the one event. The Stand is therefore neither autonomous willing nor passive reception, but the creature's persevering participation in what grace makes possible. This essay does not have the resources to adjudicate how grace and freedom cohere in that single act, and does not attempt to; that question belongs to a different essay than this one, if it belongs to this corpus at all. What can be said without overreaching is narrower and still enough to carry the argument: whatever combination of gift and cooperation makes a Stand possible, the Stand itself is where the creature's part of the relation is decided, in a way that grounding, on God's side, is not decided at all. God does not require help to hold a Now open. Whatever kind of help the creature requires to remain standing in it, it requires some, and Peter's night is the record of what happens when, for a while, that help was not met with a will still willing to stay.

<br>

## VII. What This Completes

The tetralogy now has both of its halves. God's presence to the creature is being -- a grounding that constitutes the Now it holds, needs no organ, no interval, no second act, and cannot, on its own terms, fail to reach anywhere it is directed, because it was never a reaching at all. The creature's presence to that same Now is standing -- a Stand that can be missing before it starts, taken quickly and abandoned, divided among competing cares, or held, at whatever cost, across the whole length of a tribulation the parable only warns about and Calvary suffers. The first is a gift with no earlier moment in which it was not yet given. The second is a work, however much that work depends on a grace not its own, and a life is very largely the record of how many times, in rooms no one else ever entered, it was taken up or set down.

This is why the seed on the path and the women at the cross belong in the same essay and could not have appeared in any of the three before it. Those essays had no need of a creature who could fail to receive what was perfectly given, because their whole argument was that the giving does not depend on the creature at all. But a Ground that never withdraws is not the same thing as a field that always bears, and the difference between them is exactly what this corpus lacked a name for until the sower supplied one. God was never at a distance from either the path or the cross. He held both Nows open with the same undivided presence the first three essays already established has no degrees and admits no failure. What differed was not what He gave. It was who was standing there to receive it -- nowhere, on the path; and at the foot of the cross, against everything the parable says should have driven them off, three women and one disciple who simply stood, and did not leave.

*Stabant juxta crucem.*

*They stood beside the cross. Not because the ground gave way beneath everyone else and held firm only for them -- the ground held for all alike, then as always. Because they, when it was theirs to give, gave it the one thing a creature has that God does not need: a presence that could have left, and did not.*
