## I. The Question That Follows From the Ground

The prior essay in this corpus established a single identity: for God, to sustain the creature's Now and to know what occurs within it are not two acts but one, named twice. That essay was careful to keep its claim narrow. It concerned one relation -- the dependence of a creature's present moment on its ground -- described from two angles. Nothing in it required subdividing the agent doing the grounding.[^1]

Applied to the doctrine of the Trinity, the identity immediately raises a question the earlier essay did not have to face. If Father, Son, and Spirit are really three, does "who sustains the Now" have three different answers, or one? And the question sharpens unbearably at a single point in history: the Word, without ceasing to be what He was, became what He was not -- a creature possessing a genuine human Now, subject to the very structure of dependence the corpus has been describing throughout. Does the Ground, at that point, come to need grounding? If the answer is simply yes, something has gone wrong with what it means for Him to be God. If the answer is simply no -- if His humanity floated free of the condition every other creature is under -- something has gone wrong with what it means for Him to be man. Catholic doctrine has spent sixteen centuries refusing both easy answers. This essay tries to state, as precisely as the tradition allows, why the harder answer is the true one.

[^1]: Throughout this corpus, "grounding" and its cognates designate ontological dependence -- the relation by which a contingent thing derives its being, at every instant, from that on which it does not merely temporally follow but metaphysically depends -- and not temporal succession or efficient causation of the ordinary created kind. To ground a creature's Now, in this usage, is to hold it in being, not to have earlier produced it and withdrawn.

## II. Not Three Names: Subsistent Relations

It would be a mistake to extend the earlier identity -- "one act, named twice, distinguished by us and not in Him" -- directly onto the Trinity, as though Father, Son, and Spirit were three labels our language applies to a single undifferentiated divine act. That move has a name and a condemnation: modalism, the ancient error, associated with Sabellius, that treats the Persons as successive or simultaneous masks worn by one solitary God. The Church rejected it precisely because Scripture and the Church's own experience of prayer will not permit it -- the Father sends the Son, the Son prays to the Father, the Spirit is given by both, and none of this is intelligible if there is, underneath the names, only one undifferentiated actor performing a costume change.

The distinction the doctrine actually requires is more exact than "names," and also stranger. Since Augustine and especially since Aquinas's treatment in the Summa Theologiae, the Persons have been understood as subsistent relations: not three parts of God, not three instances of a common nature, but three relations of origin -- paternity, filiation, and procession -- each of which simply is the one divine essence, considered as it relates. The Father is not God-plus-a-property-of-fatherhood; the Father is paternity itself, subsisting. The relation does not divide the essence, because relation, unlike quantity or matter, does not compose what it relates -- it distinguishes without dividing, which is exactly the impossible-sounding thing simplicity requires and exactly what the doctrine claims to deliver. This is also the answer to the worry that composition would make God dependent, a worry the contingency argument of the corpus makes unavoidable: if the Three were parts assembled into a whole, that whole would depend on its parts, and the very argument from contingency this corpus opened with would apply to God Himself. Subsistent relation is the technical device by which the tradition avoids exactly that trap. The Persons are really distinct -- the Father is not the Son -- and yet nothing is added to the essence to make three of them. There is no fourth thing, the divine nature, that the three Persons then divide up or share; each Person simply is that nature, related.

## III. One Act, Three Voices: Appropriation

If the Persons are not interchangeable names, how can the corpus's earlier essays speak, without qualification, of the Father as the one who sustains, or of Alpha as the ground and Omega as the destination, without lapsing into tritheism -- three gods dividing the labor of creation among themselves?

The doctrine's answer is the principle that the works of the Trinity outside itself are undivided: opera Trinitatis ad extra sunt indivisa. Every act toward creation -- creating, sustaining, redeeming, sanctifying -- is one act of the one divine nature, and therefore an act of all Three Persons together, not an act any one Person performs alone while the other two look on. What Scripture and liturgy nonetheless do, and what the Catechism sanctions under the name appropriation, is attribute a common act to one Person by fittingness with what is proper to Him: origin and power to the Father, because He is the principle without principle; wisdom and the ordering of things to the Son, because He is the Father's Word; goodness, gift, and the bringing of things to their completion to the Spirit, because He proceeds as the mutual love of the first two breathed outward. Creation is fittingly said of the Father, redemption of the Son, sanctification of the Spirit -- and each of those statements is true, but true as appropriation, not as an exclusive assignment of duties. When Alpha and Omega speaks of the Father holding the Now open, that sentence is correct read as appropriation. Read as a claim that only the Father does the holding, it would need correcting: it is the Trinity, undivided, that sustains every Now -- the Son as much as the Father, the Spirit as much as either, each according to what is proper to Him within the single act.

## IV. The Hardest Case: Can the Ground Need Grounding?

This is the setting in which the Incarnation has to be placed, and it is where the earlier formulation -- that Christ, being God, did not need sustaining -- has to be corrected rather than simply repeated with more confidence.

The correction turns on a distinction the tradition insists on and ordinary speech constantly blurs: the difference between a nature and a person. A nature is not itself a subject of anything; it is the principle by which a subject is what it is and acts as it acts. Only a person -- a complete subject -- exists, acts, suffers, or is sustained. When the Word assumed a human nature, He did not assume a human person alongside His divine Personhood; He assumed a complete human nature that has no personhood of its own, so that the one subject possessing both natures, acting through both, sustained in His humanity and sustaining as God, is a single Person: the Word.

So the human nature genuinely requires sustaining, exactly as any created nature does -- to deny this is to deny that the humanity was real, which is the ancient error of Docetism and its later cousin Eutychianism, both condemned for the same reason: they save God's transcendence by quietly unmaking the manhood. But the Person whose nature it is, and the Trinity whose undivided act sustains every creaturely Now, are not two rival claimants on the same job. The Word, according to His divine nature, is Himself one of the Three sustaining every Now there is, including the Now of the flesh He assumed. He does not, in becoming man, step outside the Trinitarian act of sustaining creation and become merely its object. He remains, as God, a co-principle of that very sustaining -- while becoming, as man, the term of it. What changes is not that the Ground now needs a ground outside Himself. What changes is that, for the first time, the sustaining Trinitarian act and the sustained creaturely nature terminate in one and the same Person. He, as God, sustains the humanity He possesses as man -- not because the humanity is exempt from dependence, but because the one supplying the dependence and the one receiving it are, in this case alone, not two.

<br>

## V. Enhypostasis: The Person Who Grounds His Own Nature

The Council of Chalcedon in 451 gave the formula the whole tradition since has tried to remain faithful to: one and the same Christ, Son, Lord, only-begotten, acknowledged in two natures, "without confusion, without change, without division, without separation," the distinction of natures never abolished by the union, the property of each nature preserved, both concurring into one Person and one hypostasis. Against those who fused the natures into one (Eutyches) and those who split the one Person into two (Nestorius), Chalcedon drew a line exact enough to exclude both without pretending to explain the mystery it protects.

Later theology, especially through Leontius of Byzantium, supplied the technical term for the humanity's specific mode of existing: it is enhypostatic, not anhypostatic -- not personless in the sense of unreal, but personless in the sense that it possesses no hypostasis, no "who," of its own; it subsists in the hypostasis of the Word. Every other human nature that has ever existed terminates in its own created person, someone other than God, whom God sustains as other. Christ's human nature is the sole exception in the history of creation: a complete, unabbreviated humanity -- with its own human intellect, will, and, as later theology insisted against Monothelitism, its own human will genuinely operative and not absorbed into the divine -- that nonetheless has no personal center of its own to stand at. Its personal center is the Word.

This is the more exact way to state what the earlier, looser formulation was reaching for. The claim is not that Christ's humanity floated above the ordinary condition of creatures and needed no ground. The claim is that the ground it needed, and continuously received, was supplied by a Person who is also, and without division, the very one that ground was upholding. Every other creature's Now is held open by Someone Other. His human Now was held open by Himself -- not as a nature sustaining itself, which would be either incoherent or a return to the pantheism the whole framework was built to exclude, but as a Person, God the Son, sustaining, as God, the nature that He, as man, possessed.

<br>

## VI. One Esse or Two? A Question the Essay Leaves Open

One further question belongs here, and the honest course is to mark it as open rather than settle it by fiat. Aquinas held that Christ has a single act of existing -- one esse, the uncreated esse of the Word Himself -- terminating both the divine and the human nature, since esse belongs properly to the person and not to the nature as such. Other theologians within full orthodoxy, both medieval and modern, have judged that the integrity of the human nature requires positing a distinct, created human esse as well, united to but not simply identical with the Word's own act of existing, precisely so that the humanity is not merely instrumental to the Person but a complete principle of human operation in its own right. The dispute remains a live one in contemporary Thomistic Christology; Thomas Joseph White, among recent scholars, has defended the single-esse reading while taking the force of the objection seriously, which is roughly the posture this essay adopts.

This corpus's account of grounding leans toward finding Aquinas's position congenial -- if to sustain a Now and to be present to it are one act named twice, a single terminating esse is a natural place for that identity to land in the one case where sustainer and sustained coincide in Person. But the question is a live one within the bounds of orthodoxy, turns on technical points in the metaphysics of act and potency that this essay has not argued for, and is not something the present argument needs to resolve in order to stand. It is set down here as a debt still owed, in the manner the corpus has tried to be honest about elsewhere.

## VII. The Grammar of Exchange: Communicatio Idiomatum

If one Person truly possesses two natures without their being confused, a peculiar rule of speech follows, and the tradition has a name for it: the communicatio idiomatum, the exchange of properties. Because the subject of both natures is one and the same Person, whatever is true of either nature may rightly be predicated of that one Person, using a name proper to either nature, without thereby mixing the natures themselves. Scripture already speaks this way: Paul can write that the rulers of this age crucified "the Lord of glory," and Luke can have Paul tell the Ephesian elders that God "purchased the church with his own blood" -- glory and blood, divine title and human suffering, predicated of the same one. The Council of Ephesus in 431 made the principle a matter of dogma against Nestorius by insisting that Mary may rightly be called Theotokos, God-bearer -- not because she generated the divine nature, which is eternal, but because the one she bore in her womb, according to His human nature, just is the eternal Word, according to His Person.

The Second Council of Constantinople in 553 pushed the same grammar to its sharpest edge, affirming that "one of the Holy Trinity suffered in the flesh." This sentence has to be read exactly as written and no further. It is the Person, one of the Trinity, who suffered -- truly, not apparently. But He suffered in the flesh: according to the human nature, which alone is capable of suffering, not according to the divine nature, which remains, as it has always been held, impassible. To say the divine nature itself suffered is Patripassianism's error extended into Christology, and the councils close that door as firmly as they open the other one. The result is a single, exact statement: God the Son truly suffered and truly died, and God, in His divine nature, is incapable of suffering or dying -- both fully true, because the first predicates the event of the Person and the second predicates an incapacity of the nature, and a Person is not reducible to either nature He possesses.

<br>

## VIII. Distancia Mas Inmensa

Theology protects the mystery with conceptual precision; poetry sometimes preserves the same mystery through image and proportion. Gongora's line belongs to a sonnet contemplating the crucifixion, and its argument is a small masterpiece of exactly this logic. The poet does not marvel first at the distance between life and death, though that distance is the one every human being fears most directly. He points past it, to a distance he calls more immense still: the distance from God to man. The implicit reasoning is theological, not merely poetic -- if the Word has already crossed the greater gap, in becoming man at all, then His further crossing of the lesser gap, from life into death, should astonish no one who has understood the first crossing rightly. The distance that should stop us in our tracks was closed nine months before Calvary, in a virgin's obscurity, not on the cross.

Within the vocabulary this corpus has developed, that is a precise way of naming what makes the Incarnation the crossing behind every other crossing. Distance, on this framework, belongs to the loops; it is not a property of the Now itself, which underlies every point equally and is equally near to all of them. But there is one distance that does not reduce to the geometry internal to the loops at all: the distance between necessary being and contingent being, between the One who cannot not exist and everything that might not have. This is not a large distance of the same kind as the distances within creation, as though God stood at the far end of a very long road; it is a distance of category, not of degree. No traversal, however vast, converts a contingent thing into a necessary one, because the difference is not how far apart two beings lie but what it is to be each -- and that is why no amount of creaturely motion could ever close it, the way a traveler closes the distance between two cities. It is the distance the first essay of this corpus was built to establish as absolute -- the difference between a brute contingent universe and a necessary ground of being, the gap no amount of regress, however long, was ever going to discharge on its own. Death, however terrible, is a transition internal to the loops: one state of a creature's Now succeeded by another. The Incarnation is not internal to the loops at all. It is the Ground entering the very structure whose entire mode of being is reception, and doing so not by analogy or by a specially close kind of causal influence, but in Person -- so that the crossing point the rest of creation only ever passes through from within is, in this one case, entered from without, by the One who was never inside it, and who from that moment forward sustains His own creaturely Now from a position no other creature's ground has ever occupied: not beside it, but as the very Person living it.

Read this way, Gongora is not offering a devotional exaggeration. He is stating, in fourteen lines, the same asymmetry the doctrine of subsistent relations, appropriation, and enhypostasis have taken this whole essay to state in prose: that the distance closed in the manger is greater than the distance closed in the tomb, because the second is a crossing within the world the first already had to enter from entirely outside it. Death is a doorway every loop eventually reaches. The Incarnation is the doorway being built, from the far side, by the one who was never going to need one. He built it, and then walked through it, so that the door would exist at all.

<br>

## IX. What This Essay Does Not Claim

Two boundaries are worth marking before closing, in keeping with this corpus's practice of not claiming more than the argument has earned. First, the question of one esse or two in Christ has been left open rather than adjudicated; the essay's sympathies were stated, but nothing above depends on that question being resolved one way rather than the other. Second, this essay has not attempted to show how the Trinitarian processions themselves -- generation and spiration, considered in God's own inner life apart from any relation to creatures -- relate to the corpus's language of the Now. That question, whether the eternal generation of the Son bears any structural likeness to the crossing-and-return this series has traced through cosmology, number, and the soul, is a natural next debt, and it is not paid here. What has been argued is narrower and, the author hopes, more securely grounded: that the doctrine of the Trinity as subsistent relations, the principle of appropriation, and the doctrine of the hypostatic union as enhypostatic union are not obstacles the framework of grounding has to work around, but the precise tools by which that framework can be extended to the Incarnation without collapsing into modalism on one side or Docetism on the other. Distancia mas inmensa: the greater distance is already closed. What remains is to keep stating, as exactly as the Church has always insisted on, what closing it did and did not mean.
