## I. The Question Neither Essay Was Built to Answer

[*Distancia Más Inmensa: On the Trinity, the Incarnation, and the Ground Who Became the Grounded*]
(https://oscargaitan.org/philosophy/theology/trinity/christology/2026/08/01/distancia-mas-inmensa-en.html) solved a problem of predication: how one Person may truly be said to suffer, to hunger, to die, while the nature incapable of any of these remains untouched by them. [*Eating Stones: The Temptation of Christ as Ontological Contradiction*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophy%20of%20religion/philosophical%20theology/metaphysics/christian%20philosophy/ontology/2026/06/29/eating-stones.html) solved a problem of identity: how to tell a genuine transformation, which carries a thing's interior across a change, from a substitution, which leaves the interior untouched beneath a falsified surface. Both essays did their work and stopped at their own edges. Neither had occasion to ask what happens when a single act — not a predicate applied after the fact, not a change undergone by matter, but a thing actually done, in real time, by a body in the world — must be assigned to a nature. The Passion supplies suffering; the wilderness supplies a refusal. The sea supplies neither. It supplies a deed: a man's feet resting on water that will not hold up any other man's feet. The question this essay takes up is what kind of act that is, and why the Gospels, read against their own scriptural background, insist on an answer neither essay had reason to give.

## II. Scripture's Own Argument: Dominion Over the Deep

The sea, in the poetic and prophetic books, is not a neutral body of water. It is the standing figure for what resists order — the chaos the Creator subdued at the beginning and continues to hold at bay by an act of will, not by the sea's own docility. *Who alone stretches out the heavens, and treads upon the waves of the sea,* Job says of God, and the verb matters: not calms, not parts, but treads — walks upon it, the way a man walks upon solid ground, and the poem's whole point is that only God can be the subject of that verb. Job returns to the same image later, this time in God's own voice out of the whirlwind: it is God who shut up the sea with doors, who said to it, *hitherto shalt thou come, but no further, and here shall thy proud waves be stayed.* The Psalter carries the same claim into worship: *God's way is in the sea, and His path in the great waters, yet His footsteps are not known* — the sea bears Him and does not thereby become knowable, does not leave a trail, because what walks on it is not displacing water the way a body displaces water but exercising a dominion over it that has no created analogue.

This is not incidental imagery available for any wonder-worker to borrow. In the world the Gospels were written into, treading the sea was not a feat a sufficiently empowered prophet could perform on loan. It was a predicate reserved, in the Scriptures Israel already had, for the Lord alone. So when Mark reports that Jesus came to the disciples, in the fourth watch of the night, walking upon the sea — the Vulgate's own verb, *ambulans super mare,* is Job's verb exactly — the account is not merely describing an impressive feat of physics. It is placing Jesus in the one grammatical slot the Old Testament had kept permanently reserved, and the disciples' terror, followed by their cry that this must be a ghost, is the narrative's own acknowledgment that what they are seeing does not fit any available category for a man. Mark then closes the scene with a sentence that only makes sense if the identification has landed: their hearts were hardened, because they had not understood about the loaves. The two miracles are read, by the Evangelist himself, as a single argument.

## III. Neither Substitution Nor a Suspension from Outside

Before asking which nature performed the act, it is worth running it through *Eating Stones's* own test, because the test rules out one bad answer before the right one can be stated. Satan's proposal at the stones was a substitution: the interior of the stone left untouched while the surface was made to claim the identity of bread — a lie told by the thing itself, about itself, at a word. Walking on the sea is not that. Nothing about the water is falsified. It remains, throughout the scene, fully and only water — wet, cold, capable of drowning a man, as it very nearly does to Peter within the same episode. Nothing about Christ's body is falsified either; it remains a real human body, with real weight, genuinely subject to the physics that governs every other body of comparable mass. No interior is overwritten. No creature is made to profess a vocation that is not its own. Whatever this act is, it is not the eating of stones.

Nor is it a suspension imposed from outside, the way an external cause might reach in and override a law belonging to something else. That picture would still treat the miracle as one agent acting upon a system foreign to it — God, from beyond nature, briefly overruling nature's own regularities, the way a hand might hold down a scale. But the entire argument of *Distancia* was that the divine Persons are not external to the created order in that sense at all; they are its Ground, sustaining every creature's Now from within the relation of dependence itself, with no distance between sustaining a thing and being present to it. If the Son is, as God, one of the Three who from moment to moment hold the water's own actuality in being — its weight, its surface tension, the very regularity by which it yields beneath a certain mass — then His walking upon it is not an intrusion into someone else's jurisdiction. It is the Ground exercising, at one located point, the same authority by which He is, at every other point, already holding the water in being. Nothing is suspended from outside, because there is no outside for the Ground to act from. This step is only as secure as the grounding metaphysic *Distancia* argued for and is here taken as given; granting that metaphysic, the conclusion about the sea follows. What changes is not the law but the terms on which, in this one Now, the Ground chooses to hold it.

## IV. One Operation, Two Principles: Maximus and the Theandric Act

The temptation, once substitution and external suspension are both excluded, is to reach for a third bad answer: that some third thing happened, neither human nor divine but a blend of the two, a new hybrid capacity belonging to neither nature as such. This is the very confusion Chalcedon spent its authority forbidding — two natures, without confusion, without change, without division, without separation — and it would be no advance to smuggle the confusion back in at the level of action after having excluded it at the level of being. The Church faced this exact temptation directly in the century after Chalcedon, when some, trying to honor the unity of Christ's Person, proposed that He had only a single operation, a single energeia, blending the human and the divine activity into one. The Third Council of Constantinople, in 680–681, closed that door as firmly as Chalcedon had closed the door on one nature: it defined two natural wills and two natural operations in the one Christ, concurring, the human will following and subject to the divine, not as though annihilated but as truly operative, without division and without confusion, in exactly the grammar Chalcedon had already established for the natures themselves.

Affirming two operations, however, only sharpens the question it answers: if the human and the divine activity are neither merged nor merely juxtaposed, what holds them as one act rather than two? The controversy had shifted the ground from what Christ is to how Christ acts, and it is precisely here that Maximus the Confessor becomes decisive. The council did not leave the unity of the act unexplained, and Maximus, the theologian most responsible for its victory, supplies the missing term. Drawing on a phrase from Pseudo-Dionysius — a kaine theandrike energeia, a new theandric operation[^1] — Maximus insists that Christ's acts are not divided into a human batch and a divine batch performed side by side, nor are they blended into a third thing belonging to neither nature. They are single acts, each one genuinely human and genuinely divine at once, because the one Person who acts possesses both natures entire and acts through both together, the human operation always moved by and never independent of the divine, the divine operation always terminating in and never bypassing the human. Theandric does not name a hybrid capacity. It names a single act correctly described only by keeping both natures' contributions distinct within it, the way Chalcedon required their being kept distinct in Christ's constitution. This is enhypostasis, from *Distancia,* applied to operation rather than to being: just as the human nature has no person of its own to stand at, so the human act at the sea has no independent agency of its own to stand at. It is genuinely operative — the feet are real feet, doing real walking — and it is not free-standing. Its principal is the same Word who, as God, is at that hour sustaining the very water beneath those feet.

## V. The Feet That Could Have Sunk

Matthew's account supplies, almost incidentally, the experiment that confirms the structure. Peter asks to come to Him on the water and is told to come; he walks, briefly, on the same sea; then, seeing the wind, he is afraid, begins to sink, and cries out to be saved. This scene has usually been read for its moral — a parable of faith and doubt — and that reading is not wrong. But read metaphysically, it is exactly the control the theandric account needs. If walking on water were a property that could simply be transferred to a human nature as such, conferred once and thereafter possessed, Peter's fear would be irrelevant to whether he sank; a transferred property does not evaporate because its possessor grows afraid. That he sinks the moment his attention shifts from Christ to the wind shows that no such property was ever his to keep. What held him up was never in his own nature at all, borrowed or otherwise. It was Christ's own dominion, extended to Peter contingently and moment to moment, precisely because dominion over the sea belongs to no creature's nature, only to the Person exercising it — and a dominion held only by extension can be, and here is, withdrawn the instant the one extended to turns his gaze toward the very chaos the dominion was holding off.

This is also why Christ's own feet, throughout the episode, remain genuinely capable of sinking in exactly the sense His human nature requires. The theandric account does not claim that His humanity was quietly exempted from the water's ordinary claim on any body of its weight — that would be the Eutychian move, humanity absorbed and its properties overridden by contact with divinity. His body is subject to water's law in the same way Peter's is; what is not the same is the operation holding the law's ordinary consequence at bay. Peter's capacity to sink is left to itself the instant fear breaks his attention. Christ's capacity to sink is never left to itself for a moment, because the Person whose humanity it is remains, without interruption and without needing to attend to it the way Peter must, the very Ground of the water He walks on. The two men's feet are equally human. Only one of them is also, without division, the sea's own sustaining cause.

## VI. Return to Gethsemane: The Same Act, the Opposite Pole

*Distancia* and the reading of Gethsemane it makes possible now turn out to be this essay's mirror rather than its precursor. At the sea, the divine operation is manifest and dominant — dominion over the water plainly on display — while the human operation is receptive, carried, sustained without needing itself to strain toward the result. In the garden the relation inverts without changing its structure at all: the human operation is manifest and dominant — a human will genuinely recoiling from death, a human body sweating blood under authentic terror — while the divine operation withdraws from display and does the quieter work of grounding that very terror's Now, holding the man capable of that fear in being through every instant of feeling it. What inverts between the scenes is which operation dominates in manifestation, not which leads in principle: the human will that recoils in the garden is, throughout, the same human operation Maximus described as always moved by and never independent of the divine, however fully it now fills the view. Neither scene suspends the other nature. Both scenes exhibit, from opposite sides of the same structure, the single theandric fact *Distancia* and *Eating Stones* each stated in their own vocabulary: one Person, two unconfused principles of operation, cooperating without division in every act, whichever nature that act happens to make visible. The sea shows what it looks like when the divine principle leads. The garden shows what it looks like when the human principle does. Chalcedon's grammar does not need a different clause for either. It needed only to be trusted to cover both, which is what this essay has tried to show that it does.

## VII. What This Essay Does Not Claim

Three boundaries are worth marking, in keeping with this corpus's practice. First, this essay has not attempted a history of the monoenergist and monothelite controversies in their full political and textual complexity; it has drawn from Maximus and the Third Council of Constantinople only the conceptual result needed here; the councils' own proceedings, and the case of Pope Honorius, are a much longer story left untold. Second, nothing here decides how many other Gospel acts — the multiplication of the loaves, the stilling of the storm, the various healings — divide between the theandric structure described here and the more strictly natural extensions of grace *Eating Stones* already covered under transformation; each would need its own reading rather than an assumed uniformity. Third, this essay has said nothing about how Peter's own contingent participation in Christ's dominion should be understood theologically — whether as a species of the same grace by which any creature participates in providence, or as something narrower proper to that single scene — and that question is left, deliberately, as a debt still owed.

What has been argued is narrower and, it is hoped, more securely grounded: that a located act performed by the incarnate Word is neither a human deed decorated with divine assistance nor a divine deed wearing a human costume, but one theandric operation, and that the sea, read this way, is not a different kind of Christological evidence from the garden. It is the same evidence, seen from the far side.

> *Qui graditur super fluctus maris.*
>
> It was never a title Christ borrowed for an afternoon. It was what it always was in Job — a claim about who alone can be its subject — now spoken not in poetry but in a single Person's feet, resting where no other feet could rest, and, three years later, in the same Person's sweat, falling like great drops of blood upon a garden no water could reach. The feet rest there not because the ground has been suspended beneath them, but because it has not: the water holds because the One walking on it is, at that hour, the One who had never ceased holding it in being — the Ground, for one night upon Galilee, standing upon what it never ceased to carry.

[^1]: The phrase itself has a contested history: read as a single divine-human energy, it became the proof-text of seventh-century monoenergism, the very position the Third Council of Constantinople condemned. Maximus retains the phrase but relocates its force in the word new — not one energy replacing two, but a single act of the one Person exercised through two unconfused operations at once.
