## I. The Curé

He was, by every worldly measure, unfit for the office. He nearly failed his seminary studies. He knew almost no Latin. His superiors doubted him, and the bishop who ordained him is said to have done so more from pity than from confidence. Jean-Baptiste-Marie Vianney was sent to Ars because Ars was thought to be a parish where he could do little harm.

He did more good there than most dioceses do in a century. He heard confessions sixteen and eighteen hours a day, for the better part of forty years, until pilgrims arrived from across France by the thousands to kneel before him. He fasted on little more than a few boiled potatoes a week. He was slandered, mocked by his own brother priests, and assaulted for years by what he called, without irony, the devil himself, in his own rectory at night. None of it moved him from his confessional, his altar, or his obedience to Rome.

The Church does not canonize the Curé of Ars for his brilliance. She canonizes him for his fidelity — to the sacraments as she has always understood them, to the priesthood as Christ instituted it, and to a bishop and a Pope he never once considered improving upon. On his feast, it seems fitting to write in defense of what he defended: the ordinary, unglamorous, sacramental priesthood that some who now govern the Church seem eager to dissolve.

It is worth saying plainly what that priesthood is for, since everything else here follows from it. The priest is not first the leader of a community, nor the organizer of a parish, nor the voice of a social program. He is the man through whom Christ continues to absolve sins and offer His sacrifice until the end of the age. Whatever else a parish needs, it needed that first, and Ars had almost nothing else.

## II. The Trojan Horse Inside the Walls

Cardinal Gerhard Müller, formerly the Church's chief guardian of doctrine, published an essay this week answering a request from the German bishops: that laymen and laywomen be permitted to preach the homily at Holy Mass. Rome had already said no. The German bishops asked again.

Müller's answer was not a matter of taste or discipline alone. He located the request at its root, in a denial of what he called the sacramental priesthood itself. The Second Vatican Council had taught that the Liturgy of the Word and the Liturgy of the Eucharist form a single act of worship, not two adjacent activities that different people might perform. Justin Martyr, writing in the second century, already described the same unity: the one who presides interprets the Gospel and then offers the Eucharist, the deacons assisting him in both. This is not medieval accretion. It is apostolic memory.

To hand the homily to a layperson while reserving the altar to the priest is not, on Müller's reading, a broadening of ministry. It is a quiet argument that the priest's preaching and the priest's sacrifice can be separated — that ordination confers authority over bread and wine but not over the proclamation that gives them their meaning. Once that separation is granted, the priesthood is no longer a sacramental configuration to Christ. It becomes a delegated function, one ministry among several that the community might redistribute as convenience allows. That is a Protestant ecclesiology wearing Catholic vestments, and it is precisely what Müller named it: a way of denying the sacramental priesthood without having the courage to say so directly.

This is the shape of the Trojan Horse. It rarely announces itself as heresy. It arrives as pastoral accommodation, as inclusion, as keeping pace with a modern laity that wants to participate. The wood is carved beautifully. It is only inside the walls that the soldiers climb out — a diaconate stripped of its assisting role, a priesthood reduced to a rubric, a laity told that its universal priesthood already contained everything the ordained priesthood claimed to add. One wonders what the Curé of Ars would have thought of a Church embarrassed to speak about sin while perfectly comfortable discussing emissions targets.

## III. Two Thousand Years

What is remarkable is not that men try this. Every century has produced its own version of the same proposal — that this generation, unlike all the ones before it, has finally understood something the Apostles missed. What is remarkable is that none of it has worked. Arius nearly took the whole Church with him in the fourth century, and there were decades when the bishops themselves, by overwhelming majority, held his position. Rome fell. Byzantium fell. Entire nations that were once the heartland of Catholic Christendom are now missionary territory. And still, two thousand years later, the successor of a fisherman signs the documents.

Christ did not promise His Church a smooth two millennia. He promised the opposite — that the gates of hell would mount a siege against it and fail to prevail. A siege presumes an enemy at the walls, not an absent one. The promise was never that bishops would be uniformly holy, or that a synod could not err, or that an entire episcopal conference might not, for a season, drift toward confusion. The promise was narrower and more durable than that: that the gates would not prevail. Not that they would not attack.

This is why the loyal priest does not need to win every argument in his own lifetime to remain faithful. Vianney did not live to see most of the fruit of Ars. He lived and died a simple curé in a village most Frenchmen had never heard of. The fruit was not his to secure. It was his to be faithful within. The same is true now for every priest who stands at an altar the parish council would rather see rearranged, or preaches a homily his bishop finds inconveniently traditional, or hears confessions no one is required to attend anymore. He is not responsible for outlasting the German bishops' conference. He is responsible for being found, at the end, still standing where Peter told him to stand.

## IV. Render Unto Caesar

A second confusion runs alongside the first, and it concerns not who may preach but what ought to be preached. Christ was asked whether it was lawful to pay tribute to Caesar, and He asked for a coin, asked whose image was stamped on it, and told His questioners to render to Caesar what bore Caesar's image and to God what bore God's. The distinction was not a shrug. It was a boundary — a recognition that temporal governance and divine worship are not the same currency, and that confusing them cheats both.

Much of the Church's public voice at present has inverted that order. Ample energy goes toward carbon targets and the political mechanics of environmental policy — questions on which the Magisterium has no special competence beyond what any attentive layman could offer. Meanwhile the four last things have nearly disappeared from ordinary preaching. Stewardship of creation is a genuine obligation; nothing here denies it. But it is Caesar's coin, not God's, when it crowds out the one thing only the Church can give: the sacraments, and the truth about a soul that refuses them forever. A shepherd who spends his homilies on recycling and his silence on repentance has not modernized the Gospel. He has rendered to Caesar what belongs to God, and kept for himself a coin he was never asked to mint.

## V. The Last Things, Unspoken

Hell is not a doctrine the Church invented to frighten peasants into obedience; it is a doctrine Christ Himself taught more plainly than almost any other, and one His Church has never had the authority to retract. Yet ask a Catholic under forty when he last heard a homily on the subject, and the honest answer is usually: never, or not that he can recall.

The reticence is understandable and, still, indefensible. Hell is difficult to preach without cruelty, and pastors are rightly wary of driving souls to despair rather than repentance. But the remedy for a hard doctrine badly preached is not silence; it is better preaching. Hell, rightly understood, is not God's vindictiveness toward the creature He made. It is the creature's own will, fixed forever against the only thing that could satisfy it, closing its own door from the inside. That is not a threat manufactured to control behavior. It is a mercy: a true account of the stakes, given to people who deserve to know what their choices weigh.

A priesthood that stops speaking of hell does not thereby make hell less real. It simply ensures that fewer of its people are warned before the door closes. St. John Vianney spent whole nights in tears over exactly this — parishioners who lived as though eternity were a rumor. He did not consider it uncharitable to tell them otherwise. He considered the alternative unthinkable.

## VI. The Center of the Room

A church building teaches with its architecture before a single word is preached in it. For two thousand years the instinctive grammar of Catholic sacred space put one thing at the center: the tabernacle, the burning lamp beside it announcing that the Lord of the house was, in fact, in the house. Whatever else moved around it — pulpit, choir, congregation — the center held.

In a great many churches built or renovated in the last half-century, the center no longer holds. The tabernacle has been relocated to a side chapel, sometimes explained as making room for a freestanding altar facing the people, sometimes as decluttering the sanctuary, sometimes without explanation at all. The theological argument, when one is offered, usually turns on the difference between Christ present in the assembly and Christ present in the Blessed Sacrament, as though the second were a nostalgic distraction from the first rather than its source.

The building did not stop being sacred when this happened. But it stopped teaching what it once taught wordlessly to every child who walked through its doors: that this room exists because something — someone — is here. A church whose architecture no longer says that has been handed the same proposal Müller identified in the homily debate, applied now to stone rather than to speech: that the sacramental center of Catholic worship can be quietly moved aside without loss, so long as the community feels included in what remains.

<br>

## VII. What the Curé Still Says

None of this is new, which is precisely the point. The wish to make a sign say something other than what it was made to say is the oldest proposal there is, older than any bishops' conference, older than the Church herself. It was offered first in a garden, and again in a wilderness, to Christ Himself, and He refused it there so that His Church would have the pattern for refusing it everywhere after.

St. John Vianney refused it in the smallest possible arena — one parish, one confessional, one obscure French village — and the whole Church still keeps his feast because of it. He did not reform Rome. He did not out-argue his critics. He simply remained where his ordination had placed him, offering the sacraments he had been given no authority to redesign, until the fidelity itself became the argument.

That is still available to every priest who wants it, in every diocese, including the ones presently most eager to smuggle their Trojan Horse past two thousand years of received Tradition. The gates have not prevailed yet. They did not prevail against Arius, and they will not prevail against a synod's working paper. The rock does not depend on the men presently standing on it. It depends on the One who named it.

Somewhere tonight another village priest will extinguish the sanctuary lamps, lock the church doors, and leave unnoticed after hearing confessions no newspaper will ever report. The Church has endured for two thousand years not because such men were famous, but because they remained where Christ placed them. The Rock still bears them, because the Rock has not resigned.

When I was an altar boy in the Chapel of Mary Help of Christians in Granada, Nicaragua, the priests would pause for a moment before a small framed inscription, as if drawing breath before crossing into the sacred. It bore lines that seemed to kindle the silence of the sanctuary:

> *Priest of Christ,*
> *offer this Holy Mass as if it were your first Mass,*
> *your last Mass,*
> *your only Mass.*

Today, sadly, in many parishes the celebrant begins his approach to the altar by walking through an outer corridor, passing by the merchandise arranged there — the tamales, the tacos, the trays of food — as though the fair came before the Sacrifice. And by the time he reaches the altar, the reason he is there has already faded from his soul: for whom he stands there, and above all, for Whom he stands there.
