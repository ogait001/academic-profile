## Abstract

Two objections were raised against Section V of *The Plan and the Hand,* and this essay answers both by refusing to answer them separately. The first, Nelson Pike's, holds that relocating divine knowledge from time to eternity does not remove its fixity, only its date, and so does not by itself secure the kind of freedom the essay claimed for Pilate. The second observes that Molinism, God's middle knowledge of what free creatures would do in any circumstance, is a live and respectable alternative the essay passed over in silence. Both objections, traced far enough, arrive at the same door: the identity, established in *The Ground That Knows,* between God's grounding of a creature's Now and God's knowing of it, one act named twice. That identity answers Pike more thoroughly than mere relocation to eternity does, because it removes the very item, a detachable belief standing in an entailment relation to the choice, that his argument needs. The same identity, followed honestly, forecloses Molinism rather than merely declining it, because grounding cannot reach a merely possible circumstance that was never held in being to sustain. The two results are not independent purchases. They are the same boundary, priced once and paid on both sides.

<br>

## I. Two Objections, One Refusal

*The Plan and the Hand* answered a narrower question than the one this essay takes up. It asked whether Pilate could be guilty of a sentence Scripture says was written in advance, and it answered, rightly, by distinguishing what the plan required from who was required to supply it, and by invoking the classical move that God's knowing of Pilate's choice belongs to eternity rather than to time, so that it does not sit earlier on the same road Pilate walks and cannot coerce him the way an earlier cause coerces a later effect. That is the Boethian solution, and it is not wrong. It is, however, a solution stated with more confidence than the literature built around it actually has, and two objections were raised against it worth taking seriously rather than absorbing by restatement.

<br>

The first objection is Nelson Pike's, and it does not go away merely because God's knowledge is timeless rather than antecedent. The second is that Molinism, the thesis that God possesses, logically prior to any decree, knowledge of what every possible free creature would do in every possible circumstance, is a mainstream Catholic alternative to the Boethian move, and the earlier essay's silence about it reads as though eternity were the only orthodox answer on offer rather than the oldest of several.

<br>

This essay does not patch Section V twice, once for each objection. It traces both objections down to a decision this corpus already made, one essay earlier, in *The Ground That Knows:* the identity of grounding and knowing, the thesis that for God to sustain the Now in which a creature's act occurs and to know that act are not two acts but one, named twice. That identity turns out to bear on Pike's objection and on Molinism in a single motion, and the two results are not free-standing. They are the same fact, seen from two directions, and the second half of this essay is devoted to showing that they cost exactly the same thing.

<br>

## II. The Fact Pike's Argument Needs

Pike's argument, first pressed in 1965 and refined by decades of reply and counter-reply since, does not depend on God's knowledge sitting earlier in time than the choice it concerns. It depends on something narrower and harder to dislodge: that God's knowledge is an item, a belief or a vision, ontologically distinct from the choice it is knowledge of, standing to that choice in a relation of strict entailment. If such an item exists, fixed and true no matter when or how it is fixed, then the choice it entails is, in the relevant sense, not up to the one who makes it. The agent lacks what philosophers call the power to do otherwise, not because anything pushes the choice from behind, but because nothing the agent could do would be compatible with the item's being what it already, ineradicably, is.

<br>

The classical reply relocates the item rather than removing it. God does not foreknow, in the sense of forming a belief earlier along the timeline and waiting for the world to catch up; God knows, timelessly and simultaneously, the whole of what creatures do within time, the way a person standing above a road sees at once what travelers below pass through in sequence. Since the vision is not earlier than the choice, the fixity of the past, the principle that no one now has power over what has already happened, gets no purchase on it. This is real progress against the crudest form of the objection, the one that pictures God peering down the timeline and locking in an outcome before the agent arrives at it.

<br>

It is not, however, a full answer, and Pike's later interlocutors were right to say so. The vision may not be earlier in time, but it is still an item: a state of affairs, obtaining timelessly and necessarily, that God's knowledge that Pilate condemns Christ is true. And a timelessly true item generates its own version of the fixity problem, sometimes called the translation argument. If it is timelessly true that God knows Pilate will rule as he does, then at any moment before Pilate rules, including the moment in which he is weighing Caesar's friendship against his own conviction of the prisoner's innocence, the proposition God knows, timelessly, that Pilate condemns him is already true, in the ordinary sense that true propositions do not become true later. That proposition is not future to Pilate; his own choice is what it is timelessly about. But its truth does not wait on his choice reaching completion any more than the past does, because eternity does not pass and cannot be altered from within time any more than yesterday can. Something, then, is fixed relative to Pilate at every moment of his deliberation, a timeless fact rather than a past one, and the objection Section V thought it had dissolved by moving the vantage point turns out to have followed the vantage point to its new address.

<br>

## III. Why the Ground Has No Second Thing

The reply that actually closes this door was available to this corpus before Pike's objection was ever raised against it, because *The Ground That Knows* was not written to answer Pike. It was written to answer a narrower question, what divine knowledge positively is, and it answered by denying that divine knowing is a second act at all. Reception, the essay argued, is the whole structure of finite knowing, not one feature among others: to know by reception is to be an interior at which something arrives from beyond itself. A God who knew even by reception perfected, immediate, without medium or delay, would still be positioned as a knower over against a world already there to be known, still the terminus of a crossing, only one made infinitesimally thin. The essay's positive claim was the opposite pole: not the interior to which the world arrives, but the ground in which the arriving occurs, so that grounding a creature's Now and knowing its content are one relation, distinguished by us because we know only by reception and have no other word, but not two acts in Him.

<br>

Pike's argument, examined again in this light, needs exactly the structure that identity refuses. It needs God's knowledge to be a state distinct from Pilate's choice, standing to it in an entailment relation, so that the state's fixity can be asked about independently of the choice's own actuality, which is precisely how the translation argument gets its grip: it treats God knows that Pilate condemns him as a proposition that could, in principle, be evaluated for truth apart from asking whether Pilate has actually condemned him, the way any ordinary belief-report can be evaluated apart from the state of the world it describes turning out one way or another. But on the identity thesis there is no such proposition available for separate evaluation. There is one relation, the sustaining of Pilate's actual Now, and grounding and knowing are what we call it when we attend to two different things about that single relation: grounding when we attend to the fact that the Now depends on God for its being, knowing when we attend to the fact that this same dependence leaves no content of the Now exterior to its ground. Asking whether God's knowledge could have been different while Pilate's choice remained the same is not a hard question on this account. It is not a question that has content, any more than asking whether the ground of a building could have been different while the building standing on it remained exactly the same building. The translation argument needs two things to compare. The identity thesis leaves it one.

<br>

None of this denies that Pilate condemns Christ, or that the proposition saying so is true; Pilate's actual choice is its truthmaker. What is denied is a second item, a divine knowledge-state standing apart from that choice and available to be fixed independently of it. The distinction matters, and it is worth marking now, because the Molinist half of this essay turns on a truth that has no actual choice behind it — and there the concession made here for Pilate is exactly the one that cannot be extended.

<br>

This is a stronger closure than the Boethian relocation offers by itself, and it is worth being precise about why. Relocating knowledge from time to eternity answers the objection that treats fixity as a matter of temporal priority; it does not answer the objection that treats fixity as a matter of there being a distinct, necessarily true item at all, wherever it is located. The identity of grounding and knowing answers that second, harder objection directly, because it denies the item, not merely its address.

<br>

## IV. What the Move Does Not Buy

Honesty requires marking where this stops rather than presenting it as a total victory. Removing the detachable item removes the specific mechanism Pike's argument relies on, the entailing fact standing apart from the choice it entails. It does not, by itself, answer a related and more stubborn question: whether the single act, grounding-that-is-knowing, inclusive as it is of Pilate's actual choice, could have terminated in a different choice while remaining, in every other respect, the same act of God. This is not Pike's question restated. Pike needed a second item to point at; this question needs none, and that is what makes it harder to wave off. It is the question theological determinism has always eventually had to face, the same seam Molinist critics have pressed for four centuries against Bañezian accounts of physical premotion: a grounding sufficiently thorough to leave nothing in the creature's act exterior to it can start to look, from a certain angle, less like the condition of the act's freedom and more like its efficient cause under another name, however careful the vocabulary is kept.

<br>

The lemniscate essay gestures toward an answer without fully supplying one. Its distinction between the eternal perspective, the whole curve present at once, and the creaturely perspective, sequential traversal in which the arc ahead has genuinely not yet been reached, names a compatibility between simultaneous vision and open experience without showing, at the level of metaphysics rather than geometry, why the single sustaining act does not settle, from its own side, which arc gets traversed. That gap is real, and this essay does not close it. What it can say is narrower and true: the gap that remains is not Pike's gap. Pike's objection needed a detachable fact, and detachable facts are what the identity thesis was built to refuse. The residual worry about modal collapse needs something else, an account of how one act can genuinely leave open, from within, more than one terminus, and that account belongs to a different essay than this one. Marking the boundary here is not evasion. It is the discipline this corpus has followed before: a positive claim advanced past what has actually been earned is the discarded model returning in different clothes.

<br>

Three questions run through this, and the essay keeps them apart on purpose: what divine knowing is, what it is for a Now to be grounded, and whether that grounding leaves open more than one terminus. The first two are answered far enough to starve Pike's argument and to close Molinism out; the third is the one left standing, and it is left standing deliberately.

<br>

## V. What Molina Needed

Luis de Molina proposed, in the *Concordia,* a knowledge in God distinct from two the tradition already recognized. Natural knowledge covers all necessary truths and every possibility, logically prior to any decision God makes and true independently of what he wills. Free knowledge covers what actually will occur, fixed only once God has decreed which possibility to actualize. Between them, posterior to natural knowledge and prior to the free, Molina placed a third kind, middle knowledge, *scientia media:* God's knowledge, prior to his own decree and therefore prior to his willing anything about this world in particular, of what every possible free creature would do, not merely could do, in every possible circumstance in which that creature might be placed. Call these counterfactuals of freedom. Given this circumstance, this creature would freely choose that. Molina's claim was that such counterfactuals have truth values already, before God decides anything, and that God consults them the way an author consults the settled facts about a character before choosing which scene to write.

<br>

The appeal of this picture is considerable, and it explains why serious theologians have reached for it rather than the Boethian move. It appears to buy both halves of what *The Plan and the Hand* wanted for Pilate at once, and to buy them more cheaply. Providence remains meticulous, because God selects, from among the infinite array of possible worlds whose every counterfactual of creaturely freedom he already knows, the one world he actualizes, down to Pilate standing before a particular prisoner on a particular morning. And freedom remains fully libertarian, because the counterfactuals themselves are not caused by God's will, not chosen by him, merely known and then acted on; what Pilate would freely do in that courtyard is, on this account, no more up to God than what Pilate actually does is up to Pilate's neighbor. Thomas Flint has defended the position at length in recent decades, and William Lane Craig has done much to popularize it, and it remains, as the objection to Section V correctly said, a live option inside orthodoxy rather than a fringe one.

<br>

It has also faced, since not long after Molina proposed it, an objection sharp enough that many philosophers regard it as unanswered rather than merely contested. Robert Adams pressed it classically: what makes a counterfactual of freedom true, if not God's will, since the whole point of middle knowledge is that these truths are prior to and independent of anything God decides, and not the creature's actual choice either, since in the overwhelming majority of possible circumstances the creature in question never exists to choose anything at all. A truth needs, ordinarily, something that makes it true, a truthmaker. Molina's counterfactuals are asked to be true without one: not grounded in the divine will, on pain of collapsing back into a decree that determines them, and not grounded in any actual exercise of creaturely freedom, since most of what middle knowledge covers is never exercised by anyone. William Hasker pressed the same difficulty from a different angle and found no better answer waiting. This is called the grounding objection to Molinism, and it is the reason the position, respectable as it remains, has never gone uncontested — as the objection to Section V rightly implied, it is a live option, not a closed one.

<br>

## VI. Why This Ground Cannot Hold What Never Was

It would be convenient to leave Molinism there, an alternative this corpus simply prefers not to take, a road not travelled rather than a road closed. That would understate the actual relationship, and understating it is the thing worth correcting. The identity established in *The Ground That Knows* does not merely decline middle knowledge. It rules the specific thing middle knowledge requires out of the only category this corpus has for divine knowing at all.

<br>

The identity says that for God, knowing simply is the sustaining of a creature's Now in being, with nothing of that Now exterior to the ground that holds it. Knowing, on this account, has no content that is not, at some point, actually held in being by the grounding act. This is not a restriction added to the identity from outside; it is the identity's whole substance, the very thing that let Section III of this essay close Pike's door. But a counterfactual of freedom, in Molina's own careful specification, is a truth about a creature in a circumstance that is never actualized, a Pilate who is never placed before this particular prisoner, or a Judas who never receives this particular thirty pieces of silver, because in the world God in fact chooses to actualize, that circumstance never arises for that possible person. There is no Now there. Nothing is held in being at that juncture, because that juncture never becomes a juncture at all; it remains a bare possibility, considered and passed over. And if knowing simply is the sustaining of an actual Now, then a circumstance with no Now to sustain is not an object of divine knowing under the identity thesis at all. The claim is not that such a circumstance is unintelligible, nor that God lacks all knowledge of the possible; it is the narrower architectural claim that a relation to a merely possible circumstance cannot be this corpus's knowing unless it is also a grounding, and if it is not a grounding, the identity has already been set aside.

<br>

A Molinist may answer that the counterfactual needs no actual circumstance to make it true: its truth is primitive, or modal, or read off the structure of possible worlds themselves, so that God need sustain no Now in order to know it. But that answer, granted in full, is the present point rather than a reply to it. It posits a relation running from God to a counterfactual truth without running through the grounding of any actual Now — knowing that is not grounding, the very seam *The Ground That Knows* was written to close. The counterfactual is then grounded in the divine act, and loses the prevolitional independence Molina built it to have; or grounded in no actual Now, and requires a mode of divine knowing this corpus does not possess; or grounded in nothing at all, and is not, by this corpus's only measure, knowing. Within the architecture established here, there is no fourth place for it to stand.

<br>

The choice this leaves is not between accepting Molinism and remaining silent about it. It is between two costs, and both are real. Either the corpus reopens the identity thesis to admit a second kind of divine knowing after all, one that reaches merely possible, never-actualized circumstances, in which case the very structure Section II of *The Ground That Knows* spent its argument closing, a knower turned toward an object standing over against it, an object that in this case does not even exist to be turned toward, comes back in through the door that essay worked hardest to shut. Or the corpus holds the identity as stated and accepts that it cannot say, any more than Molina's own critics could say on his behalf, what would make a counterfactual of Pilate's freedom true in a circumstance neither actualized by God's decree nor ever chosen by any actual Pilate. The grounding objection that has dogged Molinism since Adams is not a difficulty this corpus can claim to have avoided by taking a different classical route. It is a difficulty this corpus's own architecture predicts and explains, from the inside, better than Molinism's defenders have managed to answer it from within their own.

<br>

## VII. The Single Coin

Set the two results beside each other and they stop looking like two separate purchases made from the same essay. Pike's objection needed an item: a state of divine knowledge distinct from Pilate's choice, existing and fixed independently of it, available to be asked about counterfactually. The identity of grounding and knowing removes that item by refusing to let divine knowing be anything other than the sustaining of what is actually held in being. Molinism needed the opposite half of the very same allowance: knowledge that reaches past the boundary of the actual, to circumstances no Now was ever opened to hold. The same refusal that starves Pike's argument of its item leaves Molina's counterfactuals without an actual ground, and for the identical reason. Nothing is known that is not held in being; nothing is held in being that is not, in the relevant sense, actual. Pike's fact fails to exist because there is no knowing apart from the actual. Molina's counterfactual fails to have a ground to stand on for exactly the same want.

<br>

This is what the title names. The identity is not a tool this corpus reaches for when Pike is knocking and sets down again when Molinism is mentioned. It is one acquisition with two consequences, and the price of the first consequence is the second. A reader is welcome to prefer Molina's picture, meticulous providence bought through counterfactuals God consults rather than a ground that includes what it sustains, and many careful theologians have. But that preference is not compatible with also keeping the identity thesis this corpus has already spent an essay establishing. The two are not neighbors on the same shelf. Taking one is declining the other, not out of a preference stated once and left unexamined, but as a direct entailment of a commitment already made.

<br>

## VIII. Return to Pilate

None of this changes the conclusion *The Plan and the Hand* reached about the man with the basin. It sharpens the ground that conclusion stands on and marks honestly where that ground still has an edge. Pilate's guilt was never secured by there being no fact about his choice; it was secured by there being no fact about his choice detachable from the choice itself, available to have fixed him from outside the very act in which his fear of Caesar outweighed his conviction of an innocent man. That is what the identity of grounding and knowing delivers, more securely than the eternity move alone could, since eternity answers the objection about when the fact obtains and the identity answers the harder objection about whether there is a separate fact at all. What it does not yet deliver, and what this essay has been careful not to claim, is a full account of why the single act that included Pilate's choice could not, from its own side, have included a different one. That question stands open, honestly, and remains one this corpus has not yet resolved, and it is the debt this essay incurs even as it pays down the two it inherited.

<br>

What can be said now, that could not quite be said before, is that this corpus does not owe Molinism an apology for the silence Section V left around it. The silence was not an oversight waiting to be corrected by a footnote acknowledging a respectable alternative. It was, without either essay knowing it at the time, already the consequence of a choice made earlier, in an essay about what it means for a Ground to hold a Now open with nothing left outside the holding. Some doors, once a foundation is poured, are no longer live options standing beside the house. They are the rooms the house was built without, and knowing which rooms those are, and why, is worth more than leaving the plan silent about them.
