> *Quiero morir cuando decline el día,*
> *en alta mar y con la cara al cielo,*
> *donde parezca sueño la agonía,*
> *y el alma, un ave que remonta el vuelo.*
>
> *No escuchar los últimos instantes,*
> *ya con el cielo y con el mar a solas,*
> *más voces ni plegarias sollozantes*
> *que el majestuoso tumbo de las olas.*
>
> *Morir cuando la luz, triste, retira*
> *sus áureas redes de la onda verde,*
> *y ser como ese sol que lento expira:*
> *algo muy luminoso que se pierde.*
>
> *Morir, y joven: antes que destruya*
> *el tiempo aleve la gentil corona;*
> *cuando la vida dice aún: soy tuya,*
> *aunque sepamos bien que nos traiciona.*
>
> — Manuel Gutiérrez Nájera, *"Para entonces"* (1887)

## I. The Poem I Once Loved

There is a way of loving a poem that is really a way of not yet having read it. For years "Para entonces" struck me the way it has struck several generations of Spanish-language readers: as one of the most beautiful death-wishes ever written, four quatrains in which a man asks to die at sunset, at sea, alone, and is granted the request by the poem itself, which stages it flawlessly. I did not read it as confession. I read it as elegy, the kind that flatters the reader for being moved by it. It took a long detour through pride — through what pride actually is, as opposed to what we assume it is — to notice that the poem is not describing a death. It is issuing instructions.

<br>

The prior essays in this series established a single claim from several directions: pride is not thinking highly of oneself. Pride is making oneself the reference point from which everything is measured, and self-abasement is not its opposite but its inversion — the same axis, the sign reversed. Humility is neither of these positions. It is the removal of the axis, the self consenting, at the crossing point, to be ordered by what it is not. What follows is an attempt to read "Para entonces" as a specimen of the first geometry rather than the third — not to accuse a dead poet of a private vice, but because the poem is unusually precise about how that geometry feels from the inside, and precision is rare enough to be worth the trouble of examining.

<br>

## II. The Verb Before the Content

Before any image arrives — the sea, the sky, the dying light — the poem has already committed itself grammatically. *Quiero morir.* I want to die. First person, active voice, from a verb of volition — and the distinction that matters is not active against passive but agentive self-reference against receptive: where the self stands in relation to the act, not merely which voice the verb carries. The bare infinitive *morir* then recurs as anaphora at the head of three of the four quatrains, doing more work than decoration. This is not a man wondering how death will come, or asking that it come gently, or asking to be forgiven when it comes. It is a man specifying terms.

<br>

This distinction has a name already, from a different case examined in *The Place That Is Given:* two sentences a life can be, and they are grammatical opposites before they are moral ones. *Fiat mihi secundum verbum tuum* — let it be done to me according to your word — the self enters the sentence as the dative recipient (*mihi*), the one to whom it is done, rather than the nominative agent who does it, the measure not the self's own. Against it, *I did it my way* — active voice, first person, total authorship, the whole life objectified into a thing I made, my way, the measure mine end to end. "Para entonces" is written entirely in the second grammar. It does not ask to be received by death. It composes death, the way a director composes a shot, and the composition is the poem's real content — sunset for lighting, open water for isolation, face turned upward for the final image. The soul in line four does not surrender either: it becomes *un ave que remonta el vuelo,* a bird still climbing, still ascending under its own power at the very moment the man is supposedly giving himself up. Even the escape is active voice.

<br>

## III. Four Refusals

*The Servant of Servants* reads Mary's five Joyful Mysteries as five concrete motions of a de-centered will: disclosing the real lack without performing it; ascending toward the neighbor rather than negotiating a comfortable descent; taking the place that is given rather than the place that could be claimed; standing in the common line rather than the exempt one; and keeping what cannot yet be understood rather than forcing it to one's own measure. "Para entonces" does not merely fail these five motions. It inverts them with something close to precision.

<br>

The first quatrain claims a place rather than accepting one. There is no manger here, no room given because no other room was offered; there is a scene assembled to specification — sunset, open sea, face to the sky — a place authored rather than received. The second quatrain is the sharpest of the four, and the one worth sitting with longest: *No escuchar los… más voces ni plegarias sollozantes.* Not merely a wish to die without suffering witnesses. A request that prayer itself be out of earshot. This is the refusal of the common line taken to its limit — not simply declining the exempt lounge but building a private one at sea, beyond the reach of anyone who might intercede. Intercession is precisely what the will refuses here, because to be prayed for is to be, however slightly, acted upon by someone else's love, and this is a will that has already decided the terms are its own.

<br>

The third quatrain performs the lack instead of disclosing it — not *how shall this be,* Mary's honest admission of a condition she does not understand, but a fully resolved image: the self as the setting sun, expiring on schedule, luminous to the last. There is no mystery kept here, nothing carried unresolved; everything is already composed into the vocabulary of beauty. And the fourth quatrain is fear dressed as courage — not fear of dying, but fear of decline, of being outlived by one's own decay, of losing *la gentil corona* to time before the crown can be surrendered on the wearer's own terms. Of the five motions, only the disclosure of a real lack has any presence at all, and even that is inverted into its opposite: the poem discloses nothing it doesn't already control.

<br>

## IV. The Sun as Self-Portrait

The third stanza deserves its own attention, because it contains the poem's clearest act of self-flattery, and self-flattery is pride's native dialect. Vanity concerns the image the self presents; pride concerns who determines the measure. The vanity of a beautiful death is not the charge but the evidence: the wish to be seen as a sunset betrays the deeper wish to be the one who sets the terms of the seeing. *Y ser como ese sol que lento expira: algo muy luminoso que se pierde.* To be like that sun which slowly expires: something very luminous that is lost. The comparison does real work. The sun is not merely beautiful; it is cosmic, inevitable, admired by everyone who has ever watched one set, and entirely unaware of being watched. The speaker asks to borrow all three properties at once — beauty, inevitability, and an audience — while insisting, one stanza earlier, that no one be present to supply the audience. The poem wants to be witnessed and wants no witnesses, and resolves the contradiction by making the sea and sky into witnesses instead, an audience that will not weep, will not pray, and above all will not intercede. Nature is safe because nature cannot love him back.

<br>

This is the harder question underneath the comparison: is a man who compares his death to a sunset asking to disappear, or asking to be remembered as a sunset? *The Place That Is Given* names the two faces of pride as inflated and deflated, but notes that a third, subtler move is available — pride does not always claim the higher seat outright; sometimes it claims a seat so aesthetically final that no comparison is possible, and finality itself becomes the exaltation. To die like the sun is not modesty. It is the largest claim available to a mortal, made to sound like resignation.

<br>

## V. What Death Undresses, What the Poem Redresses

*The Am That Remains* makes a specific claim about what death actually does: it is the removal of the chain of interference — sensation, then passion, then discursive thought — down to the soul's own direct apprehension, the knowing that was always underneath the noise. Death, on that account, is a stripping, not a staging. "Para entonces" asks for the opposite motion at the exact moment it claims to be embracing the first. Every image in the poem is maximally sensory — the color of the light, the gold nets withdrawing from the green water, the face turned toward the open sky, the particular hour of decline. This is not a soul preparing to have its senses removed. It is a soul asking that its senses be given one last, perfectly composed object to receive, so that the final data received by a human consciousness is a scene of its own arranging.

<br>

Put the two essays side by side and the contrast sharpens: the vehicle was never the destination. "Para entonces" never gets past the vehicle. It does not ask what remains when the loops fall silent. It asks for one more loop, the best one, run once more at maximum intensity, and then — nothing is said about after. The poem ends at the moment of expiration, not a line beyond it, because the after is precisely the part the poem has no interest in. The self that wanted to author its own ending has nothing prepared for the part it cannot author.

<br>

## VI. The Harder Face

None of this makes the poem ugly, and that is the point worth insisting on. *The Place That Is Given* distinguishes two failures of humility by how they sound: self-abasement is easy to refuse because no one is seduced by a man performing his own worthlessness, while the other face of pride — the one that stands up straight and sings — sounds exactly like courage, like integrity, like a man who regrets nothing. "Para entonces" belongs entirely to the second category. Nothing about it whines. Nothing about it asks for pity. It has been read for over a century as brave, as serene, as a mature man's peace with mortality, and it earns that reading honestly — the craftsmanship is real, the melancholy is real, the wish to go before ugliness sets in is a wish nearly everyone has felt at least once. That is exactly why it is a better specimen than an obvious boast would be. Arrogance is easy to name. A death wish this well composed is not.

<br>

It is worth resisting the temptation to flatten this into a period failing, as though Nájera's Modernismo simply had bad theology and a better century would have written differently. The aestheticization of death was a convention of its moment, but the poem's appeal has outlasted the movement precisely because the desire it names is not historically local. Every era produces its own version of the wish to arrange the unarrangeable, to be the author of the one event that by definition removes the author. The particular gold light and green water are fin-de-siècle. The wish underneath them is not dated at all.

<br>

## VII. Coda: The Sentence Not Yet Spoken

The poem stops exactly where the difficulty begins. It never reaches the crossing point — the instant, named across this series, at which the will's long orientation comes to its point and is either turned or fixed. "Para entonces" is composed entirely of the approach to that instant: the wanting, the staging, the specifications. This is where the topology earns its place rather than decorates: it marks the boundary of authorship. The approach can be authored; the crossing cannot. It closes on a note of defiant vitality — *la vida dice aún: soy tuya, aunque sepamos bien que nos traiciona* — life still declaring itself his, even while both parties know the declaration is a betrayal in progress. That is an honest last line, more honest than the three stanzas that precede it, because it finally admits what the whole poem has been quietly managing: that the self saying *quiero morir* is not at peace with dying at all. It is trying to get ahead of an ending it cannot control by writing the one part it still can — the composition, not the surrender.

<br>

What the poem cannot confess, because confessing it would undo the poem, is that the scene it wants — utterly alone, utterly self-authored, beyond the reach of anyone's prayer — is not a picture of dying well. It is a picture of dying exactly the way one lived, if one lived making oneself the reference point for everything, including, at the last, one's own death.

<br>

The poem does not ask to be forgiven for wanting to die. It asks to be forgiven for wanting to be the one who arranges it.
