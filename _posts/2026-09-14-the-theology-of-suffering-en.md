## A Note on Method

This essay continues the framework of the author's prior work and introduces no new instrument. It draws out, at full length, a distinction the corpus has so far left implicit: the distinction between suffering as content and the graced turning of the will toward that content as the site of sanctification. The distinction was gestured at in [*The Cross We Think We See: On the History We Carry and the Defeat We Read Into the Cross*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophical-theology/theology-of-the-cross/christian-theology/2026/09/14/the-cross-we-think-we-see-en.html) §VII, "The Danger of Calling Defeat Holy," which named the error and moved on because the essay's business lay elsewhere. This essay is the fuller argument that section left owing.

Three debts must be named before the argument begins. [*Where Is God? Suffering, the Present Moment, and the Ground that does not intervene*](https://oscargaitan.org/catholic-theology/philosophy-of-time/ontology/2026/05/05/where-is-god.html)
established that God sustains the Now in which suffering occurs without being the author of the evil in it, and distinguished several kinds of suffering that a single undifferentiated theology of suffering cannot honestly cover. [*Not Gepetto: Why Saramago's question assumes the wrong God*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophical%20theology/atonement/free%20will/augustine/thomas%20aquinas/metaphysics/2026/07/20/not-gepetto.html) established that evil is not a rival substance to be subtracted by a stronger hand but a disorder of the will, and that force applied to the wrong order of problem does not heal what it strikes. The present essay extends both: if evil cannot be fixed by removal, suffering cannot be fixed by declaration. Calling a wound a blessing does not heal it any more than deleting a tempter straightens a will. Something else is required in both cases, and the two essays converge on naming what it is.

One target must be set down precisely at the outset, because the imprecise version of the claim would be indefensible and the precise version is hard enough to sit with as it is. This essay does not deny that suffering united to Christ becomes redemptive. Pope John Paul II's teaching in *Salvifici Doloris* — that human suffering, taken up into Christ's self-gift, acquires a new meaning and becomes a participation in his redemptive suffering — is affirmed here without qualification and is load-bearing for everything that follows. Nor does the essay deny the biblical blessing-sayings: Job's confession, the Beatitude of those who mourn, James's counsel to count trials as joy. All of these stand. What is under examination is a grammar, in the sense the corpus has already used that word: a habit of speech, sincerely meant and rarely examined, that collapses *can become* into *already is* — that hears "suffering can be transfigured" and repeats it back as "this suffering already is your blessing," flattening a conditional truth, which requires grace and the will's free response, into an unconditional one that requires nothing at all.

## I. The Sentence That Comforts and Corrupts

There is a sentence spoken over nearly every hospital bed, funeral reception, and diagnosis, and it is nearly always spoken in love: *this is a blessing in disguise.* Its relatives are legion — *everything happens for a reason, God doesn't give you more than you can handle, this is God's plan for you* — and beneath their different wording they share one grammatical structure. Each takes a thing that might become good and asserts, in the flat indicative, that it already is good, here, now, as given, before the person addressed has done anything with it at all.

The sentence is not spoken by fools. It is spoken by people who love the sufferer and cannot bear to leave them holding nothing. That is precisely why it needs examining rather than dismissing: a comfort this widespread, offered this sincerely, is doing real work in the souls that receive it, and the work is not uniformly good. Something true is being reached for. What arrives in the sentence's mouth is not quite it.

The truth being reached for is real and has a name: suffering can be redemptive. Carried in union with Christ, offered rather than merely endured, it can become — the corpus has said this before and says it again here without retreat — the very material of sanctity. But *can become* is a claim about a trajectory that passes through grace and an act of the will at the crossing point. *Already is* is a claim about a fixed property the thing possesses on its own, independent of grace and of anything the sufferer does with it. The two sentences look interchangeable in casual speech. They are not interchangeable in structure, and the difference between them is the whole subject of this essay. Stated once, plainly, so it can govern everything that follows: suffering is not a blessing merely because it is suffering; it becomes a place of blessing when, through grace and freedom, it is united to Christ in love.

Grace precedes the turning. This distinction must be guarded at once against a misreading the essay's later emphasis on the will would otherwise invite. To say that suffering can become redemptive through the will's turning is not to say that the sufferer transforms suffering by unaided power. In Catholic teaching, grace precedes and enables the free response by which suffering is united to Christ. The turning is genuinely ours — no one performs it for us — but it is not self-generated. God does not wait outside the wound for the sufferer to manufacture a meaning from it; he gives the grace by which the wound can be received, offered, endured, resisted, or transformed in charity. The human response is therefore neither automatic nor autonomous: it is free precisely because grace makes freedom possible. Wherever the argument below speaks of the will's "turning" at the crossing point, this prevenient grace is presupposed, never replaced by the will's own effort.

## II. Evil Is Not a Positive Content

Before suffering can be rightly placed, evil has to be rightly placed, because most suffering — not all, as Section III will insist — is evil's shadow falling across a good world.

The classical doctrine, which the corpus has already leaned on in *Where Is God?* and *Not Gepetto,* is that evil is not a substance. It is not a rival ingredient mixed into creation alongside the good ones, requiring its own supply, its own author, its own positive account. Evil is privation: the absence of a good that ought to be present. Blindness is not a strange kind of seeing; it is sight's absence where sight belongs. A lie is not an alternative fact standing next to the truth; it is truth deformed. Cruelty is not a second kind of love; it is love's failure to occur where it was owed. *Not Gepetto* made the same point about the will directly: the disorder in Adam, in Cain, in David, was not an external force injected into an otherwise straight will. It was the will's own curvature — something missing, not something added.

This is where the tradition's careful grammar of providence must be recalled, because the pious sentence and its critics both tend to forget it. There is a difference between God *causing* an evil, God *positively willing* an evil, God *permitting* an evil, and God *bringing good from* an evil. Catholic faith holds the first two impossible of the God who is goodness itself, the third real, and the fourth the very signature of providence: God permits no evil except to draw from it a greater good, as Augustine says and the Catechism repeats — while refusing, deliberately, to reduce the mystery of evil to a tidy formula. The point for this essay is exact. That God can bring good out of an evil does not make the evil a good; it makes the evil an occasion that a goodness not its own can redeem.

Carry the privation doctrine one step further than the earlier essays carried it, and the conclusion for suffering follows immediately. If evil is privation and much suffering is evil's visible wound, then suffering, in that portion of its extent, is not a positive good waiting to be recognized. It is an absence — of health, of justice, of the peace a rightly ordered world would have afforded — showing itself as pain. Pain itself, however, should not be confused with evil simply because it is unpleasant. In an embodied creature, pain can serve a genuine natural function: it signals injury, protects the organism, and summons the creature toward preservation. The privation lies not in the mere sensation of pain, but in the wound, loss, disorder, or diminished good to which suffering bears witness. In a creation still *in statu viae,* even painful sensation can belong to the ordered functioning of embodied life while the injury it reveals remains a privation. To call it a blessing without further qualification is to call an absence a presence. It is the same structural mistake the tradition has always refused to make about evil itself, now made instead about evil's visible mark.

This changes what gratitude is owed to, and where. One does not thank God for the absence as such — for the missing health, the missing justice, the missing peace. One thanks God, if thanks are owed at all in the moment of the wound, for the ground that continues to sustain the sufferer within the absence, and for the good that grace can still draw from what God did not will. The object of gratitude is not the privation but the sustaining, and the redemption held open from within it.

## III. What Suffering Is Made Of

*The Cross We Think We See* distinguished three sources by which a history becomes a person's own: inheritance — what arrives before any choosing; the world — the field of other free persons' decisions and an embodied creation's limits, received rather than made; and one's own condensation — what the will itself has deposited, one crossing at a time. The distinction was built to sort guilt from constitution. It sorts suffering with equal precision, and sorting it is the next step this essay owes.

Most of what a person suffers was not sent to them as a direct communication. It was inherited — a body predisposed to illness before any choice was made, a family's fracture entered at birth. It arrived by the world — another driver's negligence, an economy's collapse, a stranger's cruelty, none of it addressed to the sufferer in particular and all of it landing on them anyway. Some of it, and only some, the sufferer's own will condensed and now bears as consequence. *Where Is God?* named the same territory from a different angle: God sustains the Now in which each of these kinds of suffering occurs. God is not the author of moral evil, and suffering is not to be presumed a positive divine gift; God permits what he does not positively will, remains present within what he permits, and can bring good from it. The earthquake belongs to a creation still *in statu viae.* The stranger's cruelty belongs to a freedom God does not override. The inherited wound belongs to a lineage God did not write. In none of these cases is the suffering itself a direct dispatch from God to the sufferer, stamped with a lesson and addressed by name — though nothing in them falls outside a providence that can draw good from what it did not will.

This is where the flattened grammar does its quiet damage. *This is a blessing God gave you* relocates the authorship. It makes God not merely the ground that sustains the moment in which the wound occurs, and not merely the providence that can redeem it, but the positive author of the wound's content — the one who selected this cancer, this betrayal, this earthquake, and dispatched it as a gift. The tradition's grammar of providence will not bear that relocation. God holds the Now open for the earthquake and the birth in the same breath, as *The Corridor* showed with the two doors of the hospital; he can bring good from either; but he does not therefore compose the earthquake's geology as a personal message to the family standing in the second doorway.

None of this denies the subtlest category *Where Is God?* also named: suffering that can be experienced within divine correction — real, and ordered toward restoration where it genuinely occurs. But that category must be handled with exactly the caution the Book of Job exists to teach. Job's friends are not condemned for taking suffering seriously; they are condemned for presuming to know what God was doing with it — for reasoning from *he suffers* to *God must be correcting him.* The book dismantles that inference and vindicates the man they had diagnosed. So the essay states plainly what the friends forgot: the existence of suffering does not by itself establish that God is correcting the sufferer. Correction is one thing suffering can be associated with; it is never a conclusion the sufferer or his comforters are entitled to read off the bare fact of the wound. And the flattened grammar is precisely the friends' error at scale — it takes the narrowest kind of suffering, the deliberately measured and directed, and stretches it silently across all the rest, so that the cancer, the betrayal, and the earthquake are all spoken of as if each were a personally addressed lesson. They are not. The sufferer is owed no decoded explanation of his wound; what he is owed, and what the friends withheld, is the faith that God remains faithful within it.

## IV. What Actually Distinguishes Christ's Suffering

If most human suffering is first received rather than chosen — arriving by inheritance, by the world, by consequences the will did not entirely author — then a further distinction becomes visible, and it is the hinge the whole essay turns on: Christ's suffering is not an instance of this pattern. It is its exception, and the exception is instructive precisely because of how it differs.

*The Cross We Think We See* already established the essential point, and this essay only needs to draw out its consequence for suffering as such. Christ does not stand downstream of a fallen inheritance the way every other human person does. He is sinless; his will is not curved; nothing in him needed correcting. What he bears, he bears not because a lineage deposited it in him or because his own will condensed it, but because he freely entered the condition — assumed it from beyond it, in the Incarnation's own direction, rather than accumulating it from within as every other human history accumulates its own. His suffering is not first received and then, perhaps, freely offered in a second act of the will, the way a disciple's suffering must be. It is chosen at the root. There is no first moment of passive reception in Christ's case for a second moment of free orientation to work on, because the very entering was already the free act.

And one thing more must be said, because it sharpens the contrast to a point. Christ did not need suffering in order to become holy. His suffering is not the purification of a defective interior, as ours may be; there was no defect to purify. It is the perfect expression and accomplishment of a love already whole — obedience made visible, self-gift enacted — not a soul being mended in the fire. This yields the cleanest form of the contrast the essay has been building toward: our suffering may become an occasion of sanctification; Christ's suffering is the flawless enactment of a holiness that preceded it. The disciple is sanctified, in part, through what he suffers. Christ transforms what is suffered by taking it into his redemptive self-offering.

This is why oblation — offering — is the exact word for what happens at the Cross and only an aspirational word for what happens in an ordinary hospital bed. An oblation is chosen at its origin. An ordinary person's suffering is not chosen at its origin — the diagnosis arrives, the betrayal arrives, the earthquake arrives, none of it requested. For the disciple, such suffering becomes an offering not by being suffered, and not by the will's unaided decision, but insofar as it is taken up, through grace, into Christ's self-offering. And this taking-up need not always wear the form of a conscious, articulated act: the grace that unites a life to Christ can reach the infant, the unconscious, the one whose mind cannot frame an offering, where deliberate assent cannot go. This is the same horizon in which the Church locates baptismal incorporation and the broader category of grace's unconscious cooperation: the person can be genuinely united to Christ even when the faculties that ordinarily articulate an offering are not yet formed or are no longer able to act. Where the disciple can respond, the response is genuinely asked of him; where he cannot, grace is not thereby barred. *Take up your cross,* the earlier essay showed, names the response when it is possible — reception, not acquisition; orientation, not authorship — and Christ's own cross required no such second act, because his was oblation from the first instant. Ours becomes oblation because his already was.

The flattened grammar erases this asymmetry without noticing it has done so. It speaks of the cancer as "your cross" already meaning, already blessing, already gift — as though the sufferer's history had arrived the way Christ's did, chosen at the root, rather than the way every other human history arrives, received before it is ever offered. It quietly asks the sufferer to have already performed, unaided, the turning that only grace makes possible, at the very moment the first, unchosen blow has only just landed. That is not encouragement. It is a category error dressed as comfort, and it can crush precisely where it means to console.

## V. Blessed Are Those Who — Not "Suffering Is Blessed"

Scripture does bless the sufferer. And Catholic faith holds that suffering itself, once united to Christ, takes on a new and even redemptive meaning — this is the whole burden of *Salvifici Doloris,* and the essay does not retract a word of it. What Scripture does not do, on inspection, is bless suffering merely for being suffering: as a state good in its raw content, apart from any union with Christ, requiring nothing of grace or of the person who bears it.

*Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted* (Matt 5:4). The blessing falls on the mourner, in view of a promised comfort not yet arrived — not on the mourning itself, considered as a state to be sought or savored. Reverse the order and the sentence breaks: no one reads it as *mourning is blessed, therefore comfort is beside the point.* The comfort is not decoration on an already-complete blessing. It is what the blessing is for.

The horizon is therefore eschatological. The mourner is called blessed not because mourning has become good in itself, but because the promised comfort belongs to the coming fulfillment of God's Kingdom. The Beatitude places the present wound beneath a future that has already been announced but not yet consummated: the mourner stands in the Now, while the comfort toward which the blessing points remains ahead.

James counsels believers to *count it all joy* when they meet various trials (James 1:2), and the verse is regularly lifted to mean that the trial itself is joyful. The verse says something more exact: the trials are to be counted with joy because of what they produce — endurance, and endurance's full effect, a maturity lacking nothing. The joy is aimed at the trial's fruit, reached through faith's engagement with it, not at the trial's raw content. Job's confession in the epigraph — *shall we accept good from God, and not accept adversity* — is not a claim that the boils and the bereavement were themselves good. It is Job's refusal to make his fidelity conditional on which of the two he is currently handed. The sentence blesses the constancy, not the calamity.

Once the grammar is read this carefully, the pattern is uniform across every text the flattened sentence borrows its authority from: the blessing is pronounced on the person's posture toward the suffering — constancy, endurance, faith engaged rather than withheld — and on the promised end the posture is ordered toward, never on the suffering's bare content, considered apart from grace and apart from any posture at all. Scripture does not bless an undergone thing merely for being undergone. It blesses the one who undergoes it in faith, and it reveals how suffering, when united to Christ, can bear fruit in grace — while naming, honestly, that the fruit is not automatic.

## VI. The Danger Named Precisely

Section VII of *The Cross We Think We See* named the pastoral cost of the defeat-grammar. The blessing-grammar examined here is not the same error, but it is close kin, and its cost deserves its own accounting rather than an inherited one.

It can silence resistance to injustice. If the abuse already is the blessing, already is the gift, there is nothing left to name as wrong and nothing left to resist. *The Cross We Think We See* drew the line that matters here too: what the world unjustly imposes is to be named as injustice and, where faithfulness requires, resisted — not baptized in advance as God's chosen gift to the one suffering it. A grammar that forecloses that naming does not console the victim. It disarms them, and it does so in God's name.

It can produce despair when the reason does not appear. A person told that a tragedy already carries a hidden blessing will, eventually and reasonably, go looking for the blessing. When years pass and none discloses itself — because, as Section III showed, most suffering is not a personally addressed lesson at all — the failure to locate a meaning that was never promised in that form reads back as either God's silence or the sufferer's own spiritual failure to see what should have been visible. Neither conclusion is true, and both are avoidable, because the premise that generated the search was false from the start. This is Job's friends again, turned inward: the sufferer made to play both the accused and the accuser, demanding of himself the decoded explanation he was never owed.

It can misdirect gratitude toward the wound instead of toward the ground that sustains the wounded. Section II already named the structural version of this error: thanking God for the absence rather than for the sustaining that continues within it and the good grace can draw from it. Practiced as spirituality rather than argument, the misdirection trains a person to love the pain itself as a kind of spiritual credential, which is very close to the defeat-grammar's own error of finding suffering meritorious simply because it is suffering — the exact inference *The Cross We Think We See* refused: *this hurts, therefore this is sanctifying.*

And it can erase the response grace was enabling by performing it in advance, on the sufferer's behalf, in the very sentence meant to comfort them. Section IV showed that ordinary suffering becomes oblation only as it is taken up, through grace, into Christ's offering — the very thing *take up your cross* asks for where it can be asked, and does not presume. To announce, at the moment of the wound, that the blessing has already occurred is to narrate that taking-up as already accomplished before grace has drawn it out and before the person has had any chance to consent to it. It offers a counterfeit of the very transformation it claims to describe, and a counterfeit accepted is a real transformation postponed.

## VII. What Can Be Said Instead

None of this leaves the comforter with nothing to say. It leaves them with something more exact, and — this needs stating plainly, because precision can sound like coldness — something considerably more respectful of the person standing in front of them.

Not *this is a blessing,* but *this can become the place where grace meets you.* Not *God gave you this,* but *God has not withdrawn from you in this, and the ground that holds you now is the same ground that can draw good from what it did not will.* Not *there is a reason for this,* in the sense of a lesson already encoded and waiting to be decoded, but *there is a way through this, and it runs through grace, toward the one who entered suffering without being sent it, and asks of you now only that you let him take it up with you.* And sometimes the faithful response is not to endure the suffering a moment longer than one must, but to seek its remedy: to escape an unjust situation, report an abuser, accept treatment, ask for help, resist what can and should be resisted. The Church does not preach the passive glorification of pain. *Salvifici Doloris* itself begins not with an exhortation to endure but with the Church's duty to draw near to the suffering person on the road, as the Samaritan did — and drawing near often means acting to relieve, not only counseling to bear.

Seen this way, the pious cliché and Saramago's famous objection turn out to meet at an unexpected point. Saramago asked why God, able to end evil, did not simply remove the devil and be done with it — and *Not Gepetto* answered that the question assumes the wrong architecture of God, a carver above the stage rather than the ground beneath it. But the cliché makes a mirror-image of the same mistake. Saramago reasons: if God permits suffering and does not remove it, God must have positively chosen its painful content, and stands condemned for the choice. The comforter reasons: if God can bring good from suffering, God must have positively sent it, and is to be thanked for the gift. One says *God chose this evil;* the other says *God gave you this blessing.* Both have already granted the same premise — that the suffering itself must have been positively authored as a divine instrument. The Christian claim refuses the premise they share: God can permit what he does not positively will, remain present within what he permits, and bring grace from it without ever making the evil itself good.

The model for the faithful response is not a doctrine but a moment, and the corpus has already supplied it. *Where Is God?* read Christ's cry from the Cross — *My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?* — as the opening line of Psalm 22, prayed in full knowledge of where that Psalm turns: from desolation toward a God who has not hidden his face. Christ does not treat his own abandonment as a blessing in the flattened sense. He does not narrate the cry as already resolved, already gift, already meaning, at the very moment he is uttering it in the fullness of its desolation. He prays it — the whole arc, forsakenness and trust in one breath, addressed rather than dissolved. And the Father is not absent from Calvary, nor is his will a rival to the Son's: the Son freely offers himself, the Father permits the Passion within that freely embraced mission, and — as *Not Gepetto* insisted — it is one undivided will of self-giving, not a hand above the stage spending a victim below it. That is the orientation this essay has been describing throughout: not suffering declared holy at the outset, but suffering brought, as it actually is, to the one who sustains the Now in which it occurs and does not leave it there.

This is where *Not Gepetto's* argument closes the circle. God does not eliminate the Adversary by force, that essay showed, because force cannot heal a disordered will; the healing has to run through the will's own graced turning at the crossing point, addressed rather than coerced. The same architecture governs suffering — with one guard against being misheard. None of this means God is absent from suffering or never acts within it: providence remains present, answers prayer, heals, raises up help, and works through countless secondary causes. What providence does not ordinarily do is abolish creaturely freedom or make every evil vanish at the instant it occurs; and it does not, either, pronounce the wound already blessed so as to require nothing further of grace or of the one who bears it. Elimination by force and elimination by decree would be the same shortcut in different disguises: one erasing an enemy without touching the will that needs turning, the other erasing a wound's meaning without waiting for the grace that draws the will's own assent. What is offered instead, in both essays, is harder and more honest — a ground that does not withdraw, and a crossing point at which the sufferer, like the tempted, is addressed rather than overridden.

<br>

## VIII. Coda

Suffering is not the wage of holiness. It is not a reward smuggled into a painful wrapping, and it is not, simply by occurring, a communication from God requiring only to be correctly decoded. Most of it is the shape a wounded and unfinished world takes where a good that ought to be present is not yet present — inherited, imposed by the world, or, in its narrower portion, condensed by the sufferer's own past choices — sustained at every Now by a God who holds the moment open, permits what he does not positively will, and authors none of the evil in what he permits.

And yet the tradition's older claim stands, undiminished by anything argued here: suffering can be taken up. It does not become salvific because the sufferer succeeds in wringing a meaning from it. It becomes salvific when, by grace, the sufferer is united to Christ — whose suffering love has already broken suffering's power to have the last word. The turning is genuinely the sufferer's own, asked of him and not performed for him; but the grace that makes the turning possible, and the union it opens onto, are Christ's gift, not the will's achievement. That is not suffering declared holy in advance. It is suffering, freely and gracefully carried, becoming what it was not yet: not the gift itself, but the place where the gift is received.

And it is not the final word. Christianity does not teach the sufferer to make peace with suffering as the last condition of existence. Its center of gravity is neither *suffering is meaningless* nor *suffering is good,* but something only the Resurrection makes sayable: suffering is evil insofar as it wounds a good; Christ enters it, transforms its meaning from within by love, and finally defeats it — so that the wound carried now, in union with him, is carried toward a morning in which every wound is undone.

*My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness.* — 2 Corinthians 12:9
