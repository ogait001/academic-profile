## A Note on Method

This essay is the third panel of a triptych. The first, [*The Cross We Think We See: On the History We Carry and the Defeat We Read Into the Cross*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophical-theology/theology-of-the-cross/christian-theology/2026/09/14/the-cross-we-think-we-see-en.html), diagnosed a grammar — the habit of reading Good Friday as a defeat that Easter later corrects — and set against it the grammar of the Cross as victory revealed rather than repaired. The second, [*The Theology of Suffering: Why Suffering Is Not the Gift, and What the Cross Actually Gives*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophical-theology/theology/philosophy-of-christianity/2026/09/14/the-theology-of-suffering-en.html),
drew the line the tradition draws and pastoral habit forgets: Scripture blesses the mourner, never the mourning; pain is a privation, a wounded good, and grace does not sanctify the wound but enters it. This third panel does not rebuild those arguments. It supplies the witness the first two required and could not themselves embody: a person in whom the wound is mourned as pure wound and the plan is held in total assent, at the same hour, in the same flesh.

As in the prior work, the framework — the lemniscate, the crossing point, condensation, reception — is offered as a philosophical instrument, not demonstrated from first principles, and the Scriptural engagements are philosophical readings alongside the tradition rather than exegesis within it. One attribution must be made exact at the outset, because the essay leans on it and honesty requires it. Scripture nowhere places the words *flesh of my flesh* on the lips of Mary. The phrase is Adam's, spoken over Eve — *bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh* (Genesis 2:23). Its transfer to the Mother at the foot of the Cross is not biblical but cinematic: it is Mel Gibson's interpolation in *The Passion of the Christ,* the cry the film gives her as she reaches the fallen body. The essay treats that cry as a literary witness not as a text of Scripture, and not as a claim about what Mary said. What it is instead is a dramatist's confession that no maternal formula runs deep enough for the thing before her, so that the only words available had to be borrowed from the deepest bond Scripture already names. That borrowing is itself the argument, and the essay will come to it in its place.

## I. Two Mothers at Two Walls

Rachel enters the triptych here, at the wall. *A voice was heard in Ramah, lamentation and bitter weeping, Rachel weeping for her children, and she refused to be comforted, because they were no more* (Jeremiah 31:15). The text itself insists on the refusal: not that she could not be comforted, as though her grief were a failure of faith to be corrected, but that she refused — an act of the will, a stubborn refusal that the text does not treat as faithlessness, that would not let anyone hand her a tidy explanation for dead children. And the text insists on what God does not do in reply. He does not argue. He does not correct her. He lets the weeping stand and echo forward six hundred years, until Matthew reaches back and quotes it again over the slaughtered children of Bethlehem, at the very hour of Christ's birth.

Set beside Rachel a second mother at a second wall, and the whole of this panel opens in the distance between them. Rachel weeps without the plan. She has no Easter in view, no word that the children will be restored, nothing but the fact that they are no more; her refusal is the refusal of a grief that has been given no reason and rightly declines the counterfeit ones. Mary weeps with the plan entire. She of all mourners in Scripture has carried the promises whose fulfillment now passes through this suffering. She has carried the annunciation in her since Gabriel; she has kept the things said of this child and pondered them in her heart; she stands beneath the Cross as one who had assented, in advance and without reservation, to God's saving will, even as the form that will now takes wounds her beyond anything assent could make painless. And she weeps anyway.

This is the figure the first panel needed and could not produce from its own materials. The grammar of victory, argued in the abstract, is always vulnerable to a single objection: if Good Friday is already the triumph, if Easter only reveals what the Cross accomplished, then why grieve at all — why not stand at Golgotha in serene foreknowledge, waiting out the appearance of loss until the disclosure arrives? Mary is the answer that no argument could supply. She holds the victory-grammar in its most complete form — she does not misread the Cross as a defeat awaiting reversal; she is the one human being who most fully knows it is not — and her knowing does not exempt her from the grief. The two are not in tension in her. They are simply both true, in one person, at one Now. Foreknowledge does not cancel mourning. The mother who most completely believes is the mother who most completely weeps.

## II. Flesh of My Flesh

Now the borrowed cry can be given its weight. When the film reaches for words at the moment the body is lowered, it finds no maternal formula equal to the loss and takes Adam's — *flesh of my flesh* — the words a husband speaks over the wife drawn from his own side. The transfer is, on its face, a category crossing: the spousal bond is not the maternal one, and the dramatist is not confused about which is which. The crossing is deliberate, and it is exact. It reaches for the deepest bond of shared substance Scripture names, because the thing before the Mother is a bond of shared substance deeper than any the language had yet been built to carry.

The topology has an account of why the words had to be borrowed. Every other case the corpus has described runs one direction: a person is a confluence, a history received and condensed at a crossing, the downstream gathering of an inheritance never authored by the one who bears it. The essay [*Whose Condensation Am I?*](https://oscargaitan.org/phenomenology/christian-theology/philosophical-anthropology/2026/08/07/whose-condensation-am-i-en.html) pressed this to its root, what you took in the womb, of your mother's fear or her calm, entered your constitution as reception before it was ever choice. The ordinary mother gives her child a share in her flesh, and the child becomes, in part, a condensation of her: the direction runs from her into him. Mary's case inverts even this. The one who took flesh from her is not a downstream confluence of her history. He is, as the second essay of the divine-knowledge line named him, the Ground who became the grounded — the eternal Son who assumed human nature and entered human history from beyond it. The flesh is genuinely hers; the Incarnation is not a costume but the true assumption of human nature from her. And yet the one who wears it is the very ground through whom she herself is held open at every crossing.

So the cry *flesh of my flesh,* misattributed as it is, says something true that no correctly assigned phrase could. The maternal formula would have said only *he came from me.* The spousal formula, wrenched out of its place, says *he is my own substance, standing over against me, and now dead in my arms.* It names a mother holding, as a corpse, the flesh that is hers — and holding, in that same flesh, the God from whom her own flesh continuously receives its being. There is a distinctive privation here: not absence, which is Rachel's — her children are no more, gone past the reach of holding — but presence as death, the substance still here, in the arms, and lifeless. Rachel's wall is an emptiness. Mary's is a weight.

## III. The Disciple Who Wept

The tradition gives Mary a title that this essay must take with full seriousness, because everything pastoral in it follows from the title: she is the first disciple. Before any apostle was called, she had already given the answer discipleship consists in — *be it done to me according to your word.* Her fiat is the archetype of every subsequent yes, the assent from which the Church learns what assent is. She is not a bystander at the Cross whose grief we may indulge as natural weakness. She is the model believer, and what she does at the foot of the Cross shows that faith does not require the suppression of grief.

And what faith does there is weep. This is the whole pastoral hinge of the triptych, and it can now be stated without hedge. If the most complete faith in Scripture expressed itself, at the site of the wound, as mourning — real mourning, not a composed and knowing patience wearing grief's outward form — then mourning is not the residue of insufficient faith. It is not the part of Mary that had not yet caught up to her fiat. Her tears and her assent are not sequential, the grief to be gotten through before the faith resumes; they are simultaneous, and neither diminishes the other. She does not weep because she has momentarily forgotten the Resurrection. She weeps because her Son is being tortured to death in front of her, and that is a real horror, and knowing it will be undone does not make it less horrible while it is happening. The undoing is real. The horror is real. Faith is what holds both without collapsing either into the other.

This is the exact refutation, made flesh, of the pastoral error the second panel named. That panel argued against forced gratitude — against the habit of requiring the sufferer to thank God for the tragedy itself, to locate the blessing in the wound. Mary is the argument's proof. The one disciple with the most reason to see past the wound to the glory beyond it did not thank the Father for the nails. She was never asked to. Her assent was to the plan, to the Father's will, to the cup her Son received — never to the wound as a good in itself, never to the suffering as a thing to be celebrated rather than borne. If the Mother of God, believing perfectly, still mourned the wound as a wound, then no pastor may demand of a grieving mother the gratitude for the tragedy that Mary herself was never required to give. To ask it is to ask for a piety that stands above the first disciple.

## IV. What Grace Does, and What It Does Not

It remains only to say precisely what the mourning is joined to, so that the refusal of false comfort is not mistaken for the refusal of comfort altogether — the error that would make this a counsel of despair rather than of honesty. The prior panels have already established the distinction, and it need only be carried here, not rebuilt. Suffering in isolation is a privation, a wound, a dead end; it does not become good by being suffered, and the inference from *this hurts* to *this is therefore holy* is the false move the whole triptych exists to refuse. Grace does not make the wound good. It does not erase the pain or turn the privation itself into a good. What it does is enter the site of the pain and take the sufferer up into a communion that the pain could not, of itself, have produced — so that the isolated wall becomes a place of union with the One who also wept, and the suffering, without ceasing to be suffering, becomes capable of bearing a good it does not contain on its own.

Two things guard this from the optimism it must not become. The first is that the good is received, not manufactured: the sufferer does not construct a silver lining, does not reason her way to a hidden blessing; she is joined, by grace and by her own participation, to a self-gift already accomplished, and the joining is the gift. The second is that the wound is not erased in the joining. When Christ rose, He kept His wounds; the risen body is a wounded body, glorified without being falsified. Grace does not rewind the history and return a mother her living child as though the death had not occurred. It does not tell Rachel her children were never lost. It enters Ramah — enters the very site of the bitter weeping — so that the one who weeps is no longer weeping in absolute and isolated abandonment. Mary's tears are not answered by being dried before their time. They are answered by a God who, on the Cross she stands beneath, has entered human suffering from within.

<br>

## V. Between the Two Women

The pastoral teaching the whole triptych was moving toward can now be set down plainly, because the two mothers have made it sayable. Between Rachel and Mary stands every sufferer the Church is ever called to sit beside, and the two women mark the full permission the Church must extend and the pastoral habit keeps withdrawing.

From Rachel comes the permission to refuse the false consolation that would make the tragedy acceptable by renaming it good. The sufferer is allowed the stubborn refusal that will not accept a tidy explanation for an untidy loss, that names the tragedy as genuinely wrong and declines the cheap comfort that would rename it a gift. This is not weak faith. Scripture records it without reprimand — the text does not treat Rachel's refusal as faithlessness — and lets it echo through generations. From Mary comes the second half, and it is the half the pastoral habit most needs to recover: that this refusal is not the property of unbelief. The grief that will not pretend the wound is a blessing is the same grief the first and most perfect disciple wept beneath the Cross, with her fiat wholly intact. One may believe everything the Creed confesses, assent without reservation to the entire economy of salvation, and still stand at the wall and weep — and be, in that weeping, not a failed believer but the truest kind.

So the task at the graveside and the hospital bed and the front of the grieving room is not to explain the suffering into submission, nor to find the blessing before the family has finished naming the loss. It is to do what God does with Rachel — to let the weeping stand, unrushed and uncorrected — and to do what God does with Mary, whose Son wept at a tomb before He opened it and cried out the whole distance of abandonment from the Cross before He crossed it: to enter the grief rather than resolve it, to stay rather than to fix. The comfort Christ promises the mourner was never an argument. It is a presence — His own. Not *here is why.* But *here I am.*

Rachel refused, and God let her refusal stand until the undoing itself should answer it. Mary assented, and still wept, and God did not ask her to stop. Between the emptiness that is one mother's wall and the weight that is the other's, every grief the Church will ever hold has its place — permitted the refusal, and permitted the faith, and required to surrender neither for the sake of the other.

*Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted.* Not *blessed is the mourning.* *Blessed are those who mourn.*
