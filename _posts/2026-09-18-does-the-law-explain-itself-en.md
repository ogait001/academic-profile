## Note on Sources and Method

This essay is a companion to [*What Sustains the Now?*](https://oscargaitan.org/metaphysics/philosophical-theology/philosophy-of-god/2026/09/18/what-sustains-the-now-en.html) and presupposes the topology developed across the temporal sequence, which it recalls rather than re-derives. It presupposes, too, the topology's real distinction between essence and existence: in derivative being, actuality is received, not identical with the thing's own underived act of being. Its philosophical witnesses — Aquinas, Leibniz, Hume, Russell, Wittgenstein, de Lubac, Rahner — are parallel witnesses to aspects of the argument and not authorities from which it is drawn; the argument stands or falls on its own structural coherence, not on their names. Nothing here asserts a claim about physics that physics itself makes: no cosmic "before," no first event, no mechanism of sustaining. What is defended is a proposition about ontological dependence — the derivative standing of the physical order — and, as marked in the text, the weaker and surer form of that proposition rather than the stronger form it does not earn.

<br>

## Abstract

Every essay of this topology until now has asked its question from inside the one who asks it: the Now he stands at, the past he carries, the future he faces, the continuance he does not author. *What Sustains the Now?* ended on the lived datum beneath the whole abstraction — no one authors his own next moment — and named the dependence that datum reports: derivative actuality has its being-actual from the Non-Derivative and not from itself. This essay turns the same question outward, away from the self and toward the physical order the unbeliever trusts precisely because it seems to need no one to trust it: the laws, the constants, the standing regularity of what is. The agnostic's honest position is not that the order was caused, nor that it began, nor that it made itself, but that it is simply there — the terminus at which the asking of reasons rightly stops, brute and self-sufficient, requiring no Non-Derivative because nothing does. This essay argues that the position rests on a single equivocation and one mislocation. The equivocation is in the word stops: physics delivers only the exhaustion of method, never the exhaustion of reasons, and "the order is just there" reads the first as the second. The mislocation is in the word necessary: the naturalist who fortifies the order by calling its laws necessary either means a necessity too weak to remove contingency, or a necessity so strong that, followed honestly, it is the Non-Derivative arriving under a colder name. What is established here is the weaker and surer claim — that nothing within the figure grounds the figure, and that to deny the Ground is not to strike a being from the inventory but to withdraw the intelligibility of there being one. What is not established, and is marked as not established, is that the Ground so reached is the God the Church confesses; that is a further and humbler question, left standing.

<br>

## I. The Turn Outward

A topology that has spent its essays inside the one who asks its questions owes, at some point, the question asked from outside him. *What Sustains the Now?* closed on the most interior datum the corpus has: I do not author my own continuance; the next instant arrives, and I find myself already within it, given to be, without having produced the being by which I am there to notice. That essay named what the datum reports — constitutive dependence, the derivative's having of its being-actual from the Non-Derivative — and was careful about what the naming does not license. It does not license a temporal story. There is no first instant God authored and then withdrew from, no succession of grounding acts strung along a divine timeline; the succession is on the creature's side, the grounding on neither. To sustain is not to conserve a thing against decay but to constitute it, such that were the dependence to cease nothing would fall, because nothing was standing on its own to begin with to start falling.

The unbeliever can grant every word of that as the description of a feeling and refuse it as metaphysics. He will say: you have described the arrival of your own next moment and called the description dependence, but the description fits a world with no Non-Derivative in it just as well. The moment arrives; the laws carry it; that is all. And here the question must turn outward, because the unbeliever has just named the thing he trusts in the Ground's place. Not himself — he agrees he does not author his continuance — but the order: the laws, the fields, the constants, the standing regularity that carried the moment to him. This essay asks of that order exactly what the parent essay asked of the self. Does it author its own holding?

And it asks under the same discipline. No argument here will place the order's ground before it in time, or beneath it as a first event, or behind it as a cause that acted once and let go. The whole labor of the corpus was to move succession off the Ground's side; a companion that smuggled it back in through physics would betray the essay it means to extend. The dependence in question, if there is one, is constitutive and not successive — the order's having of its being-actual, not the order's having once been switched on.

<br>

## II. The Objection at Full Strength

Picture the scene the skeptic offers, because it is a good one and deserves its best form. A monk sits in the lotus position, doing nothing, wanting nothing, reaching for nothing. At eight o'clock he is there. At eight o'clock and one second he is there. The skeptic watches and says: look at him — he does nothing, and the next instant comes anyway. No one authored it. I could bet a great deal of money that he will be alive tomorrow, and I would probably win, and if I won it would be because life simply continued, as it tends to. Perhaps it is luck. Perhaps it is law. Either way the laws of physics carried him from this second to the next, and I need nothing further than the laws. There is no hand under the world.

Grant it its full strength; the softened versions are not worth answering. The skeptic is not committing the errors the cruder believer is forever attributing to him. He does not say the order caused itself — self-causation is incoherent and he knows it. He does not say it sprang from nothing, or that it began, or that there was a void before it into which gravity was later introduced; he makes no claim about a before at all, and is wise not to, since the physics does not obviously supply one and the metaphysics does not need one. His claim is cleaner and harder than any of these. It is that the order neither caused itself nor required causing — that being-actual, at the level of the whole physical figure, is a brute fact, the floor beneath which there is no further fact to seek. There is no Non-Derivative, on this view, not because the search for one failed, but because the search was never owed. The order is just there. That is the strongest thing the unbeliever can say, and this essay will not pretend he says anything weaker.

<br>

## III. The Two Stops

Everything in that position turns on one word, and the word is doing two jobs — as past was found to be doing three and future two, and the whole contribution, there as here, is the unbundling. The word is stops. To say the asking of reasons stops at the order can mean one of two things, and they are not the same thing.

It can mean that inquiry stops: that here method is exhausted, that beneath this law there is no deeper law we can presently reach, that the physicist sets down his instruments because they have found nothing further to grip. Call this the epistemic stop. It is a fact about us — about the reach of a science at a time, about where the questions run out of purchase.

Or it can mean that reasons stop: that beneath this there is nothing deeper to be had, that the order's being-actual is from itself and answers to nothing, that the question why this rather than not? has not merely gone unanswered but gone void — ceased to be a question, met the kind of thing of which it may not be asked. Call this the ontological stop. It is not a fact about us. It is a claim about the order, and the strongest one available: that the order is the sort of thing whose actuality needs, and admits, no ground.

Physics delivers only the first. This is not a limitation to be lamented; it is the form of the discipline's every success. Its characteristic motion is to reach past each regularity for what stands under it: the fall of the apple and the swing of the moon gathered under one law, that law folded later into the curvature of a geometry, the scattered forces drawn toward a single account, every constant that once looked simply given made the target of the question why this value and not another? To do physics is to hold each regularity as not yet explained and to reach, past it, for what explains it — and where the reaching runs out, to mark the fact unexplained, not to pronounce it unexplainable. A physicist may meet a law he cannot derive and set down his instruments there; what he cannot do, and remain a physicist in doing it, is pronounce the law its own reason, requiring no derivation — for that is not the bottom of physics but a claim about the bottom of reasons, and physics reaches no such bottom. So when the naturalist reaches the deepest law he has and calls it not merely the deepest he has reached but the deepest there is — not an epistemic stop but an ontological one — he performs at the last step the very move he forbids at every step before it. He upgrades a stopping-point that is ours into a stopping-point that is the order's own. And nothing in the order licenses the upgrade. A regularity we cannot yet derive is not, for that reason, a regularity that derives from nothing. The exhaustion of our reaching is not the self-sufficiency of the thing reached.

<br>

## IV. The Whole and Its Members

The objection has a reply, and it must be given its due and not waved off, because it is the reply a serious naturalist makes rather than the one a careless one stumbles into. Intelligibility, he will say, is local and not global. The demand for a reason is a rule that operates within the order, binding one regularity to another; there is no warrant for turning the rule upon the order as a whole. To do so is a category mistake of the kind Hume named: explain each member of a collection and you have explained the collection, and there is no further item — the whole — standing over and above its members and awaiting a reason of its own. The universe, Russell said to Copleston, is simply there, and that is all. And physics, the naturalist may add, has already seated bruteness at its table: the individual quantum outcome is decided by nothing, a brute event at the very bottom of the order — so a brute order is no stranger than the brute events the order already contains.

Meet the reply on its own ground; it yields on its own ground. The Humean move is sound for a collection whose members differ in the respect the explanation tracks — there it does dissolve the pseudo-question of why these members rather than others, and that pseudo-question is not the one being asked. But here the members do not differ in the respect that matters. Every one of them wears the identical mark: each carries a structure it does not confer upon itself, each is derivative in precisely the sense the governing question named. And a sum of things each of which is derivative is not thereby a thing that is non-derivative. So the question refused of the whole is not a new question about a spurious extra object floating above the members. It is the old question, asked once of all of them at once — the same question the naturalist answered willingly of each of them in turn, now declined for no reason except that answering it would reach past the figure.

And the quantum case does not touch the claim, for the reason the corpus already has words for. An outcome undetermined as to which is still an actuality undetermined as to nothing in its being-actual. Indeterminacy answers the ordinal question — which of the possible becomes real — and is silent on the actuality question, whether any of it is real at all; and the corpus has kept those two apart since it distinguished the two jobs of the word now. What is undetermined at the bottom of physics is the filling, not the form: which outcome arrives is open, that an outcome is actual rather than not is not thereby explained by its being open. Randomness describes how a possibility is selected. It does not confer being on what is selected, and a brute event, in the only sense physics has established, is a brute selection and not a self-existent actuality. The naturalist has found undetermined filling arriving as actuality. He has not found actuality arriving from itself.

<br>

## V. The Last Fortress: Necessity

There remains one reply stronger than the others, and an essay that stepped around it rather than through it would deserve the reader's suspicion. Everything above turned on the claim that each member of the order wears the mark of the derivative — that each carries a structure it does not confer upon itself. The naturalist's last fortress is to deny exactly this of the fundamental laws. They are not, he says, one way among many. They are necessary. They could not have been otherwise. And a thing that could not have been otherwise bears no mark of contingency, needs no ground beyond itself, is non-derivative by the very definition the essay has been using. If the deepest laws are necessary, the order has its Non-Derivative, and it is the laws, and God is a fifth wheel.

The fortress falls to a single distinction, and the distinction is between two necessities the one word hides. There is the necessity a law has within a world — call it nomological necessity: given these laws, the outcomes follow; the law holds across every situation the law governs. And there is the necessity a thing has in itself — metaphysical necessity: that it could not not-be, that its non-existence is not merely against the rules but impossible, that in it what-it-is and that-it-is are not two things one of which might have lacked the other. The naturalist needs the second; physics entitles him only to the first.

For the first necessity is trivial in exactly the way that defeats him. To say the fundamental laws hold necessarily, in the nomological sense, is to say the laws are the laws — that reality runs by them where they run. This removes no contingency at all; it merely restates the regularity whose ground was in question. The law's holding-of-itself was never in doubt as a description; what was in doubt was why there is a reality in which it holds, and repeating that it holds does not answer it. And the fundamental laws are not necessary in the second sense, the sense that would answer it. The naturalist may wager that some final theory will show the constants could not have differed; but a theory that derived the constants from a deeper structure would only move the question to that structure, of which the same two questions may be asked — is its holding necessary in the trivial sense, or in the sense that removes contingency? — and the regress does not terminate inside physics, because every terminus physics can reach is one more describable structure.

Now grant the naturalist, for the argument's sake, the thing he cannot get from physics: suppose there is, at the bottom, something metaphysically necessary — a reality whose non-existence is impossible, in which essence and existence do not come apart, which is actual not by receiving actuality but by being it. He has not, in reaching this, refuted the Non-Derivative. He has deduced it. For that — a reality whose very nature is to be, which does not have existence but is existence, from which the contingent receives what it does not own — is not the naturalist's alternative to the Ground the tradition names. It is the Ground the tradition names, described from the far side and reached by the colder road. Aquinas reached it as ipsum esse subsistens, being itself subsisting; this essay reaches it as the Non-Derivative that does not receive its own; the necessitarian reaches it as that-which-could-not-not-be. The three descriptions converge on one metaphysical role.

And a law cannot be that thing, which is the mislocation the fortress commits. A law is a structure: articulable, composite, abstractable from what instantiates it, statable in more than one form, one member of a space of describable alternatives. Whatever is structured has parts it does not account for merely by being their arrangement, and possibilities it does not close off merely by being one of them; it is the kind of thing that is had, exhibited, instantiated — not the kind of thing that simply is, underivatively, with nothing in it left over to explain. Necessity of the deep kind, if it is anywhere, is not in the figure's structure but in what grounds structure at all, because structure is precisely what wears its alternatives on its face. So the necessitarian who lodges his necessity in the laws has put it in the one place it cannot live. Chase the necessity honestly and it migrates out of the figure — out of the laws, out of the constants, out of the sum of them called the universe — and comes to rest where the essay said the ground must be: in no element of the figure, but in that upon which every element depends. The last fortress, held to the end, is not a refuge from the Non-Derivative. It is another name for it.

<br>

## VI. What the Denial Denies

It is worth saying plainly what is and is not denied when the unbeliever denies the Ground, because the corpus's earlier way of putting it — that in denying Him everything else is denied — is true only when it is made exact, and misleading in the mouth it is usually put into.

It does not mean the unbeliever believes nothing exists. He believes, as firmly as anyone, in stars and particles, in gravity and law and the monk's next breath. Denying the Non-Derivative removes not one item from that inventory. The stars remain; the laws remain; the next instant still arrives on schedule. If the denial subtracted a thing, it would be easy to refute by pointing at the thing. It subtracts no thing — and that is exactly why it is more serious than the subtraction of a thing.

What it denies is not an item in the inventory but the intelligibility of there being an inventory. Physics is the practice of treating being-actual as intelligible — of assuming, at every step, that there is a reason why this regularity rather than another, and that the reason can be sought and sometimes found. The naturalist who grounds the whole order in brute fact has said that at the foundation this assumption fails: that there, and there alone, being-actual has no reason and admits of none. He has not stopped believing in the things. He has stopped believing that their being-there is, at the root, the kind of thing that has a reason. And he is entitled to a version of this. The demand for a reason, he may say, operates within the physical order, binding one regularity to another; and he restricts it there by right. Grant him the restriction. What is not thereby shown is why the boundary of the physical order should also be the boundary of intelligibility — why a demand kept in force across every relation within the figure may be declared inapplicable to the figure's own being-actual. If the answer is that explanation is, by definition, intra-physical, then a boundary of method has been fixed, not a terminus of reasons discovered: the order's actuality is exempted from the question, but exempted by the definition of the method and not by anything in the order that marks its own being-actual as the one regularity of which the question may not be asked. He has founded the intelligible on an unintelligibility, placed the ground of everything at the single point the reason-demand is not permitted to reach, and named the dark a floor.

This is the rigorous form of the old claim. To deny the Non-Derivative is not to deny that things are; it is to deny that their being is, finally, intelligible. And that denial is not neutral ground held quietly in reserve. It is the withdrawal of the very assumption under which the unbeliever's own science was ever the search for reasons — kept in force across the whole figure and suspended only at its ground, and suspended there not because the order discloses a floor but because the reason-demand has been defined to halt at the order's edge: a boundary of method and not a terminus of reasons, performing at the last the one move forbidden at every step before it.

<br>

## VII. Not an Item in the Figure

The agnostic has an honest answer to all of this, and it should be met rather than out-argued, because it is not an argument so much as a report: I look, he says, and I do not find Him. I find fields and forces and law; I do not find, among them, the one you call the Ground. Where is He?

The answer is the guard the corpus posted long ago, turned now toward the one who asks. You are not meant to find Him among them. The Non-Derivative is not an item in the figure — not the first of the forces, not the deepest of the laws, not a hidden member of the inventory catalogued beside the rest. If He were, He would be derivative in the very sense they are: one more thing that is, one way among the ways it could have been, a structure with alternatives on its face, owing its being-actual to whatever grounds the figure. A god you could find among the things would be a god who needed grounding along with them, and so would not be the Ground. The question the essay has been asking is not where, among the things that exist, God is. It is what makes there be things that exist. And the answer to that question is not, and structurally cannot be, one of the things.

Here the essay reaches the edge of what it is entitled to claim, and should say so, keeping the method the corpus keeps. What has been argued is a philosophical proposition about ontological dependence: that nothing within the physical figure grounds the figure, that the order's being-actual is derivative, that the necessity which might have rescued it is either too weak to do so or is the Non-Derivative under another name. That is the weaker and surer claim, and it is all that has been established. It has not been established that the Ground so reached is personal, or provident, or the God the Church confesses as Father, Son, and Spirit — that is a further question, and a humbler one, not settled by anything here and not pretended to be. The essay offers the unbeliever no more than the shape of the place where a ground must be, and the demonstration that no fixture of his own physics can fill it. Whether that place has a face is the next question, and a different one.

That the ground of a being might be not a further being alongside it but the condition of there being any — present to the finite as its ground and not as its largest member — is a thought de Lubac and Rahner pressed far past where this essay stands, and they are invoked, as the corpus invokes its witnesses, as company on the road and not as authorities whose names decide anything. Wittgenstein, from the far side of every church, saw the same seam and marked it in a line: that the world is, and not how it is, is the mystical. The how is physics, and inexhaustible, and the unbeliever is its rightful master. The that is the question this essay has been unable to stop asking, and the one his brute fact answers by forbidding.

<br>

## VIII. A Formal Statement

Two sentences, offered as the definitions this essay exists to fix.

The physical order is derivative: its laws, constants, and actualities exhibit determinate structures they do not thereby confer upon themselves, so that no element of the figure — and no sum of elements under the name the universe — is that upon which the figure depends; and the necessity that would exempt the order is either the trivial necessity of a law's holding where it holds, which removes no contingency, or the metaphysical necessity of that-which-could-not-not-be, which is no fixture of physics but the Non-Derivative reached by another road.

To call the order a brute fact is not to have found its floor but to have stopped asking of the whole the question one has answered of every part: it is a decision, not a discovery — the founding of the intelligible upon an unintelligibility — and it denies not that things are, but that their being is finally intelligible, withdrawing at the ground the very assumption under which the whole search for reasons was conducted.

<br>

## IX. Coda

The skeptic may keep everything. That is the strange mercy of the argument and its whole modesty: nothing is taken from him. The monk still breathes; the laws still carry the second into the next second; the wager on tomorrow is still a good one. He may hold the entire figure in his hands and deny that it has a ground, and no fact within the figure will rise up to contradict him, because the ground was never going to be a fact within the figure.

But he cannot keep, in the denying, the light he read the figure by. He reached the depths of the order by refusing, at every rung, to call a thing its own reason; and at the last rung he calls the whole its own reason and lays the refusal down. He may do it. He may found the intelligible on the unintelligible and call the founding a floor. What he may not do is call it a discovery, or pretend the question fell silent of its own accord, or forget that the silence at the bottom is one he imposed and not one he found. The order does not explain itself. It is, of all things, the last that could — for it is the one thing every part of which was explained, by him, by being shown to stand on something further; and there is nothing further that the whole of it, alone among the things he examined, is permitted to stand on, except that it not be asked.

They studied the works, and did not find the artisan; from the good things seen, they could not know the One who is — not because He was hidden among the things and they searched too shallowly, but because He was never among the things to be found, being not the greatest thing that is, but the reason there is anything at all:

**I AM WHO I AM.**
