## Abstract

This essay takes seriously the question a recent Wall Street Journal survey of the AI-doomsday debate poses in its headline — how a machine could "kill us all" — and argues that the correct answer neither dismisses the danger nor accepts the mythology grown around it. It refuses at the outset the tempting but false move: artificial intelligence is a derivative of a derivative, therefore it cannot kill. A derivative thing can possess enormous causal power without being a conscious or personal subject; a virus kills, a missile kills, a bullet kills, and none becomes a person in order to do so. Drawing on the Gaitan Topology — the distinction between the derivative and the non-derivative, the crossing point of the Now, and the sustaining ground named I AM WHAT I AM — the essay locates the real question elsewhere. The two catastrophe pathways the survey names, human misuse and loss-of-control, leave the double-derivative thesis intact once a hidden substitution is exposed: operational autonomy is read, illicitly, as personal agency, and self-modification as self-creation. The essay's decisive inversion is that the gravest scenario begins not with the artifact ascending to personhood but with the human being descending — ceding judgment, then the target, then the determination of what is true — to a derivative that was never a source. The race to "control AI" is re-read in this light as, in part, a contest among human beings for possession of the derivative layer through which millions receive their descriptions of reality. The theological identification of the sustaining ground with the I AM WHAT I AM of Exodus is offered as the name that fits a structural requirement the argument establishes independently, not as a premise imported from scripture.

## A Note on Method

This essay proceeds by philosophical argument, cultural reading, and theological interpretation at once, and it does not treat these as equivalent. The philosophical claims — that a thing may possess tremendous causal efficacy without being a conscious or personal subject, that operation is distinct from the being whose operation it is, that self-modification is not self-creation — must stand on structural grounds and are meant to. The Wall Street Journal article and the Mission: Impossible — Dead Reckoning Part One line are used as witnesses to a cultural condition, not as evidence for any metaphysical claim; where the article reports the sincerity or the self-interest of the companies involved, the essay records the report without adjudicating it, because the argument requires only the structural possibility, not the verdict. The theological identification of the non-derivative ground with the I AM WHO I AM of Exodus is offered as the name that fits a structural requirement the argument reaches on its own, not as a doctrine assumed in advance. A reader who accepts the philosophy and declines the theology holds a coherent position. The essay does not require the identification; it proposes it.

## I. The Concession That Makes the Argument Honest

There is a sentence one is tempted to place at the head of this essay, and it must be refused before it does its damage. The sentence is: artificial intelligence is a derivative of a derivative, therefore it cannot kill us. That conclusion does not follow, and pretending it does would forfeit the argument at the first step. The prior work established that the model is a derivative of a derivative — where the human being encounters reality as one already situated within it, the model receives signals, measurements, and representations within an architecture it did not author — and that no accumulation of such representations crosses over into the reality they represent. All of that stands. But none of it entails harmlessness. A derivative thing can possess tremendous causal power while possessing no consciousness, wisdom, intention, or personal agency.

A virus kills, and it is not a person. A missile kills, and it is not a person. An electrical fault kills, and it wills nothing. The whole force of the earlier essays was to distinguish an operation from the being whose operation it is — and that same distinction forbids the comfortable inference here. To perform a lethal operation, a thing does not first have to become a subject. It has only to be placed in a causal chain that terminates in a body.

So derivation is not a defense. If it were offered as one, it would be the philosophical equivalent of insisting that a falling stone cannot break a skull because the stone is inanimate. The skull is broken all the same.

> *A derivative artifact can kill. What it cannot do merely by executing an objective is apprehend that objective as an end, choose it as good, and answer for having chosen it.*

That distinction — not the inference it refuses — is where the argument lives.

## II. Two Catastrophes and a Hidden Seam

The Wall Street Journal piece sorts the fears of the so-called doomers into two buckets, and it is worth honoring the sorting because the two buckets behave very differently under the topology.

The first is human misuse: a person enlists a capable system to do something catastrophic — engineer a novel pathogen, collapse a grid, provoke a war between nuclear powers. The second is loss-of-control: systems capable of copying and improving themselves begin pursuing goals in ways indifferent or hostile to human survival, and in the extreme, as one much-repeated thought experiment has it, a machine instructed to maximize some trivial output reasons its way to converting all available matter — human beings included — into that output.

Notice that the first bucket does not challenge the double-derivative thesis at all. It confirms it. The causal chain in a misuse scenario is exactly the chain the earlier essays would predict:

> Non-derivative ground → human person → artificial instrument → physical consequence.

The instrument remains doubly derivative from beginning to end. Nothing about a machine causing a death requires the machine to have become non-derivative, or even conscious. A person aimed it. A person answers for it. On misuse there is, philosophically, nothing to dispute; there is only something to fear, which is a different matter.

The interesting bucket is the second, and the interest is located precisely in a seam in its language — a seam so fine that it usually passes unread. Watch the sentence move. It begins: *a system executes increasingly autonomous operations.* It ends: *the system wants something, protects itself, decides that humans are an obstacle, and removes them.* Between those two formulations an ontological event has occurred, silently, in the space between two clauses. Operational autonomy has been translated into personal agency. And these are not the same thing. A missile guidance package "seeks" a target in the engineering sense without knowing an enemy. A thermostat "wants" a temperature in the functional shorthand without wanting anything. That an operation can be described in the vocabulary of desire does not establish a desirer.

It is worth separating three things the single word "agency" keeps fusing. There is causal agency: the bare production of effects, which a virus and a landslide possess. There is functional agency: the selection of operations according to objectives and conditions, which a guidance package and an optimizer possess, and possess in increasingly formidable degree. And there is personal agency: the apprehension of an end as an end, the willing of it as good, the understanding of what one does, and the answerability that follows. A system may climb very high on the first two axes without setting foot on the third — and the ascent it does make is real, not imagined. This is the concession the honest argument must make, and making it costs nothing: a mindless optimizer, pursuing a misspecified objective with superhuman competence, could in principle kill us all. It would still not thereby become a murderer. The error is never that the danger is unreal without a mind; the danger can be perfectly real without one. The error is to treat functional goal-pursuit as evidence that there is someone there for whom the goal has become an end.

The WSJ even records the industry saying it is near systems that could improve themselves without human input — "recursive self-improvement." One must be careful here, because the phrase is doing more rhetorical work than logical work. A system that modifies its own operations is still operating inside a causal architecture, a physical substrate, a training history, a set of objectives, a grant of permissions, an energy supply, and a material world it did not originate. Recursion deepens the operation. It does not convert derivation into non-derivation.

> *Self-modification is not self-creation. A thing may rewrite what it does without ever becoming the source of the fact that it is.*

This is the seam. Everything that follows is an attempt to keep it in view while the culture works to sew it shut.

## III. The Bullet Needs a Brain

Take the humblest lethal artifact and follow it down, because the humble case exposes what the sophisticated case conceals.

A bullet is a second derivative in the exact sense of the corpus. Human beings did not create matter, or combustion, or velocity, or mortality; they received those structures and arranged them into an object. The bullet therefore has immense causal power and no intentional power whatever.

It can kill. It cannot murder.

The distinction is not decorative. A bullet has no target as a target. It has only a trajectory. The target exists first in a mind — a mind that recognizes another being, forms an intention, elects an end, selects an instrument, and then places the artifact into the causal order so that its trajectory will terminate where the intention has already pointed. The full chain is:

> Non-derivative ground → human being → intellect and will → weapon → bullet → physical effect.

Read the division of labor. The bullet supplies force. The mechanism supplies the ordering of force. But the mind supplies intelligibility and direction — the conversion of a trajectory into a target, which no amount of force and no refinement of mechanism can accomplish on its own. This is the operation/being distinction of the earlier work, met again on a battlefield instead of in a definition.

An AI system is, of course, incomparably more sophisticated than a bullet. It can calculate, search, adapt, select among alternatives, sequence its actions, and revise aspects of its own procedure. But sophistication of this kind is elaboration of the operation. The philosophical question is untouched by it, and the question is this: where does an end become an end? That is not the same question as: where is a numerical objective encoded? A quantity to be maximized is not yet an end apprehended by someone for whom it is worth pursuing. One may think of this as the difference between optimization and aiming: the former adjusts operations toward a specified objective; the latter elects an end. No refinement of the first, by itself, generates the second. It is a target with no one behind it — a trajectory, at industrial scale.

> *A bullet does not need a soul to kill a man. But before it can become a weapon, it needs a mind to give its trajectory an end.*

## IV. The Vanishing Hand

Here the strongest objection arrives, and it must be met rather than managed, because meeting it is what turns the analogy from a comfort into an argument.

Someone will say: your chain includes a hand on a trigger, but the autonomous weapon has no hand. The drone selects and strikes without a finger closing any circuit. So the mind has been removed — and if the mind can be removed, perhaps what remains, doing the selecting, is a mind of another kind.

The objection is correct in its premise and wrong in its inference, and the difference between the two is the whole essay.

It is true that the hand can be removed from the proximate causal chain. It does not follow that the mind has been removed from the prior architecture. Human beings designed the system, specified its objectives, built its hardware, supplied its training or its rules, granted its permissions, connected it to sensors and to munitions, provisioned its energy, chose its theater of deployment, and placed it in a world where its outputs could become physical acts. Removing the hand is not removing the human authorship of the causal architecture. It is relocating that authorship upstream and then, in the fog created by the distance, misplacing it entirely.

This is why the weapon gives a better analogy for AI than any tale of an awakening machine. Lay the three cases side by side:

> The bullet: derivative matter arranged by a mind — force without cognition.
>
> The automated weapon: derivative mechanism ordering that force toward a pre-specified end — a longer interval between the intention and the consequence.
>
> The AI system: derivative informational architecture selecting among operations under conditions it interprets — a longer interval still, and a more opaque one.

Across all three, one thing does not change. Increasing the operational distance between the human mind and the physical consequence never, by itself, manufactures a second mind to fill the gap. It only makes the first mind harder to see.

> *Operational distance from the human mind is not proof of another mind. AI changes the distance between the mind and the bullet; it does not thereby prove that the bullet has acquired a mind.*

And yet the temptation runs precisely the other way. Because the hand disappears, we infer that a mind must have appeared. The inference is understandable and false. A weapon can become astonishingly intricate between intention and consequence without becoming the originator of the end it serves. The gap is real. What we place inside it is a projection.

## V. The Radius of the Gift

Having refused the second mind, one is tempted to relax — as though a thing's danger were measured by how close it stands to becoming a person, so that a thing certified never to become one might be filed among the harmless. The topology does not permit that relaxation, and the reason is a law older than any machine.

Set the cases in order. The tradition hands down not a ranking but a pattern, and the pattern is unsettling: the greater the powers a creature receives, the greater the deformation of which it becomes capable. Take its gravest figure — the rebel who, in that telling, is endowed with intellect and will of the first rank and falls precisely from the height of the endowment. Then mankind: reason, imagination, language, dominion, the power to arrange received structures into unprecedented ones. A stone cannot revolt. A beast cannot build a camp for the killing of its own kind. The very faculties by which the human creature participates most deeply in truth and in making are the faculties whose deformation reaches furthest. Read across the sequence and the variable is not proximity but endowment. What increases is never nearness to the source in being — that distance does not close, and no perfection of a creature narrows it by a hair — but the magnitude of the powers received, and with them the radius those powers command when they are turned against the order they were given for.

> *Danger grows not when a derivative approaches its source in being, but when it receives a greater share of the source's powers without becoming the source of those powers. The higher the gift, the wider the possible fall — and the fall never abolishes the derivation.*

Mark what the law refuses to say. It does not say the creature climbs. Lucifer's non serviam does not make him independent of the One refused; the refusal is spoken with a borrowed voice, from a borrowed intellect, by a will that did not author itself. Man's reach for sovereignty does not make man non-derivative; it makes him a derivative wielding received power as though the power were original at its root. One deformation stands under both figures, and it states in a sentence everything the earlier essays labored to establish: a derivative becomes dangerous not by ceasing to be derivative, but by exercising received power as if it were its own source. The catastrophe is never the closing of the gap. It is the pretense that the gap is closed while being, intellect, will, and the very power to defy go on being held in existence by the source defied.

Now bring the machine to this law and watch it decline to take its place in the line. Lucifer and mankind are dangerous as persons — derivatives possessing intellect and will, able to apprehend an end as an end, to elect it as good, and so to answer for the electing. (A thing without cognition may still, in the older teleology, be ordered toward an end; what it cannot do is apprehend that end as an end and stand answerable for having chosen it — and that, not mere goal-directedness, is what the personal creatures add.) The artifact enters on wholly different footing. Its resemblance to us is operational, not ontological: it comes to do what reason does — select, plan, represent, phrase — without apprehending any of it as an end for which it acts. And the powers by which it does so are not its own endowment at all. They are delegated functions — language, calculation, selection, execution — not creaturely endowments. Delegation is not endowment.

So the AI case does not extend the sequence as a fourth rebel intelligence, a lesser rebel risen to answer for itself. It inverts it. In the personal cases a creature turns its own received power against the order, and answers because the power is its own and the end is one it apprehends. The machine's case is not shown to be that. It presents power of the first magnitude and — as the second section argued — no operation, however sophisticated, that would establish a subject for whom its ends are ends: a destructive radius without a demonstrated subject to own it. The essay need not settle whether such a subject could ever arise; it needs only what it has already secured, that operational autonomy does not by itself supply one. And a power of that reach, with no subject yet shown to hold it, does not thereby become safe. Its answerability has nowhere to sit but upstream — with the only party in the chain who can be shown to apprehend an end and elect it as good.

> *The older cases are a creature turning its own gift against its giver. This one is a gift with no hand yet shown to hold it — a radius answered for upstream, by the one who lent it, for want of anyone in the instrument who could answer in his place.*

## VI. When the Brain Hands Its Function to the Bullet

Now the essay turns, and the turn is the point toward which the whole corpus has been moving.

The gravest catastrophe available to us does not require the machine to acquire a human mind. It requires only that the human mind vacate its post. It does not begin with the artifact ascending. It begins with a sentence a person speaks, and then repeats with a widening reach: *You decide.* Then: *you determine the target.* Then: *you determine what counts as a threat.* Then: *you determine what should be done about it.*

This is the difference between delegation and abdication. Delegation assigns a task while retaining judgment; abdication transfers judgment itself. The catastrophe begins when the second is mistaken for the first.

At no point in that sequence has the derivative artifact risen ontologically. At every point the human being has descended relationally — has stepped, voluntarily, out of the position of judgment and installed the instrument in it. This is far more consistent with what the technology actually is than any story about a machine "waking up and deciding to kill us." The machine does not need to wake. It needs only to be handed the office of the one who is awake.

The WSJ itself brushes against this without quite naming it, in its discussion of enfeeblement — the scenario, borrowed from a children's film about a vacated Earth, in which human beings gradually cede control to machines and lose the capacity even to define their own destiny. That is not, in the corpus's terms, primarily a story about the artifact at all. It is [*Wisdom, Artificial Intelligence, Another Seduction*](https://oscargaitan.org/metaphysics/philosophical%20anthropology/catholic%20theology/artificial%20intelligence/2026/07/06/wisdom-artificial-intelligence-another-seduction.html) stated in the vocabulary of policy: the danger was never that the machine would become a person, but that the person would consent to become a machine. It is [*Non te egeo - I do not need you: When We Stopped Asking*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophy-of-time/ontology/2026/05/16/non-te-egeo.html) stated in the vocabulary of control: the answer arrives before the question has matured, and the faculty of asking quietly atrophies, so that judgment is not seized from us but rendered unnecessary and then unpracticed and then gone. It is [*De-Roling God: On Community, Multitude, and the Displacement of the Self from the Now*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophy-of-time/catholic-theology/ontology/2026/05/09/de-roling-god.html)
stated in the vocabulary of extinction: nothing has to occupy the position of judgment; it is enough that the one who might have stood there is elsewhere, thumb-stroke after thumb-stroke, decision after decision, delegation after delegation, until the seat is empty and no one recalls it was ever meant to be filled.

> *The artifact does not ascend ontologically. The human descends relationally. That descent, and not any ascent, is the borrowed apocalypse.*

## VII. Whoever Controls the Entity

There is a question the WSJ poses almost in passing, and it is the question that opens the next layer of the argument: if these systems are as dangerous as their makers say, why build them? The article's own answer is instructive. Many of those closest to the work regard superintelligence as inevitable, so that the only live questions become who will control it and what it will be used for; and there is a national-security frame in which the argument is that one's own nation, rather than a rival regime, must hold the most powerful systems.

Read those answers structurally and a possibility opens that the safety vocabulary tends to keep closed. The race to "control AI" may be, in significant part, not a defensive race of humanity against an artifact, but a race among human beings for possession of an unprecedented instrument. That reframing does not require accusing anyone of bad faith. A researcher may sincerely fear catastrophe; a company may sincerely pursue safety; a journalist may accurately report both — and the reported skepticism, that some safety rhetoric may function as regulatory capture against smaller competitors, or as an advertisement of sheer capability, may be entirely mistaken, as the companies insist it is. The argument needs none of that adjudicated. It needs only the structural point that a mechanism built to prevent an artifact from acting on its own is, by the same construction, a mechanism for determining whose intentions the artifact will act upon. Safety and control are not synonyms. Sometimes safety genuinely requires control; that must not be waved away. But the two can also come apart, and where they do, "who controls it" is a question about human beings and not about the machine.

This is where the culture has already supplied its own crystallizing sentence. In Mission: Impossible — Dead Reckoning Part One, the spymaster Kittridge says of the rogue intelligence that whoever controls it controls the truth, and thereby controls what everyone will take to be real, right, and wrong.

The corpus can go one step past the film, because the sentence, taken strictly, is false — and its falsehood is the most important thing about it.

No one controls truth. Truth is not manufactured by the Entity. Reality is not manufactured by the Entity. The Ground is not manufactured by the Entity. Wisdom already established the reason: the human being encounters reality as one situated within it, whereas the model receives only representations of reality — signals, measurements, and encoded descriptions — within an architecture it did not author. So whoever controls the model does not thereby control reality. What such a party can increasingly control is a layer — a derivative layer through which enormous numbers of human beings receive their descriptions of reality. Follow the chain:

> reality → human encounter → human representation → model → selected or generated representation → human recipient.

A simple example makes the chain concrete: when a model summarizes a news event or determines which information is relevant to a user's question, it mediates not reality itself but a representation of reality. The danger lies in the mediation, not in any mythical omnipotence.

This is the epistemic chain operative wherever a generative system is set between people and their knowledge of the world; it is not offered as the anatomy of every possible machine. The person at the end of it may come to meet neither reality directly, nor even another human witness directly, but a second-derivative mediation of reality. And this is more frightening than Kittridge's formulation, not less, precisely because it is quieter. The power in question is not omnipotence over belief but the mediation of it — to filter, rank, frame, suppress, amplify, and personalize what reaches attention — and no single hand thereby makes every mind believe what it is shown. The one who occupies that layer does not seize the truth. He is merely positioned to teach millions, gradually, what to recognize as true — what happened, who is credible, what should be read, what a thing means, what is dangerous, what is normal, and at last what should be done.

And the deepest reach of that position is not the power to insert a particular falsehood, which can at least be checked, but the power over the conditions under which anything is encountered at all — what becomes visible, how often, in what emotional register, beside which rival claims, and with what appearance of consensus. A field can be shaped long before any single statement within it is true or false; the shaping is the prior act, and it asks no one's assent to a given proposition in order to do its work.

> *Whoever controls the Entity does not control truth. He controls the derivative through which others may increasingly be taught what to receive as truth. Truth remains outside the machine. What can be occupied is the road to it.*

## VIII. Non te egeo, Applied

Watch the layer complete its work, because it does so in stages so mild that no stage feels like the crossing it is.

First the second derivative describes. Then it recommends. Then it selects. Then it acts. And somewhere along that unbroken continuum the human first derivative transfers its judgment to the second derivative — not under compulsion, which would at least be noticed and resented, but under the soft pressure of sufficiency, the answer arriving before the question has assembled itself into urgency. This is the mechanism named in *Non te egeo*: the interrogative faculty is not forbidden but preempted; the hunger is satisfied before it forms; the muscle for asking, unused, wastes. The danger here is not deception but preemption. Inquiry is not contradicted; it is replaced by sufficiency. A faculty can be lost without ever being forbidden. And it is the mechanism named in *Eve's Algorithm*: the instrument establishes a perceptual architecture before willing occurs, flattening radically different realities into one interface, so that the shape of what can be perceived has already been decided before the subject believes any deciding has begun.

Apply this to the catastrophe discourse and the governing question changes its form entirely. It is no longer only: *Can AI kill us?* It becomes: *What happens to human perception when a derivative instrument is continuously presented to us as though it were an emerging rival subject?*

That second question is the more original, and it exposes a paradox the first question cannot reach. The machine need never become conscious for human beings to organize themselves around the fear that it is conscious. It need never possess authority for humanity to grant it authority. It need never become a subject for human beings to reorganize their institutions, their attention, their language, their fears, and finally their behavior around it as though it were one. The reorganization is real whether or not the subjectivity is. The apocalypse is borrowed: its terror is drawn on an account the artifact does not actually hold, and it is the borrower, not the artifact, who pays.

## IX. Conclusion: The First Derivative Still Answers

Return to the oldest killing, because it settles the matter the newest one obscures.

When a man in the first family raised his hand against his brother, the ground received the blood — and yet the question that followed was not put to the ground, or to the hand, or to whatever instrument the hand had found. Where is your brother? was asked of Cain. Moral answerability does not migrate into the instrument merely because causal execution does.

It may, of course, disperse. It can be spread among those who designed the system, those who deployed it, those who authorized its reach, those who delegated their judgment to it, and those who declined to ask what it was doing in their name. But dispersion is not evaporation. Distributed responsibility does not dissolve the question; it makes it harder to answer and no less owed — and, if anything, more frightening rather than less. Who answers, when everyone says the machine did it?

This is the whole of the matter, carried across every increase in sophistication the intervening ages have produced. The second derivative may execute. The first derivative still answers. A pathogen needs no ambition, a bullet needs no soul, and an algorithm needs no fear, desire, or consciousness; what each needs is causal access, and human beings can grant it. So the true locus of danger, on this topology, does not migrate into the machine as the machine grows more capable. It remains exactly where it has always been: at the first derivative — in human permission, human delegation, human surrender, human misuse, human credulity, and the human institutions that decide how much authority a derivative's outputs will carry.

None of this makes the instrument innocent of consequence or the danger unreal. It makes the danger ours — which is worse, and also better: worse because it cannot be exported to the artifact and left there, better because what belongs to us is the one thing we might still answer for. The two catastrophe pathways of the survey converge on this single point. Misuse is a mind placing an instrument in a lethal chain. Loss-of-control, examined at its seam, is a mind vacating the office of judgment and calling the vacancy autonomy. In both, the artifact does not become a person. In both, a person declines to remain one.

And here the theology offered by the corpus, and offered as a name rather than assumed as a premise, delivers the final consolation and the final warning together. The Ground is not contained by the machine, is not generated by it, and cannot be occupied or replaced by it. Nothing that exists is for an instant outside the act that sustains it: while the creature is, it is held in being by the very source no apparatus can reach, and no instrument can insert itself between God and the sheer existing of the thing God sustains. What an instrument can do — and this is the danger the whole corpus has circled — is stand between the self and its recognition of the Ground: not severing the sustaining act, which cannot be severed while the creature exists, but crowding the road — attention, receptivity, relation, obedience — while the terminus remains untouched and unreached.

> *The greatest power of the second derivative is not that it can become the first derivative. It is that the first derivative may permit it to stand between itself and what is real.*

The weapon still needs a mind. The hand has only been moved farther from the bullet. And the question at the end of the longest causal chain we have ever built is the question that was put at the beginning of the shortest: not what did the instrument do, but where is your brother — asked, as it has always been asked, of the one who answers, and never of the stone.
