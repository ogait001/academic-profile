## Note on Sources and Method

This essay belongs to the same sequence as [*Where Is God?*](https://oscargaitan.org/catholic-theology/philosophy-of-time/ontology/2026/05/05/where-is-god.html), [*The Corridor*](https://oscargaitan.org/catholic-theology/philosophy-of-time/ontology/2026/05/28/the-corridor.html), [*One Universe, Many Crossings*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophy-of-cosmology/metaphysics-of-spacetime/metaphysics-of-time/2026/09/24/one-universe-many-crossings-en.html), [*Is the Beginningless Grounded?*](https://oscargaitan.org/metaphysics/philosophy-of-time/divine-eternity/2026/09/25/is-the-beginningless-grounded-en.html), and [*God, the Universe, and the Now*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophy-of-time/catholic-theology/ontology/2026/06/07/god-the-universe-and-the-now.html), and it recalls their results rather than re-deriving them. From *Where Is God?* and *The Corridor* it takes the distinction between God as an agent within events and God as the sustaining ground of the Now. From *One Universe, Many Crossings* it takes the placement of the universe as the single physical created order, not a continuant and not the Ground. From *Is the Beginningless Grounded?* it takes the matched idols — the spatial idol that seats the Ground as the largest region, the temporal idol that seats Him as the earliest instant — and completes the pair with a third. From [*The Ground That Knows*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophical%20theology/metaphysics/ontology/2026/07/27/the-ground-that-knows.html) it takes the asymmetry of dependence, by which what is wholly derivative is wholly other. From [*Begotten, Not Made*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophical-theology/medieval-philosophy/aquinas/2026/09/26/begotten-not-made-en.html) it takes the distinction between being-from-another and being-a-part-of-another. The occasion of the essay is ordinary: a conversation, pressed with some heat, in which the proposition God is the universe — and, when the universe seemed too small to carry it, God is everything — was advanced not as a conclusion to be argued but as a definition already settled.

The method is the one the sequence has kept throughout. It takes a word that is quietly doing two jobs and pries the jobs apart, on the conviction that most of what looks like a metaphysical disagreement is a single word overheard as one claim when it is two. The witnesses invoked — Aquinas above all, with Spinoza as the honest adversary, and Augustine, Boethius, and the Scriptures of creation as company on the road — are not authorities from which the argument descends. It stands or falls on its own structural coherence. And it keeps the corpus's discipline about charity: the strongest form of the opposing position is stated before it is answered, and the true intuition buried inside the error is named and kept.

## Abstract

A proposition is advanced against the corpus from two sides at once: that God is the universe, and, when that is felt to leave something out, that God is everything. The two are one claim at two sizes, and this essay argues that the size was never the point. The equivocation lives in a single word. Aggregatively, everything names the totality of beings, the sum of the derivative; and God is everything, taken so, is pantheism. As grounding, everything means what Scripture means by from Him and through Him and to Him are all things — that every being is from and in God, which is the reverse of an identity. Pantheism turns the second sense into the first, losing the distinction in the loss of a preposition: everything is from God is not God is everything. The essay then shows three things. That the demand for a ground is not a demand for something outside the world, since dependence is not spatial exteriority and closing the inventory does not make it necessary. That totality is a third idol, after the spatial and temporal idols of the earlier sequence, and falls by the same lesson, for the Ground is on no axis the figure has: the universe cannot become God by becoming large enough, and the All cannot become God by including enough. And that the decisive question, once aggregation has done its work and Spinoza has been met at his full strength, is not how much is summed but what dependence is — whether to be from another is to inhere in it as a mode, or whether, as creation holds, one may be from another without being of it. It closes by returning to the pantheist the nearness he was reaching for, which identity abolishes and grounding secures: not we are He, but in Him.

<br>

## I. The Two Sentences

Two sentences are set side by side, as the sequence has learned to set them, because the grinding between them is where the work is. God is the universe. God is everything. They arrive together, and they arrive from a speaker who is not asking a question but correcting a confusion he believes you have fallen into — that you have gone on talking about a God somewhere beyond or behind the world when the world, or the whole of what there is, was God all along. The first sentence names the cosmos and calls it divine. The second, said a moment later and usually with more heat, names not merely the cosmos but the totality of whatever exists, material and immaterial, and calls that divine. The move from the first to the second is quick and is rarely noticed by the one making it, and noticing it is the beginning of the answer.

For the two sentences are not two claims. They are one claim at two sizes. The first identifies God with the physical whole. The second, having felt that the physical whole leaves something out, that there are thoughts and numbers and souls and the moral law that no telescope finds, enlarges the identification until nothing is left outside it. God is the universe becomes God is everything by the simple expedient of making the subject bigger. And the thesis of this essay, stated before it is earned so that the reader knows where the road goes, is that the enlargement changes nothing, because the error was never that the identification was too small. One does not repair a category mistake by increasing its scope. If the universe could not be God, the All cannot be God either, and for the very same reason; and the reason has nothing to do with how much either one contains.

It is worth saying at once what is not in dispute. This essay does not deny that everything which exists is intimately related to God, nor that God is present to every particle and every thought, nor that there is nowhere a creature could go to escape Him. The corpus has affirmed those things at length and will affirm them again here, more strongly than the pantheist can. What is in dispute is a single word — is — and behind it a single operation, identity. Between God is present to everything and God is everything there lies the whole distance between the Creator and what He makes, and the pantheist has stepped across it without noticing there was a step.

<br>

## II. The Objection at Full Strength

The sequence does not answer softened objections, and this one has a strong form that deserves to be built before it is met. It is not the careless pantheism of the bumper sticker, for which God is a warm name draped over the sum of nice things. It is Spinoza's, and it is formidable.

Deus sive Natura: God, or Nature. On this account there is exactly one substance, that which is in itself and is conceived through itself, that which is its own cause, whose essence involves existence. Spinoza saw, with more rigor than most of his admirers, that there cannot be two such substances, for they would have to be distinguished by something, and there is nothing outside the one substance by which to distinguish a second. So there is one, and everything that is — every body, every mind, every event — is not a thing apart from it but a mode of it, a way the one substance is modified, as a wave is a way the sea is moving and not a second thing beside the sea. On this view the whole apparatus of this corpus is a mistake at the root. When you speak of the creature depending on a Ground other than itself, Spinoza answers that you have smuggled in a duality the concept of substance forbids. There are not two things, the derivative and the Non-Derivative, with a relation of dependence running between them. There is one thing, and your "dependence" is merely the way a mode follows from the substance whose mode it is.

And the interlocutor has a second move, blunter than Spinoza's and harder to shake because it feels like plain sense. The universe, he says, is everything there is. There is nothing outside it. Therefore it cannot depend on anything, because depending on something means being held up by something else, and there is no else. Your whole topology of grounding, he says, requires a beyond — something the universe leans on — and the one thing we can be certain of is that there is no beyond. Whatever you want to call God, if it exists, it is inside everything, which is to say it is part of everything, which is to say it is everything, or a piece of it. You have no room left for a Ground that is not simply one more item in the total you have already closed.

Behind both moves stands the thing that actually drives the conversation, and it is not an argument but a prior identity, held before any argument begins: God equals Nature equals the Universe equals Everything. Once that identity is in place, every sentence the classical tradition speaks will sound to him like a failure to grasp the obvious — that there is only one thing and we are inside it — and the heat in the conversation is the heat of a man watching someone decline to see what he takes to be plainly in front of them both. He is not, in that moment, disagreeing with a conclusion of yours. He is defending a definition. And that, precisely, is where the answer must begin, because an argument and a definition cannot be made to collide until it is clear which one is on the table.

<br>

## III. The Hidden Leap

Start where the sequence always starts, with the grammar, because the decisive move is made in the grammar before it is made in the metaphysics. The pantheist reasons, in effect, from a premise everyone grants to a conclusion the premise does not contain. The premise is a tautology: everything that exists belongs to the totality of what exists. Of course it does; that is what totality means. There is no being that is left over, no item outside the inventory of items, for the inventory is defined as the one that leaves nothing out. Grant it entirely; it costs nothing, because it says nothing. It is true the way a bachelor is unmarried.

The conclusion is an identity: the totality is God. And between the premise and the conclusion there is a step that is never taken in the open, because taken in the open it would not survive. From everything belongs to the totality nothing whatever follows about whether the totality is divine, is necessary, is self-sufficient, or is a mode of one substance. All that follows is that the totality is total. To get from there to the totality is God, one needs a second premise — that the totality is the kind of thing that could be God, that it has aseity, that it is its own cause — and that premise is exactly the thing in question. It has not been established. It has been assumed under cover of the tautology, so that the hearer, having nodded at the trivial truth that everything is in everything, is invited to nod again at the substantive falsehood that everything is God, as though the second nod were owed by the first.

This is the hidden leap, and once it is seen it cannot be unseen. Belonging to a totality is not being identical with it, and being identical with it is not being its ground. Three different relations have been pressed into one word. A brick belongs to the wall; the brick is not the wall; and neither the brick nor the wall is the architect. The pantheist's argument has the form: every brick is in the wall, therefore the wall is the architect — and the therefore is carrying weight that nothing under it supports. The burden, properly placed, is entirely his. It is not for the theist to find something outside everything; it is for the pantheist to show that the totality, which he has correctly observed contains all beings, is itself the sort of being that needs no ground. He has shown that it is total. He has not shown that it is God, and the one does not imply the other any more than the sum of all debts is a fortune.

## IV. Dependence Is Not Exteriority

The second move of Section II now stands exposed enough to answer: the insistence that since nothing is outside the universe, the universe can depend on nothing. This is the most intuitive of the pantheist's weapons, and it is intuitive because it pictures dependence as a spatial relation — one thing propped against another thing beside it, a wall shoring up a roof, a floor holding up a bed. On that picture, to depend is to lean on something external, and if there is no external, there is nothing to lean on, and the demand for a Ground is incoherent from the start.

But the picture is the very one the whole sequence was written to dismantle, and it has been dismantled before under other names. *Is the Beginningless Grounded?* showed that the Ground is not reached by going far enough back along the line of time, because grounding was never a first push at the head of the series. *One Universe, Many Crossings* showed that the Ground is not found by going far enough out into space, because He is no region of the manifold and the manifold has no outside for Him to occupy. The present confusion is the same spatial figure wearing its last disguise. To say the creature depends on God is not to say that God is somewhere outside the creature, holding it up across a gap, as a hand holds a cup. It is to say that the creature's very being-actual is received and not had of itself — that at this crossing, now, its existence is given rather than owned — and this is a fact about the creature's ontological status, not about its spatial neighbors.

So the question dependence poses has never been what is outside the universe. That question invents a God who is one more object, merely a very large and distant one, sitting beyond the cosmic edge — the object the pantheist is right to deny and that the corpus denies with him. The question dependence poses is why there is a universe at all, rather than nothing — why the totality, closed and complete and with nothing beside it, is actual rather than not. And that question is not answered, is not even touched, by the observation that the totality is closed. A closed totality that might not have existed, and does, has precisely the feature that calls for a ground: its existence is not given with its concept. Closing the inventory does not make the inventory necessary. It makes it complete, which is a different predicate entirely, and the slide from complete to necessary is the temporal slide of the beginningless essay — from the series has no first term to the series needs no ground — run now across the whole rather than along the line. There is no outside, and there did not need to be one, because the Ground was never going to be found outside. It is found, as it was always found, by asking of the whole the question each part already raised: not where it leans, but why it is.

## V. The Word That Does Two Jobs

Now the central unbundling, for which the rest has been preparation. The word is everything, and like now and past and beginningless and necessary before it, it is doing two jobs that the sequence must pry apart, because what is true in one of them is false in the other, and the pantheist's whole case lives in the crossing of the wires.

The first job is aggregative. Here everything names the totality of beings taken as a collection — the sum, the All, the complete list of what there is, gathered under one word. In this sense everything is a how-much: it answers the question how many things, how much being, and its answer is all of it, none omitted. Taken this way, God is everything says that God is this sum, this collection, the aggregate of all that exists. And in this sense the sentence is pantheism, and it is false, for reasons the following sections will make structural: the sum of beings is composed, is derivative, is other than its ground, and to call it God is to identify the Creator with the heap of creatures.

The second job is grounding. Here everything does not name a collection at all but functions as the Scriptures of creation use it, and the tradition after them, when they say that from Him and through Him and to Him are all things, and that in Him all things hold together. Read these with care and they do not say that God is the sum of beings. They say the reverse: that all beings are from Him, are sustained through Him, are held together in Him, return to Him. The little word all is here a word about derivation and not about aggregation. It tells you where everything comes from and in what it holds together; it does not tell you that everything is the one it comes from, any more than in him we live and move and have our being tells you that we are God — it tells you the opposite, that our living and moving and being are had in another. (Scripture does also say that God will be all in all, and the phrase is tempting to a pantheism essay; but it must be handled with care, for its context is eschatological. It is the promise of a consummation not yet given — what creation will be when it is wholly referred to God — and not a present identity of God with the sum of things. Romans and Colossians speak of the grounding that holds now; the all in all speaks of the end toward which the grounded is moving, and the two should not be made to say one thing.)

Set the two jobs beside each other and the pantheist's sentence falls into focus. God is everything, heard aggregatively, is the false identification of God with the sum. God is everything, heard as grounding, is a clumsy gesture at the true doctrine that everything is from and in God — which, said properly, is not God is everything but everything is God's, or more exactly, everything is from God and nothing is apart from Him. The pantheist has heard the second, which is true, and said the first, which is false, because the one word carried him from in Him all things hold together to God is all things without his noticing that the preposition changed everything. In Him is grounding. Is is identity. The whole error is the loss of the preposition.

<br>

## VI. The Universe Was Not Too Small

Return now to the first of the two sentences, God is the universe, and to the moment it became the second. The sequence has already fixed what the universe is. *One Universe, Many Crossings* placed it as the single physical created order — spacetime, matter and energy, fields, and the physical relations among them — within which physical continuants stand in relation; and it placed it with precision as not a crossing, not a lemniscate, and not a continuant, and emphatically not the Ground, which is no member of the order and not the sum of its members. *God, the Universe, and the Now* said the same thing in four words that this essay could take as its own: the universe is not God. It is the theater where the creature lives the encounter the Now makes possible.

So God is the universe fails directly, and the pantheist is himself quickest to feel why, for the identification with the physical order is the one he is readiest to abandon. The argument to come does not require the physical universe to exhaust what is; and whatever one finally concludes about mind, number, and the moral law — a quarrel this essay does not need to open — the pantheist rarely wishes to rest the divine on the physical alone. Feeling the cosmos too narrow a vessel for the divine, he enlarges the vessel until it holds literally everything — matter and mind, fact and value, the visible and the invisible — and offers that enlarged totality, the All, as the true referent of God.

And here is the hinge of the whole essay. The enlargement is a real improvement on exactly one axis and no improvement at all on the axis that matters. It is true that the All is more than the universe; the immaterial is not left out of it; it is, by construction, the thing that leaves nothing out. But the objection to God is the universe was never that the universe omitted some beings. The objection was that the universe is the wrong kind of thing to be God — composed, differentiated, changing, derivative, a structure of related parts none of which, and no sum of which, is its own reason. Enlarging it to the All does not touch that objection, because the All inherits every one of those marks and acquires a new one. It is still composed; it is now composed of more. It is still differentiated; it is now differentiated across more kinds. It is still a sum of the derivative; it is now the maximal such sum. The pantheist has answered the complaint the universe was too small and has not noticed that smallness was never the complaint. He has built a bigger theater and called it the playwright. And this is why the argument that follows needs no quarrel about what the universe does or does not contain: it grants the All everything it claims to include, concedes the inventory closed and complete, and denies only that completeness is grounding.

<br>

## VII. The Third Idol

The pattern of the corpus now completes itself, and it is worth seeing whole, because *Is the Beginningless Grounded?* demolished two idols and named them a matched pair, and this essay adds the third and shows that the three are one.

The spatial idol, exposed by [*Does Relativity Divide the Now?*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophy-of-time/philosophy-of-physics/special-relativity/2026/09/20/does-relativity-divide-the-now-en.html) and named in the beginningless essay, is the God who would be the largest region — the privileged frame, the true slice, the master clock, the biggest and best-placed thing in space. He is refused because a god who is the largest region is a god within the geometry, one more thing located in space, and the Ground is no member of what it grounds. The temporal idol, exposed by the beginningless essay itself, is the same error rotated onto the axis of time: the God who would be the earliest instant, the first cause at the head of the line, the oldest thing, whose sole office is to have been there before the rest. He is refused for the identical reason: a god who is the first term is a god within the series, one more moment supremely early, and the Ground is no member of the succession He grounds. Both idols, the essay found, arise from maximizing a creaturely magnitude — the widest space, the oldest time — and mistaking the limit case for the Ground.

The pantheist's All is the third member of the family, and it is in one way the purest of the three. For the spatial idol maximizes one magnitude, extent, and the temporal idol maximizes one magnitude, age; but the idol of totality maximizes being as such, across every axis at once. It does not seat the Ground at the far end of one dimension of the figure. It seats Him at the far end of all of them together, as the complete sum along every direction there is — all space, all time, all kinds, all beings, the figure pushed not to the limit of one of its axes but to the limit of the whole figure. The All is what you get when you maximize not a magnitude but the thing of which magnitudes are measures. And precisely because it is the most complete maximization, it is the clearest case of the single error the family embodies: that the Ground is reached by pushing some feature of the figure to its uttermost. He is not. He is on no axis the figure has, and the sum of all the axes is still a fact about the axes.

This is why the governing sentence of the beginningless essay returns here with a word changed and its force intact. There the sentence was: the line does not ground itself by being long; it does not ground itself by being endless. Here it is: the whole does not ground itself by being complete; it does not ground itself by being everything. One does not arrive at the Ground by maximizing extent, as the master clock supposed, nor by maximizing age, as the first cause supposed, nor by maximizing totality, as the All supposes — by the biggest, by the oldest, or by the most complete — for these are all members of the series in which bigness and age and completeness arise. The universe cannot become God merely by becoming sufficiently large. And the All cannot become God merely by including sufficiently much. To make the subject bigger was never going to work, because the Ground was never the biggest subject. He is the reason there is a subject at all.

Say it as the structural identity the corpus prefers. The two idols of the beginningless essay were one idol twice rotated; the idol of totality is that same idol a third time, and in its most honest dress, because it drops the pretense of a single dimension and simply maximizes everything, which is what the other two were covertly trying to do all along. Take away the biggest region and you have not taken away the Ground; take away the oldest instant and you have not taken away the Ground; take away — or rather, grant in full — the most complete totality, the All with nothing left out, and you still have not reached the Ground, because completeness was never the road to Him. You have the counterfeit: the Ground imagined as the sum of what He grounds. And the true one stands exactly where He always stood, under the whole totality and at no point within it, the reason there is an everything to be complete.

## VIII. The Simple and the Composite

Two results of the tradition now seal what the idol-pattern has exposed, and the first concerns composition. Here a careful opponent will object before the argument begins, and meeting the objection only strengthens it. Grant, he says, that everything names all beings; it does not follow that there is a further being, one enormous object composed of them all. Everything may be merely a domain of quantification — the complete inventory of what is — and not itself an item in the inventory, as the class of all men is not itself a man. The reply is a dilemma, and both horns are fatal to his thesis. If everything is merely the complete inventory of beings, then the inventory is not another being at all, and a fortiori not the kind of being that could be God; the inventory supplies no further being that could bear the name God. If instead everything is treated as a real whole composed of the beings it contains — one totality that genuinely is, the All as a thing and not a manner of speaking — then it is the most composite of all things: composed of every being there is and of every relation among them, the whole with the most parts, than which nothing is more assembled. Either the All is no being, and cannot be God; or the All is the maximal composite, and for that reason cannot be God. The pantheist needs it to be a being, so that his is has a subject; and the moment it is a being, it is a composite one.

And the God of the classical tradition is simple. This is not a pious flourish but a conclusion, and Aquinas treats it near the beginning of the Summa because the simplicity of God governs much of what follows in his account of the divine attributes. That which is first, the Non-Derivative, cannot be composed, for whatever is composed depends on its parts and on whatever put the parts together, and so is derivative by the very fact of composition; a composite is posterior to its components, and the First is posterior to nothing. God is therefore not a body, not a thing with this part here and that part there; not a compound of matter and form; not even a compound of essence and existence, for in Him alone what He is and that He is are not two. He is not in a genus, not a member of a kind alongside others of the kind. He is ipsum esse subsistens, being itself subsisting, not a being that has being but Being not received into anything that would limit it.

Set the two side by side and the pantheist's identification becomes the sharpest category mistake it is possible to make. He proposes to identify the maximal composite with the pure simple — the most assembled of all things with the one that has no parts to assemble. It is not a near miss or an overreach by a degree. It is the conjunction of a thing with its exact contrary. And Aquinas marked the error by name in the article set at the head of this essay, when he asked whether God enters into the composition of other things and answered against those who had said so — against David of Dinant, who identified God with prime matter, the least formed and most potential of all things, which is the ancestor of every doctrine that makes God the stuff or the sum of the world. Neque est possibile Deum aliquo modo in compositionem alicuius venire, nec sicut principium formale, nec sicut principium materiale: it is not possible for God in any way to enter into the composition of anything, neither as a formal nor as a material principle; unde Deus non potest esse pars alicuius compositi — whence God cannot be a part of any composite. And therefore a fortiori He is not the composition itself, not the total assembly, not the All. The simple is not the sum of the composite. Eternity is not reached by adding up enough time; divine infinity is not reached by aggregating finite beings; and God is not reached by adding up enough world.

<br>

## IX. A Sum of the Received Is Received

The second result is the cut the sequence forged for the cases of law and of time, in [*Does the Law Explain Itself?*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophy/metaphysics/philosophy-of-science/2026/09/18/does-the-law-explain-itself-en.html) and the beginningless essay, and it transfers without alteration to the aggregative pantheism of Section V — the All understood as the sum of beings. It does not yet reach the refined monist, and the reach must be earned rather than claimed; but the aggregative case must be closed first, and it closes cleanly.

Every member of the totality wears the mark of the received. This is the governing observation of the whole sequence: each thing in the world is such that its essence does not include its existence — it is the kind of thing that might not have been, and is, and so holds its being rather than being it. That is what it is to be a creature, and the essays have traced the mark at every level: in the contingency of the physical laws, which are determinate structures that do not confer themselves; in the dependence of the Now, which has no thickness and no reserves and is held open rather than self-standing; in the duration of the series, no inch of which grounds the inch before it. Now apply the governing sentence of the beginningless essay, which was built for exactly this and needs not a word changed: a sum of things each of which is derivative is not thereby a thing that is non-derivative. Adding together the received does not produce the non-received. It produces a larger quantity of the received. The totality of the contingent is the contingent in its totality; the sum does not launder the marks of its members, and there is no term in the sum — not one — at which the mark is absent and from which the whole might inherit aseity.

This defeats the All conceived as a heap, and it would be a comfort to stop here. But the most serious form of the monist's position is not a heap, and the sequence does not answer softened adversaries. Spinoza does not hold that God is assembled out of the modes; his order runs the other way. There is one infinite substance, that which is in itself and is conceived through itself; the modes are in the substance and cannot be conceived without it; and the substance is in no sense the resulting sum of them. More than this, his system is thoroughly necessitarian: in Nature, he writes, there is nothing contingent, and all things follow from the necessity of the divine nature. So the knowledgeable Spinozist can answer the preceding paragraph in a single sentence, and he is owed the hearing: you have imported the Thomistic contingency of creatures into my modes and then used it to refute my substance. He is right that this would be a cheat. Every mode might have been otherwise is precisely what he denies, and an argument that assumes it has not met him — it has met an aggregative pantheist wearing his name. Section V's equivocation is dispatched by the cut; Spinoza is not, and saying otherwise would only disguise the place where the real disagreement lives.

The argument from aggregation has therefore reached its limit, and the limit tells us where to look. Against the All as a sum, a sum of the received is received, and the matter is closed. Against Spinoza, the question must change, because he grants the very thing that argument leveraged — that finite things are dependent, that they do not stand of themselves. The disagreement is no longer whether dependent members become independent by being added together. It is what their dependence is. For Spinoza, to depend is to inhere: the finite thing is a mode of the one substance, a way the substance is, with no being that is not the substance's being under a modification. For the doctrine of creation, to depend is to be caused: the finite thing is from the Ground, receiving an act of being of its own, and is no mode of the Ground and no modification of its substance. These are two readings of the single word dependence, and everything now turns on which one dependence actually requires. That is no longer a question about totality, and nothing said about sums can settle it. It is the question the next section was written to answer; and it is there, not here, that the confrontation with Spinoza is really joined.

<br>

## X. Begotten, Not Made — From Another Without Being Of It

The question Section IX handed over is the deepest in the dispute, and the creed had already preserved the distinction in three words before Spinoza was born. Does dependence mean inherence — being a mode of that on which one depends — or can it mean something else, being from another without being a part or a modification of it? The Spinozist monist does not admit the second option: for him every from collapses into an of, every dependence into an inherence, so that to be from God is to be in God as a wave is in the sea. This, and not the arithmetic of sums, is the true fault line between Spinozist immanence and the doctrine of creation. *Begotten, Not Made* addressed one face of it in the generation of the Son; here is its general form, and it is the hinge on which the whole essay turns.

There are, the tradition holds, at least two ways of being from another that are not the way of being a part of it — and two are all the present argument requires, for a single instance would already break the pantheist's inference, and two give the contrast its force. The first is generation: the Son is from the Father, begotten not made, receiving the whole divine essence from the Father and yet not a portion of the Father, not a region carved out of Him, not an item added alongside Him — consubstantial, wholly from and in no way of as a part is of a whole. The second is creation: the creature is from God, made not begotten, receiving its whole being from God and yet other than God by the entire distance between the caused and the uncaused, not a fragment of the divine substance nor a mode of it but a reality given existence out of nothing. These are not the same mode of procession, and the analogy must be held under control: eternal generation is consubstantial, the Son being of one being with the Father, whereas creation produces a being distinct from the divine substance. But both show the one thing the argument needs — that from does not by itself mean a part of. In neither case does from mean of. In neither case is the one who is from a part of the one he is from. And pantheism is precisely the collapse of this distinction — the inference that if the creature is from God and wholly dependent on God, then the creature must be in God as a part in a whole, a fold in the one substance. It is the refusal to allow a from that is not an of.

But the corpus has already shown, in *The Ground That Knows*, that the refusal gets the dependence exactly backwards. There it was argued that what is wholly dependent is wholly other — that total dependence and total otherness are not in tension but are the same fact, because what possesses no being of its own is not its own ground, and so is other than its ground by the whole distance of the caused from the uncaused, precisely while keeping nothing back from it. The creature is not a fold in God. It is a dependent actuality, other than God, with no being except the being it is given. The pantheist, hearing wholly dependent, concludes a part of, or a mode of; the corpus, hearing wholly dependent, concludes wholly other — and the corpus is right, because a part shares the being of its whole and a mode the being of its substance, whereas the creature shares no portion of the divine essence and is no mode of God, but receives from God a created act of being proper to itself. This is not to deny that the creature participates in being and in the divine likeness; the tradition of participation affirms exactly that. But to participate in being is not to be a piece of the Being participated, as to receive light is not to be a fragment of the sun: the participant has a reality of its own, given and dependent, precisely in not being a part of what it participates. Creation is being-from without being-of, and that is the exact relation both pantheism and Spinozist immanence cannot represent, because each has only one preposition for dependence — in — and it is the wrong one.

<br>

## XI. The Nearness He Was Reaching For

It would be a failure of the charity the sequence has tried to keep if the essay ended with the error refuted and the man who held it merely corrected. For there is a true thing in the pantheist's instinct, and it is worth more than the false thing is worth refuting, and the corpus can give it to him more fully than his own position ever could.

What drives the identification, underneath the arguments, is a refusal of distance. The pantheist will not have a God who is elsewhere — a remote architect who built the world and withdrew, an object beyond the farthest galaxy, a sovereign across an unbridgeable gap. He wants a God who is here, in the rain and the cell and the thought, nearer than breathing, with nothing of the world shut out from Him. And he is right to want it. The deist's God, the watchmaker who wound the world and left, is a smaller and colder thing than the truth, and the pantheist's revolt against him is a revolt in the right direction. The trouble is only that, reaching for the nearness, the pantheist grabbed the nearest tool for it, which was identity, and identity destroys the very thing he wanted. For if God is the world, then there is no one near the world; there is only the world being itself, and the intimacy he sought — of a presence that holds him — has nothing left to do the holding. Identity does not secure nearness. It abolishes the two terms between which nearness could obtain.

The corpus offers him the nearness without the abolition, and offers it more radically than he dared. *Where Is God?* and *The Corridor* located God not as an agent beside events but as the sustaining ground of the Now in which every event occurs — not far from the dying and the grieving but the very holding-open of the moment in which they suffer, the ground that does not withdraw. *The Ground That Knows* went further: because God holds the creature's Now in being with nothing of it exterior to the holding, there is nothing in the creature — no thought under the fig tree, no turning of the will in a room no one enters — that is outside Him. This is a nearer nearness than pantheism can purchase. The pantheist's God is near the way the sea is near the wave, which is to say there is no one there to be near; the creature is just a ripple in the divine stuff. The corpus's God is near the way the giver of being is near to what would not exist for an instant without the giving — present to each thing by essence, Aquinas says, as that in which it stands, closer to it than it is to itself, and yet other than it, so that there is a creature to be held and a Holder to hold it. In Him we live and move and have our being: not we are He, but in Him — and the in, which the pantheist dropped, is the whole of the nearness he was looking for. He traded it for an is, and the is cost him the God who could have been near.

<br>

## XII. A Formal Statement

Three sentences, offered as the definitions this essay exists to fix.

The word everything does two jobs: aggregatively it names the totality of beings, the complete sum of the derivative, and God is everything taken so is pantheism and is false; as grounding it means what Scripture means by from Him and through Him and to Him are all things and in Him all things hold together, namely that every being is from and in God, which is true and is the opposite of an identity, since it is told entirely by the preposition in and not by the copula is.

The universe is the single physical created order and is not God; and when it is felt too small and enlarged to the All, the totality of all that exists, the enlargement improves the identification on the axis of scope, on which it never failed, and not on the axis of kind, on which it always failed: if the All is merely the complete inventory, it supplies no further being that could be God; if it is treated as a real whole, it inherits composition, differentiation, and derivation from its members and becomes the maximal composite.

The idol of totality is the third of a family: after the spatial idol that seats the Ground as the largest region and the temporal idol that seats Him as the earliest instant comes the idol of the All, which seats Him as the most complete sum; all three maximize a feature of the figure and mistake the limit for the Ground, and the Ground is on no axis the figure has — so that the universe cannot become God by becoming large enough, and the All cannot become God by including enough, because a sum of the received is received, the simple is not the sum of the composite, and what is from God is not thereby of God.

<br>

## XIII. Coda

The two sentences came in together, and they can go out together, each kept where it can be kept and refused where it cannot. God is the universe is refused entire: the universe is the theater and not the playwright, the order and not its Ground, one physical whole among whatever wholes there are, wearing the mark of the made on every part of it. God is everything is refused in the sense the speaker meant it and granted in the sense he did not: God is not the sum of all things, and all things are from God and held in God, and the distance between those two readings is the distance between pantheism and the faith, measured by a single preposition.

The pantheist may keep his totality, as the skeptic of the beginningless essay was allowed to keep his endless past. Let there be everything; let nothing be left out; let the inventory be closed and complete and without remainder, matter and mind and number and law, the whole of what is. Not one item is struck from it by anything said here. What he may not do is call the inventory its own author, or imagine that by summing the dependent he has reached the independent, or that by making the subject of his sentence large enough he has at last made it divine. The All is everything except the one thing he wanted it to be. It is everything except its own reason. And the reason it cannot supply, the ground under the whole of it and at no point within it, present to each thing as that in which it stands and identical with none, is not the largest thing that is, nor the oldest, nor the sum of all that is — is not, in the end, one thing among things, but ipsum esse subsistens, being itself subsisting, that in whom all things, each of them from Him and none of them He, live and move and have their being:

**I AM WHO I AM.**
