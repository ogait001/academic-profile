## Note on Sources and Method

This essay works within the already established vocabulary of the Gaitan Topology: the Now as crossing, the Ground as the Non-Derived on which actuality depends, the Wall as the limit of creaturely access to the interior of another, and reception as the basic structure of created knowing. It takes those results as given rather than deriving them again.

The present question is narrower. Believing in God and having faith are often treated as if they named the same act. This essay argues that they do not. It asks what reason can establish, what only revelation can make known, and what occurs when a creature freely receives, by grace, the One whom reason can reach only as Ground. The method remains structural: when a single word hides several relations, the relations are separated until the act itself becomes visible.

Two limits of its ambition should be stated at once, because the argument depends on keeping them. First, the essay describes the structure of the act of faith and marks the boundary at which reason—having reached that there is a Ground—yields to revelation, in which the Ground reveals who He is; it does not deduce the content of that revelation—neither the Trinity, nor the Incarnation, nor salvation—from the metaphysics of grounding, for these things are given rather than demonstrated, and the essay indicates where the giving would begin instead of entering into it. Second, it does not treat faith as an achievement of the creature unaided: faith, the tradition holds, is itself first the gift and interior motion of grace, freely exercised by the one whom grace moves, and the exposition preserves that order at every moment. The witnesses—above all Augustine, Thomas Aquinas, James, the Letter to the Hebrews, and the Church's teaching on faith and grace—are companions on the way, not authorities from which the argument descends. It stands or falls by its own structural coherence.

<br>

## Abstract

Believing in God and having faith are ordinarily taken for one thing, and this essay argues that they are not. The tradition distinguished, within one act of believing, three relations and not three acts: credere Deum, assent that God is; credere Deo, trust in the First Truth on whose authority one believes; and credere in Deum, the movement of the whole person toward God as its end. English folds these into one phrase, which may name only bare assent that God exists, while living faith is the one act quickened in all three relations and formed by charity. The demons, James says, have the assent and it does not save them. Reason reaches Deus est — that there is a Ground — as a conclusion of demonstration, not as the act of faith; the topology establishes the that. For the structure yields a That and not the Who: who the Ground is, whether He has spoken, is not deduced from dependence but disclosed, if at all, by His own word — revelation being not the creature's breach of the Wall but the One behind it crossing freely from His side. Because the Ground is not an object among objects but the condition of the field, the demand for object-like evidence is a category mistake, though signs may make the reception of His word reasonable without compelling it; faith is therefore not weak knowledge but a relation of another order, and things not seen names the wayfarer's condition, not a bar to the vision of glory to which faith gives way. And the turning is free not self-grounded: God gives the habit of faith and inwardly moves the believer, who, so moved, truly and freely elicits the act — received habit, graced motion, free act. It is the recovery, in the register of the will, of the preposition the pantheist lost: to be in the Ground is ontological and unavoidable; to go into God is personal, graced, and free.

## I. The Demons' Belief

Begin where the Letter of James puts the knife, because it cuts the two things apart before the essay has to. You believe that God is one; you do well. Even the demons believe — and shudder. The sentence grants the belief and withholds the congratulation. It does not say the demons are mistaken, that their theology is thin, that they have the proposition slightly wrong. On the proposition at issue — that God is, and is one — their assent is not in doubt, and it does not save them. What it produces in them is a shudder.

Hold the limit case in view, because it governs the whole essay. The demon is the being of maximal assent and no living faith. Aquinas, asking directly whether the demons have faith, answers with a distinction the essay will need throughout: they have a belief wrung from them by the sheer evidence of signs they cannot gainsay, a belief of their natural acumen and not a gift of grace, and they lack entirely the faith that is praiseworthy — the faith that proceeds from a will moved toward the good and is formed by charity. Theirs is faith only in a deficient and compelled sense: assent extorted, will turned away. So one must not say the demons have zero belief; one must say they have belief without the life that ought to accompany it, and that this is precisely what does not save. If faith in the saving sense were the holding-true of the proposition that God is, the demons would be the most faithful beings in creation, and James would have praised them instead of naming them the counterexample. He names them the counterexample. Whatever living faith is, then, it is not the assent that God exists, however firm — for here is the firmest such assent there is, in beings with no charity at all.

This is the shape the sequence met in the essays on the Ground. There it was found that one does not reach God by maximizing a magnitude of the figure — not the largest region, not the earliest instant, not the most complete totality — because the Ground lies on no axis the figure has. Here the epistemic twin appears: one does not reach living faith by maximizing the firmness of a belief, because faith lies on no axis that assent has. The demon has run the axis of assent to its limit and stands as far from living faith as a creature can. The question Believe in God and have faith? assumes the two are one point further along a single line. James has already shown they are not on one line at all.

<br>

## II. Three Relations Within One Believing

As so often in this topology, the confusion lives in a word doing more than one job, and the tradition had pried the jobs apart before the modern ear fused them. The word is believe, and within the single act of believing Aquinas distinguishes three relations to its object — and he is careful to say that these are not three acts but one act regarded in three ways. Credere Deum: faith according to what is believed, its material object — the truths believed concerning God insofar as they are referred to Him; and, in the case this essay isolates, the assent that God is. Credere Deo: to believe God — to assent on the authority of the First Truth to which the believer adheres, the formal reason by which anything is believed at all. And Credere in Deum: to believe into God — the First Truth taken now as the will's end, the whole person moved toward God as toward its good.

One act, three relations: the one believing stands to its object as content held true, as witness trusted, and as end gone toward. The relations are distinguishable without being separable — and yet in the order of a creature's life they can fall grievously out of step, and the falling-out is the subject of this essay. A man may hold that God exists and trust nothing and love nothing; the relation to the content stands and the relation to the end is dead. That dead condition — assent without the movement toward God as end — is what the tradition calls unformed or lifeless faith, faith not yet, or no longer, formed by charity. It is analogous to the demon's condition without being identical to it: lifeless faith in a human being remains an infused gift, though deprived of charity's form, whereas the demons' compelled belief is not the supernatural gift of faith at all but an assent forced upon them by signs they cannot gainsay. So what ordinary English calls believing in God can name either the whole living act, in which the person is borne toward God, or merely the standing of the first relation, bare assent that God is. Faith in the full and saving sense is the living act, in which credere in Deum is alive; and the burden of what follows is that this is not the first relation with its confidence increased. It is the one act turned, in all its relations, toward God as end — and, as the later sections must add, turned there not by the creature's own resources but by a grace freely received and freely enacted.

## III. What Reason Reaches

It must be said plainly, because the sequence has spent many essays earning it, that reason reaches God. [*Does the Law Explain Itself?*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophy/metaphysics/philosophy-of-science/2026/09/18/does-the-law-explain-itself-en.html), [*What Sustains the Now?*](https://oscargaitan.org/metaphysics/philosophical-theology/philosophy-of-god/2026/09/18/what-sustains-the-now-en.html), and [*Is the Beginningless Grounded?*](https://oscargaitan.org/metaphysics/philosophy-of-time/divine-eternity/2026/09/25/is-the-beginningless-grounded-en.html) did not offer faith in the place of argument; they argued, and what they reached they claimed as known: that the physical order does not ground its own actuality, that the Now is held rather than self-standing, that no length of duration confers aseity, and so that there is a Non-Derivative on which the whole depends. But the name for what reason reaches must be exact, or the essay slips at its first step. Reason reaches Deus est — that God is. It reaches it as the conclusion of a demonstration, on no one's testimony, by following dependence to its terminus. It does not thereby reach credere Deum as an act of faith.

For the tradition draws a line the essay must honor. The same truth that one person holds by demonstration another may hold by faith, and no one holds the same truth, in the same respect, by both at once. That God is can stand at the end of an argument or be received on the word of the One who reveals; in the first it is science, in the second it is faith, and they are not the same act even when the proposition is the same. The grounding essays perform the first. They establish the that. They do not, and cannot, perform the act of faith, because the act of faith is not the having of a conclusion.

And look carefully at what the demonstration hands the mind at its end, because the handing is where everything turns. It does not hand over one more object, a very large and very old one, to be set beside the others in the inventory of things known to exist. The sequence was emphatic: the Ground is reached by leaving the series, not by arriving at its biggest member; He is on no axis the figure has. So the terminus of the proof is not an object the mind now possesses as it possesses its other conclusions. It is the affirmation that there is that on which every object, and the mind affirming it, all depend. This is the intellectual arrival at the Wall — the barrier [*No One Under the Fig Tree*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophy%20of%20mind/philosophical%20anthropology/metaphysics/philosophy%20of%20technology/2026/07/26/no-one-under-the-fig-tree.html) named, past which only the externalized crosses, and which in the knowledge of God takes its purest form, since the Ground offers no externalization that natural inquiry can extract, inspect, and carry across the Wall as it does with created objects, and is no object standing over against the knower. Reason reaches the certainty that the Ground is and finds that what it has reached is not available to be handled as an object is handled. Deus est is genuine knowledge, and it is not faith, and the distance between them is not a distance of certainty still to be closed by a better argument. It is the distance between a conclusion that the Ground is and the reception of the Ground as One who speaks, and the turning toward Him as end — and no addition of argument crosses it, because argument yields conclusions and the things not yet done are not conclusions.

## IV. The That and the Who

Here is the hinge of the essay, and the place where the corpus reaches its own frontier. The structure the sequence has built yields a That; it does not yield the Who. By reason the creature can reach that there is a Ground, a Non-Derivative on which all depends, present to each thing as that in which it stands. What the creature cannot reach by the same road is who that Ground is — whether He is silent or has spoken, whether He wills the creature's good and has done anything about it, what, if anything, He asks. These are not longer inferences awaiting a sharper argument. They are not the kind of thing an argument from dependence can deliver at all, because such an argument delivers the conditions of the field, and these belong to the free interior of the One on whom the field depends — and the interior of a will, this corpus has held since the fig tree, is not read off from outside, not even a creature's, and least of all the Ground's.

So between the That and the Who there is not a gap of information but a change of mode. The Who is not deduced. He speaks — or He does not; and if the creature is to know who the Ground is, the Ground must disclose Himself, must cross from His own interior into the creature's reception by an act of His own that no argument could compel or supply. He must, in a word, reveal. And this is exactly where the middle relation of Section II, credere Deo, which has waited in the wings, takes the stage. Credere Deum was the that, which reason can reach and faith can also hold. Credere Deo is the receiving of the Ground's own word — the assent given not because the creature has demonstrated the content but because it is the First Truth who discloses it, and the First Truth can neither deceive nor be deceived. Faith in its proper and supernatural sense begins here: not at the conclusion that a Ground is, but at the reception of the Ground as the One who reveals, on His own authority, what no demonstration reaches. And this is why revelation does not contradict the Wall but presupposes it: revelation does not abolish the Wall by letting the creature penetrate the divine interior and seize the Who; it is the Ground Himself freely crossing the Wall from His own side. The Wall forbids intrusion; it does not forbid disclosure. The creature cannot cross inward and take the Who; the Who may cross outward and speak.

This marks the boundary the method note promised to keep. The topology reaches the That and halts. It does not deduce the Who — not the Trinity, not the Incarnation, not the self-gift of the Ground or the creature's salvation — from the metaphysics of grounding, and it would be a grave overreach to pretend it could; a metaphysics claiming to deduce the inner life and free decisions of God from the structure of dependence would have committed the very idolatry the sequence spent its essays refusing, the Ground made a term legible within the figure. What the topology can do is locate the frontier exactly: show where reason arrives, show that what it arrives at is a Ground and not an answer to the question who, and show that the question who can be answered only if the Ground elects to answer it. Revelation is that answer, if it is given; faith is the creature's reception of it. The structure yields a That; it does not yield the Who. The Who is not deduced. He speaks — and the receiving of His word is already past the frontier this corpus, by itself, can reach.

## V. Not an Object Among Objects

Why can the mind not hold the Ground as it holds its other conclusions? Because to believe, in the ordinary way, that something exists is to place it within the field of objects and weigh what speaks for it. I believe there is a planet I have not seen because the orbits of the ones I have seen require it; I have set a new object into the field and its witnesses point to its place. Every ordinary existence-belief has this shape. The belief that God is cannot have it, and the sequence has shown why: the Ground is not an item within the field but the condition of there being a field with items in it. To treat the proposition God is as the positing of one more object, even the largest, is to commit in the register of knowledge the error the pantheism essay refused in the register of being — to seat the Ground within the figure as its maximal member, the master-clock of the mind.

A distinction must be marked here with care, because the corpus cites it and an informed reader will press it. To say the Ground is not an object among objects is not to say that He can never, in any mode, be present to a created mind. The tradition teaches that in glory the blessed are given to see the divine essence itself, the created intellect raised by a light not its own to a vision that is not the handling of one object among others but the immediate presence of God to the mind. That vision is the consummation toward which faith tends, not its contradiction, and this essay neither denies nor annexes it. The claim here is narrower and exact: in this life, by its natural powers, the creature does not meet God as one object within the field of created objects, and cannot, because He is the condition of the field and not a tenant of it. He is not an object among objects, nor an item within the created evidential field. That is all the argument needs, and it leaves the vision of glory untouched.

So the assent that God is, even held as knowledge, is already unlike the mind's other assents and already straining past the shape of object-knowledge. It is not the addition of an item to the inventory but the affirmation that the whole inventory, and the affirming of it, are received. It is assent to the condition of all one's other assents, and an assent of that kind cannot rest where ordinary existence-beliefs rest — in the satisfaction of having placed an object correctly — because it has placed no object. It has acknowledged a dependence. The conclusion, honestly held, points past itself toward a relation it cannot itself be — and that pointing-past is the threshold of faith.

## VI. Faith Is Not Weak Knowledge

The modern ear hears faith and supplies a definition before the sentence is finished: belief held on insufficient evidence, assent in excess of what the facts warrant. On this picture faith is belief-that overclocked — a probability-judgment about an object inflated past its warrant. Clifford made it a sin: it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence. The caricature is so settled that the believer himself often accepts its terms and hunts more evidence for the object, as though faith were a bet to be justified by lengthening the odds.

But the whole picture assumes what the previous sections denied: that the Ground is an object in the field, about which evidence could be sufficient or insufficient. The demand for object-like empirical evidence of the Ground — produce Him, or the trace He leaves, as one more item among items — is a category mistake, and not because the case is weak but because evidence of that kind is a relation among items in the field and the Ground is not such an item. One cannot photograph the light by which every photograph is taken.

But the mistake must be named precisely, or the essay falls into the opposite error and makes faith a leap in the dark. To deny that God is an object for empirical demonstration is not to deny that the assent of faith is reasonable, or that anything speaks for it. The tradition has held that there are reasons and signs that render the act of faith reasonable without converting revelation into a demonstration — among them the intelligibility of creation the grounding essays themselves traced, the testimony surrounding revelation and its endurance and fruit across history, and the classical motives of credibility — none of which, of themselves, produce the faith they make reasonable. Reasons can establish credibility; they cannot manufacture the thing itself. So the answer to Clifford is not that faith scorns evidence but that it is not, and could not be, assent to an object weighed as objects are weighed — while remaining, for all that, the reasonable reception of a word, supported by signs and compelled by none.

This is the sense of the old definition the modern ear mishears. Faith is the conviction of things not seen. The things are not unseen because they hide behind a screen a sharper instrument might lift; they are not seen because, in this life, the Ground is not given as an object of sight among the sighted — the Wall again, which marks the limit of the wayfarer's natural knowing. It is not an eternal bar: faith is the knowing proper to the one on the way, and in glory it gives way to a vision that is not object-sight either but the immediate presence of God. Faith is therefore not knowledge with the lights turned low, a dim version of an object-sight that clearer heads enjoy; there is no object-sight of God to be the bright version of. It is the relation proper to a knower whose Ground is, in this life, not naturally given to him as an object of direct vision.

## VII. The Turning at the Crossing, and the Grace That Gives It

[*Where Is God?*](https://oscargaitan.org/catholic-theology/philosophy-of-time/ontology/2026/05/05/where-is-god.html) located the Now as the crossing where the will faces one way or another. Faith, in the act proper to it, is a movement at that crossing: the turning of the whole person toward the Ground as its end — credere in Deum, the First Truth taken as the good toward which one goes. It is enacted, when it is enacted, at the crossing, now; it is an act and not merely a state.

Two things follow. First, faith is not the product of proof, because proof delivers conclusions and the turning is not a conclusion — the demon has the conclusion, wrung from him entire, and does not turn. The gap is not between a weaker and a stronger argument; it is between the intellect's assent and the will's movement toward the good, and the second is not contained in the first. Proof may bring the intellect to a conclusion; it cannot, of itself, produce the will's self-entrustment. This is the precise lesson of the demon, and it must be stated precisely, because the corpus will not overclaim: evident signs can compel the intellect — the demon's assent is compelled — without moving the will to love; conviction and surrender are two, and the second does not follow from the first by any necessity. That is why one can be argued to Deus est and cannot be argued into credere in Deum.

It is tempting to go further and say that God remains unseen in order to leave the will free — that if He were plainly given, the turning would be compelled and love made impossible. The essay declines that step, because it proves too much and conflicts with what the tradition holds of glory: the blessed see God and love Him freely and fully, so vision plainly does not abolish the free love of God. The unavailability of God as an object of sight in this life is the limit of the wayfarer's natural knowing, not a device installed to keep choice open. What secures the freedom of faith is not that God is hidden but that the will's movement toward its end is never entailed by the intellect's grasp of a truth — that assent to what is does not necessitate love of the good, as the demon, assenting without loving, eternally shows.

And here the account must add what it has so far left unsaid, or it will describe a creature turning by its own resources, which the tradition flatly denies and the metaphysics of this corpus should be the first to deny. The turning of faith is free, and it is not the creature's unaided achievement. Both hold at once. Faith is a genuinely free act of the person, and it is possible only because God gives the supernatural habit of faith and inwardly moves the believer to assent — faith, as regards its assent, is from God moving man inwardly by grace, and the beginning of faith is not from free will alone. This is no second agent crossing in the creature's place. Grace does not perform the turning instead of the creature; nor does the creature generate from itself the power to turn. Grace gives the power and moves it, and the creature, so moved, truly turns — the act received without ceasing to be its own. The metaphysics of reception the corpus has built is made for exactly this. Existence is received without the creature's consent — no one is asked whether to be created. Faith is received too, as grace — an infused habit and the inward motion to its act — and so it is received and then, by the one so moved, truly and freely elicited as consent. That threefold truth — received habit, graced motion, free act — is the thing this essay adds to the sequence, and it is only the law of the whole corpus, that the creature has nothing it was not given, carried into the one act where what is given is the power freely to say yes.

## VIII. The Preposition Recovered

[*Is God the Universe, or Is God Everything?*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophy/metaphysics/philosophical-theology/2026/10/01/is-god-the-universe-or-everything-en.html) ended at a sentence the present essay turns over to its other face: the whole error is the loss of the preposition. The pantheist said God is everything when the truth was that everything is from God and in God; he had dropped the in and the from and kept only the is, and in dropping them he had lost the Creator and kept an idol. Faith is the recovery of the preposition — the same word, carried now from metaphysics into the register of the will, and spoken not as a fact conceded but as a motion made and given to be made. Credere in Deum. To believe into God.

Augustine drew the whole of it into one line. What is it to believe in Him? By believing, to love; by believing, to go into Him, and to be incorporated into His members. Believing in Him is not, for Augustine, a firmer grade of believing that He is. It is credendo in eum ire — by believing, to go into Him: a verb of motion, a direction taken, a self borne toward and into. The in of credere in Deum is the very in of the sentence the corpus has leaned on since [*The Corridor*](https://oscargaitan.org/catholic-theology/philosophy-of-time/ontology/2026/05/28/the-corridor.html) — in Him we live and move and have our being. But there it named a fact that holds whether or not the creature consents: the creature stands in the Ground as the sustained stands in what sustains it, and cannot do otherwise, and the demon no less than the saint lives and moves and has his being in God. Faith is not the knowledge of that fact. In the topology, credere in Deum appears as the free, graced inhabiting of it — the creature's own turning-into the One in whom it already, and helplessly, is. Belief-that grants the standing-in; faith goes in.

This is the exact line between the bare relation credere Deum and the living credere in Deum, and it is not a line of information. The demon cannot escape the fact that he depends upon God — he knows the Ground and cannot deny it — and will not go into God. He lacks no certainty about the point at issue and makes no motion of love. The believer may have fewer of the facts, held less firmly, and makes the motion. Faith adds no proposition to what the demon knows. It adds the turning the demon withholds — the turning that is, in the believer, not a feat of his own but the grace he has received taking effect as his own free act. So the answer to the title begins to show itself: to believe in God can mean only to grant, as the demon grants, that there is a Ground one stands in; or it can mean, as the saint means, to go freely into the Ground one stands in. The first is not yet faith. The second is faith alive. One preposition, spoken two ways — as a fact about where one is, or as a motion of where, by grace, one is going.

## IX. Faith and the Reception

Set faith now within the epistemology the sequence has built, because it falls into place there exactly. Creatures know by reception: they stand as the interior to which a world arrives from beyond, and can carry inward only what a thing releases across the Wall. The Ground offers no such content for natural inquiry to extract and is no such object; He is known, [*The Ground That Knows*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophical%20theology/metaphysics/ontology/2026/07/27/the-ground-that-knows.html) argued, not as an object received but as that in whose light receiving happens at all. The Psalm fixed it: In Your light we shall see light. Not by it, as by a lamp the eye then uses on its own. In it — the seeing itself occurring within a light that is not the seer's and is not, in this life, set before the seer as a seen thing.

Faith is the relation proper to a knower so placed. It is reception consented to: the creature cannot take the Ground in as it takes in objects, but it can accept that its own being, its own light, its very standing at the crossing are received and not owned, and freely rest in the One who gives them. That acceptance is not a seeing of the Ground; it is a seeing-in, a consent to see and to be within a light that is not one's own. And here a distinction the earlier sections prepared must be kept, lest the praise of faith overreach. Faith is not an inferior version of ordinary object-sight — there is no ordinary creaturely seeing of God, analogous to the inspection of created objects, for faith to be the dim copy of. But neither is faith superior to the sight of God simply; it is not the highest knowing of God there is. The tradition is clear that faith is the knowing of the wayfarer and gives way, in glory, to the vision of the divine essence, which exceeds it. Faith does not become that sight; it yields to it when its end is attained, for the obscurity of the unseen belongs to faith's very nature, and vision voids it. What faith is superior to is not that vision but the mode of object-knowledge the modern demand mistakes it for. Set beside the inspection of an object held at the distance every seen thing keeps, faith is not poorer but nearer: it does not hold God over against itself across the gap vision needs, but rests within the One in whom it already stands. That nearness is not the rival of the vision of glory; it is its seed. The vision does not abolish the communion toward which faith tends but perfects that communion, replacing the resting-in-shadow proper to faith with the seeing-in-light that was always its end. This is the nearness the pantheism essay promised and would not let identity counterfeit: not we are He, and not merely that we are in Him as a bare fact, but that we rest, freely and by grace, in Him.

## X. Why Belief Alone Shudders

Return now to the demons, with the apparatus in hand, and to a distinction [*God, the Universe, and the Now*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophy-of-time/catholic-theology/ontology/2026/06/07/god-the-universe-and-the-now.html) drew between two refusals. The demon has the assent relevant to credere Deum without the movement proper to credere in Deum; his assent on the point at issue is firm and compelled, and the movement of charity toward God is wholly absent. He is the limit case not of belief without any faith whatever — the tradition grants him a kind of faith, the unformed and compelled belief of one who cannot deny the signs — but of assent without charity — not human lifeless faith simply, which is still an infused gift, but its dark analogue in a will wholly turned away. His condition names, in its purest form, what that essay called the non te egeo set beside the older non serviam. The demon's difficulty is not non est Deus — he of all beings cannot say it. It is non serviam, and beneath it the colder non te egeo: the refusal not of the fact but of the end, not of the proposition but of the going-in. His assent on the point at issue is firm and his will is turned away, and the shudder is what knowledge of the Ground becomes when the will will not go toward it — belief held and the end refused, the preposition known and not entered.

This exposes the deep miscarriage in the evidential framing of unbelief. The honest unbeliever often locates his difficulty in the evidence: were the argument stronger, were the case for the object clearer, he would believe. But the demon has the strongest possible case — he does not infer the Ground, he cannot evade it — and the strength of the case has not produced in him a grain of charity. The thing living faith is was never waiting on the strength of a case. One can be driven to Deus est by argument, as the sequence's own essays drive the mind. No argument can drive the will into credere in Deum, because the movement is not the terminus of a proof but a free turning of the will moved by grace. The man who says he would have faith if only the evidence were better has mistaken which act is in question. Better evidence could perfect his grasp of the that. It could not perform his going-in, which only he can do, only freely, and only by a grace that argument neither contains nor supplies.

And the inverse image completes the picture, though it is not a perfect mirror, and the Gospel hands it to us to pray. A father brings his afflicted son, is asked whether he believes, and answers: I believe; help my unbelief. His cry is not the demon turned exactly around, for it is itself a mixture — belief and its incompleteness, trust and need, confession and petition, all at once. But set against the demon it shows where faith lives. The demon has certainty and no surrender; the father has an assent he himself calls halting, joined to a real turning — he has come, he has brought his child, he has rested his need on Christ. He is doing credere in Deum with a credere Deum he confesses weak, and the Gospel treats his faith as real, indeed as the faith it asks. Which settles the question the essay has pressed: faith does not live on the axis of how much is assented but on the axis of the turning. A flawless assent without the turning is the shudder. A halting assent with a real turning is faith — and the turning itself, the father all but says, is not his own doing, for in the same breath he asks help even to believe, and the asking is already the grace at work.

<br>

## XI. A Formal Statement

Three sentences, offered as the definitions this essay exists to fix.

The tradition distinguished, within the single act of believing, three relations and not three acts: credere Deum, according to what is believed, its material object (in the case this essay isolates, that God is); credere Deo, according to the First Truth on whose authority one believes; and credere in Deum, according to God as the end toward whom the believer is moved — so that what English flattens into believe in God may name only the first relation, bare assent that God is, while living faith is the one act alive in all three, quickened and formed by charity, which is a distinct virtue and not faith renamed.

Reason reaches Deus est, that there is a Ground, as a conclusion of demonstration and not as the act of faith; the structure yields a That and not the Who, for who the Ground is — whether He has spoken, and what He asks — is not deduced from dependence but disclosed, if at all, by the Ground's own word, and faith in its proper sense begins there, in the reception of the revealing God as First Truth, past the frontier the topology can reach but not cross.

The Ground is not an object among objects nor an item within the created evidential field, so the demand for object-like evidence is a category mistake — though signs may make the reception of His word reasonable without compelling it — and faith is accordingly not weak knowledge but a relation of another order: the free turning of the dependent, at the crossing it does not sustain, toward the Ground in whom it already stands; a turning that grace first gives and inwardly moves and the creature then truly makes, received without ceasing to be its own, and the recovery in the register of the will of the preposition the pantheist lost — not we are He, and not merely that we are in Him as a bare fact, but, graced and free, credendo in eum ire, that by believing we go into Him.

<br>

## XII. Coda

The title asked whether to believe in God and to have faith are one thing, and the answer is that believe in carries two freights, and only one of them is faith. To grant that a Ground exists — to run the axis of assent even to the demon's compelled certainty — is not yet faith; the Letter of James will not let us pretend otherwise. To have faith is to receive the Ground, who by reason is only a That, as the One who has spoken and disclosed the Who — to believe His word because He is First Truth, and to go toward Him as end. The arguments of this corpus can carry a mind to the frontier and set it down before the certainty that the Ground is. They cannot carry it across, and not because they are defective, but because across the frontier is not a further conclusion but a Person who must speak and be received — and the receiving is the creature's own free act, an act, the essay has been at pains to add, that the creature makes only because grace gives and moves the power to make it.

Francis, whose memorial this is, left the world almost no arguments and the whole of the turning. He is not remembered for having established that God is; he is remembered for having received the One who had spoken and gone toward Him with the whole weight of a life, freely, and by a grace he was the first to say he had not earned. That is credere in Deum, and it is what the demons, for all they know, will not do, and what the father of the afflicted boy, for all he does not know, did. Faith is not the mind's last conclusion. It is the person's first step — credendo in eum ire, by believing, to go into Him — taken at the crossing one does not hold, toward the One in whom one already, and whether one wills it or not, lives and moves and has one's being, and by a power that same One gives for the taking of it. The demon knows the sentence and will not move. The father, knowing less and holding it less firmly, moves — and knows even the moving is not all his own, which is why his faith and his prayer are the same words. Faith has only ever had one language, and it is a prayer, and it is the prayer of the one who has the turning and begs for the rest of the assent, and for the grace to make even the begging:

> ***Credo, Domine; adiuva incredulitatem meam***  
>
> ***I believe; help my unbelief***
