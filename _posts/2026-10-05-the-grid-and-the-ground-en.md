## Note on Sources and Method

This essay works within the vocabulary the sequence has already fixed and recalls its results rather than re-deriving them. It presupposes the distinction between the Ground and every member of the figure; the refusal of God as the largest object, earliest cause, totality, engineer, or carver standing above the stage; the collapse of reflective distance produced by the manufactured crowd; the distinction between the That reached by reason and the Who disclosed in revelation; and the account of constitutive dependence as having no how much. These results are used here as established coordinates. The earlier essays in which they were developed are listed in the references.

The occasion is ordinary and of the age: a grid of panels, forwarded without comment, in which a cartoon God delivers one self-incriminating line after another — that He took three days over the earth and an afternoon over the galaxies, that He hates everyone His followers hate, that He sacrificed Himself to Himself, that He thinks bats are birds. The panel is reconstructed here from the reader's own transcription, and where a line arrived garbled it has been rendered in the form the genre makes unmistakable.

The method is the one the sequence keeps throughout. The strongest form of each jab is stated before it is met; the true intuition buried inside it is named and kept; and a word doing two jobs is pried apart whenever the whole force of the jab turns out to live in the crossing of the wires. One limit of ambition should be stated at once. This is apologetics at the frontier, not a deduction of the Who. It clears a caricature; it does not pretend that clearing the caricature is the same as the turning the last essay called credere in Deum.

## Abstract

A meme-panel is advanced against God, and it is answered here not line by line as a list of errors but as a single genre with a single structure. The genre is the one *The Circus and the Cage* described: a device engineered to be received rather than reasoned, to foreclose the reflective distance at which its claims would have to be weighed. And beneath the many jabs lies one error, the error of the whole idol-family: nearly every panel mocks a God who is an object within the figure — the largest carver, the oldest engineer, the tribal chief, the clumsy designer, the vindictive sovereign — and mistakes the refutation of that object for the refutation of the Ground. The atheist of the panel and the pantheist of the earlier essay turn out to worship and to mock the same God, the God-object, and neither has reached the One on no axis the figure has. The essay then takes the panels in their natural groups — the carver above the stage, the idols measured by a magnitude, the projected tribal god, the clumsy engineer, the letter mistaken for a science text, and the punishment measured as a quantity — and shows in each that the jab lands cleanly on the idol and never touches the Ground, that several jabs trade on a single equivocation, and that the moral force some of them carry invokes an objective "ought" whose grounding the panel itself leaves unexamined. It closes where *The Circus and the Cage* closed: to answer a meme is first to slow it down, to stand for a moment on the floor the feed denies exists, and to discover that it holds.

## I. The Panel That Was Received

*The Circus and the Cage* began with a broadcast that was believed. On an evening in 1938 a voice in the cadence of a news bulletin wrote a Martian invasion onto the nervous system of a nation, and what frightened the country afterward was not the Martians but the discovery that the form of truth, borrowed for an hour, could bypass judgment entirely. The listeners did not reason their way into terror; they received it. And the susceptibility Welles exposed no longer needs a Welles to operate it: it now runs continuously, in every pocket.

The meme-panel is that mechanism refined to its smallest unit. It is the broadcast compressed until nothing remains but the form — the authoritative cadence, the air of a case already closed — with the argument boiled off. A panel does not make a claim and offer its grounds; it stages a verdict and invites the nod that the staging has already extorted. Infinite punishment for finite crime? sounds legit. The two words that carry the whole are sounds legit, and they do not argue; they perform the sneer that marks the matter settled, so that to pause over it feels like siding with the absurdity the panel has already convicted. Trolololol is not an afterthought appended to the fossil record and the light of distant stars; it is the actual content of that panel, the laugh that does the work an argument would otherwise have to do. The form is the message. The grid is a channel that borrows the look of self-evidence and calls the borrowing reason.

Seen this way, the panel is a specimen of exactly what the earlier essay named: the collapse of reflective distance, the closing of the gap between stimulus and response until a reader no longer judges but only reacts. It manufactures a crowd in the precise sense defined there — an aggregate routed into the same contempt at the same hour, feeling as one, answerable for nothing — and it does so under the banner of the one virtue it most conspicuously lacks, which is thought. The reader forwards it because it felt like reason, and the feeling was the whole transaction. This is why the panels cannot simply be rebutted fact by fact and left there, as though the trouble were a column of mistaken claims. The trouble is a form that works by preventing the very act — the return to the crossing, the standing still long enough to weigh — that any rebuttal requires. To answer a meme, then, is first to do the one thing its form is built to prevent. It is to slow it down.

## II. The One Error Beneath the Many

Slow the grid down and a surprising economy appears in it. The panels are many and the error is one. Read them with the sequence's habit of asking which God is actually being refuted here, and nearly every one turns out to be aimed at a single figure: a God who is an item within the world, a very large and powerful object set among the other objects, graded by the standards that apply to objects. He takes three days over one task and ought to have budgeted His effort better. He designed an appendix and ought to have designed a cleaner one. He holds strings above a stage and ought to have pulled the kinder one. He is a tribal chieftain who ought not to hate so freely, an engineer who ought to have warned us about bacteria, a sovereign whose penalties are out of proportion to the offenses. Every ought in the grid is the ought one applies to a powerful being within the field — and that being is precisely the idol the whole corpus has spent its essays refusing.

This is the idol-family in a new dress. [*Is the Beginningless Grounded?*](https://oscargaitan.org/metaphysics/philosophy-of-time/divine-eternity/2026/09/25/is-the-beginningless-grounded-en.html) exposed the spatial idol that seats the Ground as the largest region and the temporal idol that seats Him as the earliest instant; [*Is God the Universe, or Is God Everything?*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophy/metaphysics/philosophical-theology/2026/10/01/is-god-the-universe-or-everything-en.html) added the third, the idol of totality that seats Him as the most complete sum. All three, the essays found, arise from maximizing a creaturely magnitude and mistaking the limit case for the Ground, who is reached by leaving the series and not by arriving at its biggest member. The meme trades in the same counterfeit, only it mocks the idol instead of worshipping it. The pantheist said this greatest object is God; the panel says your greatest object is a buffoon; and they are talking about the same object, neither of them about the Ground. The one error beneath the many panels is this: the grid has built a God out of the materials of the figure — power scaled up, knowledge scaled up, a will like ours scaled up — and has then triumphantly shown that this construction is incoherent, cruel, or ignorant, and has mistaken the demolition of its own construction for an argument against God.

Aquinas marked the mechanism in the sentence set at the head of this essay: an error concerning creatures spills over into a false opinion about God. Get the creature wrong — treat being as a resource, time as an exertion, dependence as puppetry, revelation as a data-feed — and the God inferred from that mistaken creature will be a monster or a fool, and will deserve the panel drawn of him. The grid is a gallery of such spillovers. It is not, for the most part, an argument against the Ground that the grounding essays reached by following dependence to its terminus. It is an argument against a cartoon, and the cartoon was drawn by getting the world wrong first. What follows takes the panels in their groups and shows, in each, where the jab lands — always on the idol, never on the Ground — and what the panel would have had to notice about the creature to have aimed any higher.

One temptation must be refused at the outset, though, or the diagnosis becomes its own kind of flattening. Not every panel is merely a metaphysical category mistake to be dissolved from above, and the essay that treats them all so has committed, in reverse, the error it names. Some panels are exegetical mistakes, to be conceded and corrected at the level of the text: the bat is a matter of ancient classificatory language, the "rest" of a word with two senses, the fossil record and the long quarrel between Scripture and natural science. One is a straightforward question in logic: the rock too heavy to lift. And one — Joshua — is a genuinely serious moral and theological problem that deserves far more respect than "the meme has mistaken the Ground." This diversity does not weaken the thesis; it is the thesis. For the point is not that the panels are empty. Some panels contain real questions. What the grid does is prevent those questions from becoming questions — it routes them past the mind as verdicts before the mind can receive them as problems. The trouble was never that memes are short. The trouble is that the sneer arrives exactly where the question should have begun.

## III. The Carver Above the Stage

A cluster of the most quoted panels share one picture, and it is the picture [*Not Gepetto*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophical%20theology/atonement/free%20will/augustine/thomas%20aquinas/metaphysics/2026/07/20/not-gepetto.html) was written to dismantle: God as a carver standing above a stage, holding strings that run down into the world, with levers in reach. Satan — why did I make you, again? is José Saramago's question distilled into a meme-sized form: if the hand on the strings can lift any figure off the boards, why lift the Son and not the Adversary? Sacrificed myself to myself and its companion, the father who hands over his child, mock the atonement as a transaction between two wills on the same set of strings. Let me save you from what I'm going to do to you if you don't worship me draws the same carver as a hostage-taker, threatening from above the board. And can I make a boulder so big that I can't lift it? asks whether the strongman on the stage can out-muscle himself. Four panels, one architecture: God as the most powerful operator inside the system, to be caught in the contradictions that catch powerful operators.

Take the boulder first, because it is the purest case and the tradition settled it long ago. Omnipotence has never meant the power to do the self-contradictory, because the self-contradictory is not a feat that is merely very hard; it is nothing at all, a form of words with no object. "A boulder too heavy for an omnipotent being to lift" does not name a difficult task God fails; it names no task, the way "a square circle" names no shape. To say God cannot make one is not to find a weight He cannot bench but to notice that the sentence described nothing to be made. But the deeper answer is the corpus's own: the paradox only bites if God is a lifter among lifters, a strongman located on the stage whose power is one magnitude on the figure's axis of force. He is not. He is not the biggest thing that lifts; He is the reason there is anything to be lifted and anyone to lift it. The question measures omnipotence as a quantity within the figure and then is surprised to find the quantity paradoxical. The Ground is on no axis the figure has, and "force" is one of the figure's axes.

Satan — why did I make you, again? is answered in full by *Not Gepetto*, and only its hinge need be recalled. The panel assumes that evil is an external criminal loose on the boards, a rival figure the carver could simply lift away, so that subtracting the Adversary would subtract the evil. But Adam did not fall because a serpent was stronger than he; the serpent supplied the occasion and the turning was Adam's own. Cain needed no devil at his shoulder to lift his hand. This is not to deny demonic temptation, which the tradition plainly affirms; it is to locate responsibility where the tradition also places it. The existence of the Adversary does not transfer moral responsibility from the creature to the tempter: Satan can tempt, but he cannot turn another's will for him, in that other's place — and so the removal of the tempter would leave untouched the one thing that actually falls. Evil is a disorder of the will, a curvature of the wood itself, and the destruction of one creature does not straighten the curvature in another. Remove every tempter from every stage and the wound remains exactly where it was, in the wood and not around it. Worse, to answer evil by lifting the enemy off the board is to answer force with greater force — the bigger hammer — and a universe whose ultimate principle is the bigger hammer is not saved from evil but confirmed in it. The panel demands that God be the strongest practitioner of exactly the logic that produced the complaint.

Sacrificed myself to myself and the handed-over child require a different discipline, and the border must be stated before the answer. This essay does not claim that the topology proves the Cross. The grounding argument reaches a That and halts; Calvary is not deduced from dependence but given in revelation, and [*Believe in God and Have Faith?*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophy/metaphysics/philosophical-theology/2026/10/04/believe-in-god-and-have-faith-en.html) was at pains to guard exactly this frontier, that the metaphysics of grounding yields the That and never the Who — not the Trinity, not the Incarnation, not the salvation the Cross effects. So the reply to this panel is not a proof but a demand for honest criticism. The panel mocks a thing the classical tradition also denies: two separate wills on the strings, a greater inflicting suffering on a lesser to settle a private grievance. The mockery is deserved — by the picture, which is not the doctrine. Trinitarian theology holds that the Son is not a second divine agent whose will stands externally over against the Father's: Father and Son possess the one divine will; and in the Incarnation the Son also assumes a genuinely human will, whose obedience is not coercion from outside but the free human obedience of the incarnate Son. The cross is not a carver spending one puppet to spare the workshop; it is God, undivided, entering the closed system of evil-answered-by-evil and refusing to operate on its terms — bearing from the inside the wound that force could only have postponed. The panel's "to himself" is drawn to sound absurd because it collapses three relations into a single identity — giver, recipient, and victim fused into one lonely egotist talking to his own reflection. But once it is the Christian revelation that is being criticized, the critic is obliged to criticize what that revelation actually says — Father and Son, human nature and divine nature, obedience and sacrifice and love — and not the compression of all of it into "myself to myself." Read through the doctrine it means to attack, the joke collapses distinctions the doctrine carefully preserves: Father and Son are distinct persons who possess the one divine will; the incarnate Son possesses a genuinely human will, and his human obedience is freely given. The Cross is therefore not a transaction between two competing divine wills — but neither is it a solitary God performing a private drama upon himself. It is the free self-offering of the incarnate Son to the Father, within the unity of the one divine will; and that is the one thing the grid, having already divided God into the wrong relations, cannot draw.

Let me save you from what I'm going to do to you is the carver as extortionist, and its sting depends entirely on the Gepetto architecture — a God outside the creature, manufacturing a threat so that the creature will submit to escape it. But the Ground does not stand outside the creature holding a whip; He is, *Not Gepetto* argued and [*Where Is God?*](https://oscargaitan.org/catholic-theology/philosophy-of-time/ontology/2026/05/05/where-is-god.html) before it, the floor the stage rests on, the holding-open of the very moment in which the creature turns or refuses. The next section but one will take up what hell actually is on this account, and it is not a torture the carver inflicts on the non-compliant. Here it is enough to see that the panel has to put God above the board, with a lever marked "punish," for the extortion to make sense — and that this is precisely the location the corpus has denied Him from its first essay. The threat the panel draws is the threat a tyrant inside the system makes. The Ground is not inside the system, and cannot occupy the tyrant's chair, because there is no chair within the figure that the reason for the figure could sit in.

## IV. The Idols on Their Axes

A second cluster measures God by a magnitude of the figure and finds Him wanting on the scale. Takes three days to create earth; eighty billion galaxies? One day, tops. The joke is arithmetical: the effort is misproportioned to the bulk, as though the Maker were a contractor who overcharged for the patio and threw in the cathedral for free. Finely tuned the universe for life — 0.0000001% of it, anyway runs the scale the other way: so much cosmos, so little life, what a waste, what a poorly optimized plant. Invented E = mc² mocks the believer for crediting God with what physics evidently does on its own. Three panels, one assumption: that God is a worker, an engineer, or a hypothesis within the figure, to be audited by the figure's measures of effort, efficiency, and explanatory economy.

The creation-days panel mistakes constitutive dependence for exertion. [*What Sustains the Now?*](https://oscargaitan.org/metaphysics/philosophical-theology/philosophy-of-god/2026/09/18/what-sustains-the-now-en.html) retired the picture of a God who holds the world up the way a hand holds a cup, against a native tendency to fall; to constitute a thing's being-actual is not to do work on it, and constitutive dependence has no how much. There is no quantity of divine effort that a large object consumes and a small one spares, because the Ground is not expending a resource that volume could exhaust. The galaxies do not "cost more" than the earth; being is not priced by the cubic parsec. The panel is funny only if one first imagines God sweating at a bench, and the sweating God is the idol — the carver again. (That the "days" of Genesis were never read by the tradition as a labor-timesheet is a point the seventh section will reach; here the error is prior to any reading of Genesis, in the very notion that creation is an output scaled to effort.)

The cosmic-waste panel is the totality idol inverted into an efficiency complaint. It grants, rightly, that the universe is overwhelmingly lifeless, and infers that a universe "for life" would have been built with less dead margin — that the vast cold remainder is a design flaw, evidence of no designer or a careless one. But the inference seats God as a utilitarian maker optimizing a resource, space, for an output, biomass, and then grades the ratio. That is the engineer-idol wearing a lab coat. On the account the corpus actually holds, the scale of the cosmos is not waste to be justified against a biomass quota, because the cosmos was never a warehouse rented by the organism and billed for its empty shelves. One may add, though the argument does not need it, that the same physics which makes the universe habitable at all makes it enormous and old — the long cooking of heavy elements in generations of stars is not margin around life but its precondition. The deeper reply, though, is the standing one: "fine-tuned for life" treats God as a factory optimizing throughput, and the Ground is not a factory. The panel has refuted an engineer the tradition never hired.

Invented E = mc² is the panel that thinks physics has made God redundant, and it is answered by [*Does the Law Explain Itself?*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophy/metaphysics/philosophy-of-science/2026/09/18/does-the-law-explain-itself-en.html) The sarcasm assumes that "God" and "the laws of physics" are two hypotheses competing for the same job — explaining why the apple falls, why mass and energy convert — so that crediting the law is debiting the deity. But the law and the Ground do not compete, because they answer different questions and stand at different depths. The law states how the figure behaves; it is a determinate structure, elegant and real. What the law does not do, and cannot, is confer its own actuality — make it the case that there is a law-governed world rather than nothing, that the equation is not merely true but instantiated in a cosmos that exists. "The physics does it" names the figure and stops at the figure's edge. The Ground is the reason there is a figure for the physics to be the physics of. To mock the believer for crediting God with E = mc² is to imagine the believer thinks God is a rival term inside the equation, a hidden variable physics will one day eliminate. He is not a term in the equation. He is why the equation has a world to be about.

## V. The Tribal God and the Projected Hatreds

Two panels aim at the moral character of God, and they are the ones the sequence must handle with the most care, because the true intuition inside them is strong and must be kept. I hate everyone that you hate draws God as the echo of His followers' resentments — Feuerbach's thesis in a speech bubble, the deity as the projection of the tribe onto the sky. Joshua had some of the most bloody military campaigns of all time indicts the God of the conquest narratives, the commander of the herem, and asks how such a God can be called good. The first panel is a theory of religion; the second is a moral accusation; and they deserve different answers.

The projection panel, taken as a theory, proves less than it thinks and more than it intends. It proves less, because the fact that a people often remakes God in the image of its own hatreds does not show that there is no God to be distorted — a counterfeit presupposes a genuine article to counterfeit, and "men make idols of their resentments" is a premise the prophets state more fiercely than Feuerbach ever did. The whole prophetic literature is God refusing to be the tribe's echo: your feasts I hate, says the God of Amos to His own people, in the very idiom the panel puts in His mouth, turned against them. But the panel proves more than it intends, because it is itself a specimen of the thing it describes. A deity assembled to validate a crowd's contempt — "He hates whom we hate" — is exactly the manufactured god of the manufactured crowd of *The Circus and the Cage*, and the meme-grid is a manufactured-crowd artifact through and through, routing its readers into a shared contempt that costs them nothing and asks them to reflect on nothing. The panel has drawn, with real accuracy, the god of the feed — and then attributed the feed's god to the tradition that spent its prophets smashing him. The Ground, who has an interior behind the Wall that no projection can read off and no crowd can vote into being, is not available to be anyone's echo, because He is not an object the tribe's wishes could shape. The projected god is a creature of the figure. The panel is right that he is contemptible. It is wrong that he is God.

Joshua is the hardest panel in the grid, and the honesty the sequence owes requires saying so plainly rather than reaching for a quick dissolution. The conquest narratives are genuinely terrible, and the discomfort they cause is not a failure of faith but a mark of a conscience the tradition itself formed. Three things can be said without pretending the difficulty away, and none of them is the word mystery said quickly to make the asking stop. The first is a point about the panel's form, not its substance: it flattens into a single trigger what is in fact a thicket of disputed questions — the genre of ancient Near Eastern war rhetoric, in which "utterly destroyed" is a formulaic hyperbole of victory found across the region and not a body count; the archaeological evidence that the conquest was far less total than the rhetoric; the arc of a revelation that moves, across the canon, from the war-herem to "love your enemies." None of this is settled, and none of it is settleable in a speech bubble, which is exactly why the panel chose a speech bubble. The second is pressed carefully, because it is easy to overstate. The moral objection is serious — it remains serious even when the critic's own account of moral obligation is itself contested — and nothing here makes it less so. But the accusation invokes a real "ought," binding on God himself, and if it invokes an objective moral order it has thereby opened a philosophical question it cannot leave closed: the panel cannot simply assume, without argument, the binding moral order by which it judges the biblical narrative while at the same time treating the figure as explanatorily complete in itself. This is not the claim that one must first become a theist before one may condemn a massacre; it is the narrower observation that an indignation this absolute is leaning on a ground the panel has not shown its own metaphysics can bear, and that the leaning is worth noticing before the indignation is spent. The third is the deepest and the most carefully bounded: the panel judges God as one moral agent among agents, a very powerful general on the stage, answerable at the bar the figure sets for its agents. But the Giver of being is not one more agent on the boards, and the Giver of life is not related to the life He gives as one creature is related to another. This does not dissolve the moral seriousness — it is not a license, and the tradition has never read it as one — but it relocates where the question has to be asked, from "why did this general exceed his authority" to "what is the relation of the source of all life to the lives it gives and gathers," which is not a panel's question and not this essay's to close. Joshua deserves an essay, not a rebuttal. What the panel deserves is the observation that it has performed, on the hardest material in the Bible, the one operation the genre exists to perform: it has replaced a question with a verdict, and the verdict with a laugh.

## VI. The Clumsy Engineer

A further cluster grades God on the quality of His product. Appendix — for teh lulz. Hangovers — you're welcome. Warn humans about bacteria and viruses? Nah. I'll just tell them demons cause sickness. The argument is from suboptimal design: a competent engineer would not have shipped the appendix, would have left out the hangover, would have mentioned the germ. The panels assume that God is a maker producing artifacts to specification, and that His competence can be read off the defects in the goods.

Two replies, the first local and the second structural. The local reply is that the "defects" are, with some regularity, dated science mistaken for timeless fact — which is a delicious irony in a grid that mocks the Bible for being out of date. The appendix, long treated as a paradigmatic useless vestige, is now known to contain abundant gut-associated lymphoid tissue and has been proposed to serve, among other functions, as a reservoir supporting the restoration of the intestinal microbiome after illness; the supposedly useless organ turns out, on current evidence, to have a function. The hangover is not a design feature at all but the predictable cost of ingesting a toxin, which is to say it is the body working, reporting an insult accurately, in a creature left free to drink. The panel that most confidently convicts the design is the one most likely to be holding an obsolete picture of the organism. But the structural reply is the one that matters, and it is the corpus's standing distinction: creation is the gift of being, not the manufacture of gadgets, and the Ground is not an engineer optimizing artifacts against a spec sheet. To exist as a dependent, finite, bodily, free creature is not to be a product that could have been machined to tighter tolerances; it is to receive, non-autonomously, a real nature with real causal powers of its own — powers that are genuinely the creature's and not a puppet's. The engineer-idol is graded on his tolerances. The Ground gives being, and what has being acts through genuinely creaturely causes rather than as a puppet of the First Cause. Finitude therefore entails real limits and the natural susceptibility of material causes to defect; but disease, suffering, and death in human life cannot simply be identified with finitude as such, for the tradition distinguishes created finitude from the loss of original justice. Moral evil is another matter again: it belongs not to created agency simply as such but to the disorder of a free creature's act. The three levels must be kept apart. At the level of nature, secondary causes are genuinely finite rather than extensions of the First Cause; at the level of fallen human existence, bodily disorder belongs to a nature deprived of its original integrity; and at the level of moral evil, the fault lies in disordered freedom and not in the Maker's workmanship. What the engineer-idol reads as shoddy manufacture is, across all three, something other than a defect in the gift of being: the finitude of a real secondary cause, the wound of a fallen nature, or the disorder of a free will — never a flaw in the One who gives the creature to be.

The germ-theory panel — "I'll just tell them demons cause sickness" — deserves its own sentence, because it mistakes what revelation is for. It assumes that Scripture is, or should have been, a data-feed about the figure: a manual in which the Maker downloads the true causes of things, germ theory included, to spare us the trouble of inquiry. But revelation, *Believe in God and Have Faith?* argued, is not the abolition of the Wall by information-transfer; it is the crossing of the Who, the disclosure of the One who speaks, not a dump of facts about the what that reason was given to find for itself. God did not hand over microbiology for the same reason He did not hand over the periodic table or the value of g: the intelligibility of the figure — the very order that made germ theory discoverable — is itself the gift, and the finding of it is the dignity of a creature given a world it can actually understand. A universe in which the Maker pre-empts every discovery with a dictated answer is a universe of puppets taking dictation, which is the Gepetto architecture one more time. And it should be said that "the Bible teaches demons cause all sickness" is itself a flattening: the same Scriptures distinguish illness from possession and prescribe forms of isolation and hygiene in Leviticus, and nowhere offer a unified demonic etiology of disease. The panel has invented a biblical theory of medicine in order to mock it.

<br>

## VII. The Letter and the Bat

The last cluster attacks the text — its science, its consistency, its chronology. Thinks bats are birds, pointing to the dietary code of Leviticus, which lists the bat among "flying things" that may not be eaten. [God] never rests — Isaiah 40:28 … except when I rest — Genesis, setting the God who "neither faints nor grows weary" against the God who "rested on the seventh day." And the fossil panel — genetic tree? sedimentary layers? fossil record? continental drift? light of distant stars? trolololol — aimed at a God who supposedly planted a false antiquity to deceive, or whose six days cannot survive the evidence of deep time. All three assume that fidelity to Scripture means reading it as a modern scientific report and scoring it as one.

The bat is the clearest case of anachronism dressed as a gotcha. The Hebrew word ʿoph, formed from the verb "to fly," is a collective term for flying, winged creatures — it takes in birds, and in the same lists winged insects, and the bat — and it functions as a folk-taxonomy that sorts creatures by how they live rather than by the cladistic classes of eighteenth-century biology: these fly, these swarm, these crawl. A text that groups the bat with the winged is not failing a Linnaean exam it never sat; it is classifying by flight, correctly, within a scheme whose question is "how does this creature move through the world," not "what is its evolutionary descent." To convict Leviticus of a zoology error is to demand that a three-thousand-year-old dietary law anticipate a taxonomy invented three millennia later — which is itself the category mistake the whole corpus hunts, the measuring of one order by the instrument built for another. The panel does not catch the Bible being stupid. It catches the reader being anachronistic.

The rest-contradiction is the sequence's own favorite kind of error — a single word doing two jobs, overheard as one. The Hebrew of Genesis, shabat, does not mean "recuperated from exhaustion"; it means "ceased, desisted, stopped the work," and the Sabbath it founds is consecration, not a nap — a setting-apart of completed work, not the recovery of a tired laborer. Isaiah, denying that God "faints or grows weary," denies fatigue. The two sentences collide only if "rest" is forced to mean the same thing in both, and it does not: one is cessation, the other is tiredness, and nothing in the texts makes the Creator who ceased on the seventh day a Creator who was tired by the sixth. The panel manufactures the contradiction by the equivocation the method note promised to expose — the whole force of the jab lives in the crossing of the wires between two senses of one English word, and pried apart, there is no contradiction left to enjoy.

The fossil panel is the one that mistakes a modern brittleness for the faith it attacks. Its targets — the genetic tree, the strata, the fossil record, continental drift, the light that left distant stars billions of years ago — are indeed fatal to a particular reading of Genesis, the young-earth reading that requires a cosmos a few thousand years old. But that reading is not the only one the Christian tradition has permitted, nor the only one found within it, and some of the oldest voices warned against it in advance. Augustine, in De Genesi ad litteram, cautioned Christians not to make Scripture assert false things about the natural world, lest they discredit the faith before those who know the natural world better — a warning written fourteen centuries before the first fossil was catalogued, and aimed at precisely the posture the panel assumes every believer must hold. The "days" were read non-literally by Fathers who had never heard of a stratum, for reasons internal to the text. And deep time is no embarrassment to the God the grounding essays reached, because *Is the Beginningless Grounded?* already showed that no length of duration grounds itself: an earth of four billion years and a cosmos of fourteen are as dependent, as unable to confer their own actuality, as an earth of six days would be. Age is a magnitude of the figure, and the Ground is on no axis the figure has — so the believer loses exactly nothing if the rocks are old. The panel scores a direct hit on a hermeneutic the tradition never required and the oldest voices in it explicitly cautioned against. It mistakes the most brittle believer for the faith, which is the easiest idol to draw and the least worth drawing.

<br>

## VIII. Punishment, Hiddenness, and the Preposition

Two panels remain, and they turn on the two things the last essay in the sequence was written to clarify: what the refusal of God actually is, and why God is not available as an object of sight. Infinite punishment for finite crime? sounds legit is the proportionality objection: an eternal penalty for a temporal offense is a sentence no just magistrate would pass. Too sexy for my creation — the vanity jab, the God who will not simply show himself and settle the matter — is the hiddenness objection in a sneer. Both are answered not by softening the doctrine but by locating it correctly.

The punishment panel measures hell as a quantity on an axis of retribution — so much offense, so much penalty, and the books do not balance. But hell, on the account *Believe in God and Have Faith?* built, is not a sentence a magistrate imposes from outside; it is the fixed form of a will that will not go in. The demon is the limit case: he has an intellect that cannot simply deny what it knows of the Ground, yet a will that refuses the end it knows — not non est Deus, which he of all beings cannot say, but non serviam, and beneath it the colder non te egeo, "I have no need of you." His condition is not a fine levied for a crime; it is the permanent self-exclusion of a will that has turned from the only end that could fulfill it, the preposition in withheld forever. Read so, "infinite punishment for finite crime" misdescribes both terms. It is not punishment in the sense of an external penalty scaled to an offense; it is the intrinsic shape of a refusal, the state of a creature who will not enter the One in whom it already, and helplessly, stands. And it is not finite in the sense the panel needs, because what is at stake is not the magnitude of a deed but the direction of a will — and a will fixed against its end is not a small thing with a disproportionate price but a whole life turned the wrong way. The panel's arithmetic — crime here, punishment there, compare the sizes — is the quantitative idol one last time, applied to the soul. Yet self-exclusion must not be heard as a denial of judgment or a shrinking of hell to a mood. Its chief punishment is precisely the definitive loss of communion with God, consequent upon the creature's own definitive refusal of that communion; and to call this self-exclusion is neither to abolish the divine judgment that pronounces and confirms it nor to reduce hell to a merely psychological state. The refusal is real, and so is the judgment that confirms the refused in their refusal. The reality the panel caricatures is therefore relational, not quantitative: not a tariff, but a turning, or the refusal of one.

The hiddenness panel demands that God end the argument by appearing — step onto the stage, produce himself as an object, and be counted. But this is the category mistake *Believe in God and Have Faith?* named precisely: the demand for object-like evidence of the Ground, the insistence that He show up as one more item in the field to be inspected and weighed. One cannot photograph the light by which every photograph is taken. God is not unavailable as an object of sight because He is shy, or vain, or playing hard to get; He is unavailable as an object of natural sight in this life because He is not an object within the created field at all — not a tenant of the field but the condition of there being a field with tenants in it. This is the Wall, which marks the limit of the wayfarer's natural knowing, and revelation does not breach it from our side but is the Ground crossing it from His. The "too sexy to show himself" sneer assumes a God who could trivially appear among the objects and perversely declines — the idol again, a celebrity withholding an appearance, a shy extraterrestrial who keeps to the far side of the furniture. But the Ground is not hiding behind the furniture of the universe, because He is not in the room as the furniture is; He is the reason the room is. The Ground does not decline to be an object. He is not the kind of thing an object-appearance could display, any more than the rules of a game are one more move within it. And the reaching for the nearness the panel thinks it is denied is, as *Is God the Universe, or Is God Everything?* ended, a reaching the tradition can answer more fully than the sneer can imagine: not a God who flickers into visibility to win a debate, but the One nearer to the creature than it is to itself, in whom it lives and moves and has its being — present not as a seen object but as that in whose light seeing happens at all. And this must not be left sounding like agnosticism, as though the essay's last word were that God cannot be known. The opposite is the case. God is not an object within the created field, and He is nonetheless genuinely knowable, because He has made Himself known — the That reached by reason, the Who disclosed in revelation, received in faith. The Christian does not hold that God is absent; he holds that God is present in a mode the panel has ruled out by definition when it demanded an object. And the God the panel demands to see as an object is precisely the God the tradition says will be seen — not naturally in this life, and not ever as one object among objects, but by the gift of glory, in the vision of the divine essence toward which faith is even now the way. The answer to "why won't He show himself" is not that He hides, but that what is asked for is, in this life, the wrong kind of seeing, and in the next, a gift greater than the one demanded.

<br>

## IX. The Reflection the Panel Prevents

Set the grid down now, slowed, its panels answered, and the shape of the whole is visible. The jabs are many and the target is one: a God built out of the figure's materials — power as a magnitude, knowledge as a data-feed, will as a lever, making as manufacture, justice as a tariff — and then convicted of the incoherences and cruelties that belong to any object so built. The grid is a gallery of idols, drawn with real wit, each one genuinely worth mocking, and not one of them the Ground the grounding essays reached by leaving the series rather than maximizing a member of it. The atheist of the panel and the pantheist of the earlier essay have more in common than either would like: both have mistaken the biggest object for God, the one to worship it and the other to ridicule it, and both have missed, on the same axis-less exit, the One who is the reason there is a series of objects at all.

But the point of this essay was never only the panels; it was the form. *The Circus and the Cage* argued that the deepest work of the apparatus is not any particular falsehood it circulates but its implicit promise that there is nothing beneath the feed — no floor, no ground the channel has not already mediated — so that a reader held in perpetual reaction never returns to the crossing where presence and judgment become possible. The meme-grid is that promise in miniature. It does not merely assert that God is a buffoon; it assures the reader, by its very form, that the matter is so obvious it needs no reflection, that to pause is to be slow, that the laugh is the argument. And the laugh is engineered precisely to keep the reader from the one act that would dissolve the panels: standing still long enough to ask which God is actually being drawn. Every answer in the foregoing required only that single act. None of them needed new facts the maker of the grid lacked. They needed the reflective distance the grid is built to close.

So the reply to the grid is not, in the end, a better grid — a counter-meme flattering the opposite reflex, which *The Circus and the Cage* warned is not escape but relocation within the same cage. The reply is the recovery of the act itself: the refusal to be assembled into the crowd the panel is recruiting, the insistence on remaining a person who weighs rather than a node that reacts. And the charity the sequence keeps extends even here, even to the maker of the grid. He is not the enemy; the flattening is. The wit in the panels is real, and the anger underneath some of them — at a cruel god, a tribal god, a careless engineer — is often anger at an idol that deserves to be torn down, an anger the prophets would have recognized and shared. The tragedy is only that he tore down the idol and thought he had reached the altar, demolished the carver and thought he had refuted the floor. The idol he was right to despise was never the One on no axis the figure has. Beneath the feed there is a ground, and the ground was given, and a reader who stops circling long enough to stand on it discovers the thing the panel is built, above all, to prevent him from discovering: that it holds.

So the whole reply narrows, in the end, to the one act the apparatus exists to prevent: judgment. Not another verdict, not another panel, but the recovery of the distance in which a person can stop, weigh, and ask. Set the grid down.
