## Abstract

A question asked in the first person — do I have a say on that new monad? — conflates two questions this topology must keep apart: whether I have authority over the other, and what authority I have over myself at a crossing where my choice will cross into another's life. This essay separates them and shows that the first is answered before the moral case begins, by structures already standing. No monad is the possession of another: this is windowlessness read inward, the same wall *The Family of Crossing Points* raised against any super-monad, now turned on the smallest grouping there is. The one thing that is mine, and even here the word is analogical rather than proprietary, is the will; the body is received rather than self-authored, confluence constitutive of my embodied identity rather than property at my disposal; and the other monad is neither — it is the one center in the room that is categorically not mine, grounded past me. Pregnancy does not weaken this. It is the maximal asymmetric tangency: contact that is radically asymmetric in dependence, constitutive, and addressed to one in whom reciprocal response is not yet possible. But asymmetry of dependence is not authorship of being — I host the biology; I do not ground the person, whose crossing is held open directly by the one Ground that holds mine. Birth changes the mode of the tangency, not the standing of the monad, so whatever say I lack over the child after birth I lacked before it, with no seam between. What I have instead of a say is a station — the office *The Family of Crossing Points* named — and it is the one office whose vacancy no neighboring point can cover. That uncoverability is the whole content of the phrase responsibility of responsibilities. The essay meets the strongest objection, the bodily-autonomy argument in its most careful form, and discharges a debt left open in *Tangent Relational Lemniscates*: to show not merely that an asymmetric tangency is possible, but that this one is the structure to which obligation attaches. It closes, in the corpus's habit, by marking the narrower claim the metaphysics actually settles and the further claims — including the normative principle itself — that it does not.

## I. The Question That Hides a Second Question

Ask the question in the first person, the way it is actually asked, and its grammar has already decided half the answer before any argument begins. Do I have a say on that new monad? The phrase a say on wants the monad to be the kind of thing a say could be held over — a holding, a possession, a matter fallen within one's jurisdiction. But two different questions are wearing that one sentence, and they pull in opposite directions. One is a question about authority over another: do I decide what becomes of this second center? The other is a question about authority over myself: what may my own will do, here, at a crossing where its response will pass into a life that is not mine? The topology answers the first before the second is even reached, and answers it not as a special ruling handed down for this hard case but as a plain consequence of what a monad is.

The confusion worth naming at the outset is that the first question usually arrives disguised as the second. It is my body, it is my life, therefore my say: that is the sentence most people mean, and the whole weight of the prior essays in this corpus falls on the word therefore. The premises concern what is mine; the conclusion asserts a say over what is not me at all. Between them sits an inference that looks innocent and is not, because it treats a say over myself as though it straightforwardly extended into a say over whatever my self is, for a while, joined to. The essay's first task is simply to refuse the slide — to hold the two questions apart long enough to see that they have different subjects, different answers, and that only one of them was ever mine to decide.

## II. The One Thing That Is Mine

Begin with the premise the slogan leans on. It is tempting to answer it by observing that the body is not something I authored — that I did not make my body but received it, from a confluence with no bottom link, as [*The Weight of the Present*](https://oscargaitan.org/metaphysics/philosophical%20theology/ontology/catholic%20philosophy/thomism/2026/07/02/the-weight-of-the-present.html) and [*Whose Condensation Am I?*](https://oscargaitan.org/phenomenology/christian-theology/philosophical-anthropology/2026/08/07/whose-condensation-am-i-en.html) established — and to conclude that what one did not author one does not own. That move is too quick, and it is not the one this essay needs. Ownership does not in general require authorship; a person can own what was given or bequeathed without having made it. So the reclassification of the body cannot turn on the bare fact of reception, and the argument does not rest it there.

The narrower and sounder claim is this. The body is not a possession held at absolute disposal — not a chattel over which its bearer exercises authorship-and-disposal, the dominion that would include a right to dispose of whatever is bound up with it. This is not to deny that a woman has a genuine and morally weighty authority over her own embodied person; she plainly does, and the argument neither needs nor wants to deny it. It is to deny only that this authority is dominion of the absolute kind — that bodily intimacy with a life entails metaphysical ownership of it. The body, in this corpus's vocabulary, is confluence: inheritance and reception, the seed and the uninherited rain, constitutive of my embodied identity rather than an object owned by an interior self standing over it. It belongs to me as what I am, not as what I hold; and what one is, one stewards, one does not dispose of as a thing. This both preserves embodiment — no self hovering behind the body it merely uses — and withholds from the possessive the one sense, absolute disposal, that the moral conclusion would have to borrow.

What does survive as mine in the strictest sense is what this corpus has singled out from the beginning as the open side of every condensation: the response of the will at the Now, the one genuinely uninherited contribution a life makes. Even here the word mine is analogical, not proprietary — the will is not a chattel I hold, and not even the only thing constitutive of me, since intellect, memory, and embodied action are no less me, but the uniquely non-inherited locus at which I respond and act. Autonomy, then, is real, and the slogan was not wrong to prize it; it was wrong about where it lives and what kind of thing it is. It lives in the response, not in a dominion over the body, and it is answerability before it is ownership. So before the second monad is even in view, the ground beneath the question has shifted: the authority the conclusion needs — absolute disposal, reaching through the body into another center — is not the authority the premises secure, which is stewardship of a received life and the ownership, analogically, of one's own response.

## III. No Monad Is Owned

Now the decisive structure, which settles the first question before the second is reached. [*The Monad That Receives*](https://oscargaitan.org/metaphysics/philosophy%20of%20mind/philosophy%20of%20religion/leibniz/2026/07/17/the-monad-that-receives.html) argued that a monad is windowless not for want of doors but for want of width: it is a crossing point of zero thickness, one substance precisely because there is no extension in it for a second to occupy. [*The Family of Crossing Points*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophy%20of%20religion/metaphysics/social%20ontology/philosophical%20anthropology/catholic%20philosophy/2026/07/19/the-family-of-crossing-points.html) carried the same fact outward and drew the wall against Hegel: no created grouping has a crossing point of its own — no family-monad, no clan-monad, no nation-monad, no super-substance whose members are its organs. The choir sings on key without a throat above the throats.

Turn that wall on the one dyad this essay cares about. If no creature stands above the crossing of another, then I do not stand above the crossing of the one I carry. [*Tangent Relational Lemniscates*](https://oscargaitan.org/ontology/philosophy-of-time/phenomenology/2026/08/25/tangent-relational-lemniscates-en.html) supplied the theorem that makes this exact: presence is not participation. I can be present to another — really, non-metaphorically present, in the most sustained tangency there is — without being a source of that other's becoming, without occupying any part of its crossing, without our histories sharing a single locus of actualization. And *The Family of Crossing Points* already said what a birth is and is not: the newborn is not a fragment added to the parents and not a fragment split off from them; it is one, entire, from its own first crossing point onward, already condensing what arrives at it. Put these together and the answer to the first question is forced. The new monad is the single thing in the room that is categorically not mine — less mine, strictly, than my own body, for the body is at least constitutive of me, the convergence I am, whereas the other monad is not even that. It is another center entirely, and a center is the one thing that cannot be possessed, because to possess a center would be to stand above a crossing, which the whole topology forbids to every creature without exception. I do not have a say over the new monad for precisely the reason I do not have a say over any monad: there is no vantage above a crossing from which a creature could exercise one.

It is worth separating what is easily run together here, because a reader will rightly object that parents plainly exercise authority over their children without owning them. They do — but the authority is office-relative: authority for the child, exercised from within the office of parent and bounded by the child's own good, not authority over the child's being, the power to determine whether that center is at all. Ownership, jurisdiction, and office-relative authority are three different things, and only the first, with the dominion it would confer, is what the disposal question needs. So the claim is not that I may hold no authority toward the new monad; it is that I cannot occupy the one position — above its crossing — from which it would become mine to determine. Lack of ownership entails lack of say in the strict sense at issue not because authority always requires ownership, which it does not, but because this particular authority, authority over another's being, requires a vantage no creature holds over any other. None of this claims that every act with a fatal effect is, as such, an exercise of authority over another's being; many deaths follow from acts that claim no such authority at all. The claim is narrower, and it concerns a particular justification: when the warrant offered is this is mine to decide because the other life falls within my bodily authority, that warrant silently converts authority over my own response into authority over another monad's continued being — and it is the conversion that fails, because the second authority was never mine to exercise.

## IV. Host, Not Ground

One inference will try to reopen what Section III closed, and it is the inference that does all the real work in the ordinary argument: I sustain it, therefore it is mine to end. The dependence is total; does total dependence not confer disposal? *Tangent Relational Lemniscates* dismantled this in advance, in the section on the ground beneath relation. If two curves are tangent and both are sustained by the one Ground, it is tempting to route the relation through the Ground as a third term — but the correct structure is not A → Ground → B; it is A ↔ B, both terms and their relation alike sustained by a Ground that is not itself a relatum. The Ground sustains; it does not stand between, and it does not delegate its sustaining to either curve.

Apply this to the womb and the disposal inference comes apart at the joint it was resting on. The mother is not the child's ground. She is its host — the biological condition of its continuance, which is real, asymmetric, and for a season total. But [*Does Time Need Me, or Do I Need Time?*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophy-of-time/ontology/2026/04/10/does-time-need-me-or-do-i-need-time.html) and [*When Is the Present?*](https://oscargaitan.org/metaphysics/ontology/philosophical%20theology/catholic%20philosophy/thomism/2026/07/03/when-is-the-present.html) located the holding-open of any crossing outside the series altogether, in the self-sustaining I AM WHO I AM, and nothing in pregnancy moves that holding into the mother. The crossing of the one she carries is held open directly by the same Ground that holds hers open, not by her. She supplies the biology; she does not supply the being. Nothing in that biological dependence confers the authority the disposal inference claims, because the authority claimed is authority over whether the other is at all, and that was never hers to hold. The soil is co-constitutive of the tree, as *Whose Condensation Am I?* insisted, and the soil is no less a cause for being uninherited; but the soil does not own the tree, and could not end it by right of having fed it. Host is not ground. Feeding is not authorship of being. The Ground is not wheeled in here to settle the question from outside; it is the same distinction the corpus has used throughout, between what sustains a crossing and what merely feeds it, and the point could be made without the word at all — biological sustenance and ontological grounding are not the same relation, and biological sustenance cannot, by itself, confer authority over whether the sustained being is at all. The most total dependence a creature can stand in toward another creature still runs only along the biology, and the biology was never the floor.

## V. The Maximal Asymmetric Tangency

A precise reader will press: is the womb a tangency at all? The child is within, not beside; the figure of two curves touching seems to describe friendship better than pregnancy. But the corpus is already committed, and committed rightly. *The Monad That Receives* treated the child in the womb as a distinct monad from its first crossing, already condensing what arrives — the mother's fear or her calm entering its constitution directly, as reception rather than inheritance. A monad has no width in which to contain a second monad; so the womb cannot be one center holding another inside it. What it is, is tangency — in its most intimate and most asymmetric form. Two crossings, each its own, in sustained contact, neither the history of the other.

*Tangent Relational Lemniscates* had already conceded that tangency need not be symmetrical in what it carries: teaching, causation, and address can all be modeled as sustained tangency while remaining asymmetrical in which way the content passes — "a person can address another who does not respond, or cannot." Pregnancy is the limit of that concession. Gestational support is radically asymmetric: the one carried depends on maternal physiology for its very continuance in a way the mother does not depend on fetal physiology for hers. The point is the asymmetry of dependence, not a claim that nothing passes the other way — there is, in fact, documented traffic in both directions at the cellular level, and the argument needs none of it suppressed. And the relation is addressed to one who has, as yet, no developed capacity through which to exercise or express a response: a paradigmatic human relation in which reciprocal agency is not yet possible. This is not the claim that the one carried lacks subjectivity, and the argument does not require that claim; it requires only that response cannot yet run the other way. Nor is the asymmetry a defect in the relation, to be corrected toward reciprocity; it is the relation's defining shape. And it is exactly the shape the language of a say is least able to read, because a say presumes a jurisdiction between parties who could in principle contend, and finds here instead a dependence so complete that the dependent party cannot contend at all. Where the ordinary argument sees the absence of the other's voice and reads it as the absence of the other's standing, the topology sees the asymmetric tangency at its maximum and reads it as standing at its most defenseless.

## VI. Birth Changes the Mode, Not the Monad

The relation is, as the question rightly says, incarnate for a while — integrated for a short period and then not. Does the temporariness bear on what is mine? *Tangent Relational Lemniscates* drew the distinction that answers this: the point of tangency is not the duration of the relation. A tangency is the instantaneous topology of contact; sustained tangency is the relational persistence that re-instantiates that shape across a history. The womb is sustained tangency of finite duration, and finite duration is the ordinary condition of tangencies, not a mark against their reality.

What birth is, on this account, is not the beginning of the monad — the monad already crossed, already is one, entire — but a change in the mode of the tangency: from internal-and-dependent to external-and-dependent. The dependence itself does not end at birth; the infant is as helpless the hour after as the hour before. Only the mode of contact changes. And from this a continuity argument follows that the bodily-autonomy framing structurally cannot run. Whatever say I lack over the child the hour after birth — and no one holds that birth confers on anyone a say to end the child — I lacked the hour before, because nothing crossed at that threshold except the mode of the tangency, not the monad's standing as its own crossing. There is no seam at which not mine to end became mine to end; birth is a change of place and office, not a change of ontological status, and it cannot be made to carry a moral discontinuity it does not contain. The principle beneath the phrase can be stated on its own, for it reaches past this case: a change in relational configuration cannot, by itself, constitute a change in the ontological identity of the related term. Birth reconfigures the relation; it does not re-identify the one who stands in it. The argument is available to this corpus and not to its rival for one reason: the rival never granted that there was a second monad before birth to be continuous with. The topology granted it several essays ago, and must now live with what the grant entails.

## VII. The Uncoverable Office

If I have no say over the monad, what do I have? A say over myself, and a station toward the other. *The Family of Crossing Points* gave the vocabulary exactly: at a crossing point one integrates or one abdicates, and "mother" and "father" are not possessions held but offices occupied or vacated. [*Legion of Monads*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophy/metaphysics/philosophy%20of%20religion/philosophical%20anthropology/philosophy%20of%20mind/2026/07/18/legion-of-monads.html) added the warning that a vacated point does not stay empty — something moves into it: a false center, a grievance, or a neighboring point pressed into an office not its own, which ordinary language calls parentification and the topology diagnoses as one crossing straining to cover a vacancy that was never structurally its to hold. And the office is not a social label laid over the relation from outside: society names a relation "mother" because the relation is already there to be named, not the other way round. The office is generated by the relational structure itself — a topological fact before it is ever a cultural one.

Here is the observation that makes this office unlike the others, and that gives the phrase its precision. Of the tangencies a life stands in, this is the one whose vacancy cannot presently be covered. An older sibling can be pressed, wrongly but really, into "parent" to the younger ones; a grandparent can stand where a father abdicated. But no neighboring crossing point can be pressed into "mother to this child at this stage." The office is, at this stage, strictly uncoverable — there is no second point in the whole network that can hold toward this monad what this one holds. Two qualifications keep the word exact. Uncoverable does not mean the child will never be cared for by another: a later point may assume its care entirely — adoption, the father, medicine, another woman are all real coverings of the offices that come after. It means that no other crossing can retroactively occupy this particular relational position, gestational host to this monad at this stage. The office is structurally uncoverable within the tangency, not empirically irreplaceable in every respect; what cannot be transferred is the position, not every function that will later follow from it. And an uncoverable office is the gravest a will can occupy, because to abdicate a coverable office is to shift a burden onto another who can bear it, whereas an uncoverable office, once abdicated, leaves a vacancy that no other crossing can fill. This is the entire content of responsibility of responsibilities, and it is not rhetorical intensification. It is the exact description of an office that is held by one alone, that no other point can presently cover, and that is held toward a monad in whom reciprocal agency is not yet possible — one who cannot, as yet, hold anything answering in return. Three features, one station: solitary, uncoverable, and owed to one who cannot yet answer. The topology does not need the empirical claim that no other human relation ever exhibits all three; it needs only that this is the paradigmatic case in which the three coincide, and that where they coincide, this weight is what a will is asked to carry.

## VIII. The Violinist

The strongest objection has not yet been met, and it must be met in its most careful form rather than a convenient one. Judith Thomson's argument grants, for the sake of argument, everything Sections III through VII have claimed about the standing of the fetus — grants it full personhood — and still denies the conclusion. You wake to find a famous violinist plugged into your circulation; he will die if you unplug him before nine months are out; no one denies he is a person; and yet, Thomson says, you do not commit murder by declining to serve nine months as his life-support. The right to life does not include a right to the continued use of another's body. And the objection does not depend on any crude claim that the body is property because one made it. Its strongest form concedes that the body was received, not authored, and asserts only this: that a person has a morally privileged authority over what may be done to and through her own embodied person — an authority that does not reduce to property and is not answered merely by reclassifying the body as stewardship. That is the version worth confronting, and it is considerably harder than the property version. It helps to separate the two objections the violinist is made to carry. One is a general principle of autonomy: no person automatically acquires a right to the use of another's body. The other is a claim of equivalence: that pregnancy is relevantly like the violinist, so that whatever holds for him holds for it. The topology does not dispute the first. It disputes the second.

The principal answer is not that the body is stewardship rather than property; it is that the violinist analogy abstracts away the very relational structure whose moral significance is under examination. The violinist is person-plus-dependence-plus-stranger: there is no antecedent office toward him, and he is fastened to you by a third party's act, an arrangement lowered onto two lives from outside both. Pregnancy, on the structure these essays have already built, is person-plus-dependence-plus-office — and then, per Section IX, plus uncoverability. The morally decisive features are exactly the ones the thought experiment is built to remove. Thomson's case isolates bare dependence and shows, rightly, that bare dependence between strangers does not bind one to nine months of life-support; the topology agrees, and Section IX will say so in general terms. But the relation under examination is not bare dependence between strangers. It is dependence inside an office no third point can presently cover, and the violinist scenario is precisely that relation with the office subtracted. An analogy that removes the feature in dispute cannot settle the dispute.

A second and lesser point can be added, but held modestly, because it does not reach every form of the objection. Insofar as a particular telling of the violinist leans on the picture of the body as a holding one may lend or withhold at will, it leans on exactly the absolute-disposal model Section II set aside; strip that picture and that much of its intuitive pull slackens. But the strongest version, as stated, does not rest there — it rests on privileged authority over the embodied person, not on property — so the ownership reply cannot be the load-bearing one, and is not asked to be. In fairness, a reader who rejects the topology's account of the embodied person will reject the principal reply along with it, and is entitled to make the account of embodiment the place to fight. The claim here is the bounded one: that within this framework the violinist cannot perform the work it performs elsewhere, because the framework never granted the stranger-relation the analogy smuggles in as though it were the whole of the case.

## IX. From Asymmetry to Obligation

A debt was left open where this essay's vocabulary was built. *Tangent Relational Lemniscates* established that a geometrically symmetrical tangency can carry a relationally asymmetrical content — teaching, causation, address — but admitted plainly that it could "name without discharging" the taxonomy of which asymmetries carry which freight, and in particular which carry obligation. This essay is that debt coming due for one asymmetry, and it is worth paying in a form general enough to be tested elsewhere rather than fitted only to the case that provoked it.

Here is the schema. An asymmetric tangency has the structure to which obligation attaches when two conditions hold together: the dependent term's capacity to continue toward its own next crossing runs through the other term's response, and the other term occupies an office that no third point can presently cover. The first condition alone does not bind — Thomson is right that bare dependence cannot, or a life would be buried under a lattice of infinite claims, every distant need a summons. The discipline is worth stating sharply: dependence explains why another needs me, not why I owe them anything; uncoverability explains why the responsibility, once owed, cannot be handed off, not why it is owed at all. Neither the need nor the non-transferability is yet a norm. What the structure gathers is dependence conjoined with uncoverability. Where other points can cover, the responsibility is sharable and distributes among them — the drowning stranger may be owed rescue by several who can reach him, and the duty is borne in common; when every other point capable of covering the need disappears, the responsibility concentrates in the one that remains, the only adult left at the lake. Where no point can cover, a duty that is owed rests undivided on the one point that can hold it — uncoverability settles where the responsibility falls once there is one, not whether there is one to fall. This is why the weight falls heaviest exactly where it was least chosen — not because the unchosen binds more than the chosen, which would be a strange principle, but because the uncoverable bears more heavily than the coverable, and pregnancy is the case in which the unchosen and the uncoverable most often coincide in a single office. The schema also predicts its own limits: as the office becomes coverable — as other points appear who can hold what this one holds — the duty becomes sharable in proportion, which is why the moral texture of the obligation changes after birth even though the monad's standing does not. The standing is constant; the coverability is what varies, and the duty varies with it.

One honesty must be posted here, in the corpus's habit of marking exactly where one kind of claim ends and another begins. What has been exhibited is a structure — dependence running through a single response, conjoined with an office no third point can cover. A structure is not yet an obligation, and it would be a sleight of hand to let the geometry appear to secrete an ought on its own. The topology does not manufacture obligation from geometry; it identifies the structure to which moral obligation attaches, and must then name the principle that crosses the remaining distance. Three levels are in play, and the essay gains by holding them apart: the topology supplies the structure — the subject, the relation, and the distribution of responsibility; a moral anthropology identifies the one toward whom the structure runs as an innocent person; and natural law supplies the norm. That norm is not itself metaphysical but moral, and this corpus takes it from the natural-law tradition it already stands within: that an innocent and dependent person has a claim not to be deliberately destroyed, and that one who uniquely occupies an office of care toward such a person carries duties that mere proximity never generates. Only the third step crosses from is to ought. Grant that principle — which the essay does not pretend to derive from the Now, the monad, or the tangent — and the structure exhibited here is precisely what it binds to, and binds most heavily where the office is uncoverable. Withhold it, and the essay has still done something: it has located, with more precision than the slogans allow, the exact structure over which the moral question must be fought, and has shown that the question is not whether a woman owns her body but what is owed from an uncoverable office to a dependent who cannot yet answer.

## X. A Formal Statement

Everything above compresses into one statement, offered here as the claim the essay exists to make. I have a say over exactly one thing: my own will, the single uninherited contribution at my crossing, and even that is mine analogically, as answerability rather than as property. Over the new monad I have no say, because no monad is the possession of another and this one is grounded past me, held open by the Ground that holds me. What I have toward it is not a say but a station — the office "mother," which I may occupy or abdicate but cannot own. And what converts the narrow say-over-myself into the gravest of responsibilities is not that the monad is mine, for it is not, but that the office is uncoverable: there is no neighboring crossing point that can hold toward this monad, at this stage, what I hold. The transformation the essay has worked can be put as a pair: I have no authority over the other — the other as something to be determined — but I have an authority for, the answerability of one who occupies an office toward the other; and across the argument the title's say migrates from a say over the child to a responsibility for my own response. The question do I have a say on that new monad? dissolves on inspection into a category error, because the say was never over the monad. It was always and only over myself — and that is precisely why it weighs what it weighs.

In the habit of this corpus, the narrower claim must be marked off from the larger ones it does not reach. What the metaphysics settles is this much and no more: that the new monad is not mine as property, that my authority runs over my response and not over its being, and that the office I occupy toward it is, at this stage, uncoverable. What the metaphysics does not settle, by itself, is even the normative principle that turns that structure into a duty — that principle is moral, named in Section IX, not read off the geometry — nor the full slate of questions that follow once it is granted: how law should weigh an uncoverable office against the other genuine goods of the one who bears it, what mercy is owed to a woman who stands in that office unwillingly or in anguish, how the tragic case is to be adjudicated where two grounded lives are set against each other — for there are never two grounds, only the one Ground and the several continuances it holds — and the mother's own continuance is itself at stake. Those turn on further premises the topology does not contain, and it would be the super-monad's error in a new disguise to pretend a geometry had decided them. The work done here is the clearing of the ground: it removes the word mine from the center of the question and sets the word office in its place, and leaves to moral theology, to prudence, and to mercy what must be reasoned once the category error is gone. That is less than the whole of what the question asks, and it is the surer part.

<br>

## XI. Coda

An earlier essay in this corpus set a figure at the mirror and found that the one who looks never appears among the contents of the glass — the ground does not show up inside what it grounds. Stand a pregnant woman at that same mirror. She sees one face and carries two crossings, and the second crossing does not appear in the glass any more than the first self ever did, and for the identical reason: what grounds a crossing is never among the contents the crossing holds. She is not regarding a possession she may keep or discard. She is standing, for a while, at the one tangency a human being can bear that no one else can be pressed to bear in her stead — host to a history that is not hers, present to a center she did not author, answerable with the single thing that is hers to answer with.

The question was never whose the child is. The child does not belong to either parent as a possession; from its first crossing it is its own created center, sustained by the One who sustains them both. The question was only ever what the mother's will would do at the crossing where the entire continuity of another has, for a season, been placed upon her crossing. That is not a say over the new monad. It is the heaviest thing a say over oneself has ever been asked to carry.

**That's why it's a sin to kill a mockingbird**
> — Harper Lee, *To Kill a Mockingbird*

The mockingbird does not reproduce the whole structure traced here; it names one of its deepest moral features. It is innocent and defenseless. It cannot contend on equal terms with the one who can destroy it. In literary form, the image gives voice to what the topology has encountered in another register: vulnerability does not diminish the standing of the one who cannot answer. It makes heavier the responsibility of the one who can.
