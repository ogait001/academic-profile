## Abstract

[*When Is the Present?*](https://oscargaitan.org/metaphysics/ontology/philosophical%20theology/catholic%20philosophy/thomism/2026/07/03/when-is-the-present.html) and [*What Is the Future?*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophy-of-time/metaphysics/philosophy-of-religion/philosophical-theology/2026/08/18/what-is-the-future-en.html) settled that the path ahead is not a region but a horizon, made actual only at the crossing; [*What Is the Past?*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophy-of-time/metaphysics/philosophy-of-memory/philosophical-theology/2026/08/17/what-is-the-past-en.html) settled that the path behind is not a territory but a closed confluence condensed into the one who walks; and [*The Angle of Reception*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophy-of-religion/moral-psychology/philosophy-of-perception/2026/09/27/the-angle-of-reception-en.html) settled that this condensed past is the very shape through which the next world is received. Antonio Machado, who built no topology, said all three in ten lines — *se hace camino al andar*, the *senda* that is never trodden twice, the *estelas en la mar* — and this essay reads the poem back through the structure it already shows. Two results follow. First, Machado's *al volver la vista atrás* can be inverted: the traveler who turns to look at the road is not looking at it but through it, because the road has entered him as inheritance and become the lens of his looking. Second, set beside Matthew 9:5 — the two sayings, *Thy sins are forgiven thee* and *Arise and walk* — the Gospel's pairing discloses, in the vocabulary of [*The River That Returns to Its Course*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophy%20of%20religion/christian%20philosophy/catholic%20theology/2026/07/31/the-river-that-returns.html), the distinction between status, which grace reverses in a single Now, and structure, which the same traveler must still walk. From this comes the sentence the essay exists to state: grace does not give the traveler another past; it gives him another next step. The deed already walked is beyond editing, as [*The Ownerless Past*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophy-of-time/metaphysics/epistemology/2026/09/04/the-ownerless-past-en.html) showed; what forgiveness restores is not the road behind but the relation from which the same traveler, bearing the same road, may receive — and therefore walk — otherwise.

<br>

## I. What the Poem Already Sees

Antonio Machado's lines are quoted so often, and on so many walls, that it is easy to forget they are a piece of exact phenomenology.  

<br>

*Caminante, son tus huellas*      
*el camino, y nada más.*

<br>

Wayfarer, your footprints are the road, and nothing more. The poem denies, in its first breath, that there is a road laid out ahead to be found and followed. There is no *camino* prior to the *andar*. What there is, is *huellas*: the marks left where a foot has already fallen. And then the line that states the whole of it — *no hay camino, se hace camino al andar*. The path is not discovered; it is made, and made only in the making, at the point where the next step falls.

This corpus has a name for that point. It is the crossing — the Now, which has no sides and no thickness, at which alone anything is actualized. And it has a doctrine of what is actualized there. The future, When Is the Present? and What Is the Future? argued, is not a store of facts-in-waiting through which a traveler advances, but an open horizon determined in part at the crossing by three co-determinants meeting there — the word names a meeting, not a mechanism, for one of the three is the free response of the will: the traces the walker brings, the world's uninvited givens, and that response itself. Machado says the road is made in walking. The topology specifies the conditions under which that making occurs. The two are not rivals; they are a phenomenology and a metaphysics of one event, and the first can sometimes disclose what the second must labor to formulate. This essay does not conscript the poet into claims he did not make. It reads the structure his images already display, and finds that the structure is one this corpus built on other grounds.

<br>

## II. Footprints Are the Road

Begin with the first image, because it is more precise than it looks. *Son tus huellas el camino, y nada más*. The *huella* is, in this corpus's exact word, a trace — the deposit a completed act leaves in what comes after. Machado's claim that the footprints are the road, and nothing more, is the poetic form of two theses already defended here. That there is no pre-laid road is the denial the corpus made of the block: the future holds nothing laid out in advance, for if it did the walk would be the reading of a map and not the making of a path, and the will's response at the crossing would decide nothing. And that the road is *huellas* — trace and not territory — is the claim that what a walk leaves behind is deposit, not a standing place one could revisit.

So *se hace camino al andar* is the crossing, read in full. The next stretch of path is not found but actualized, and it is actualized of the three co-determinants at once. The traces the walker brings are the road already carved into his gait — the settled heading, the structural inertia that inclines the next step before he deliberates it. The world's givens are the terrain that was there before him, the slope and the stones and the weather, chosen by no one and received by all. And the response of the will is the one place where something not derivable from the road behind enters the road ahead. The path is therefore neither fate nor chance: not fate, which would be inheritance alone, the next step a corollary of the gait; not chance, which would be variation without gift, a swerve that enriches nothing. It is made — Machado's word — and made of inheritance and of what is received.

<br>

## III. The Senda That Is Never Trodden Twice

Then the poem turns the traveler around.

<br>

<br>

*Al andar se hace camino,*    
*y al volver la vista atrás,*    
*se ve la senda que nunca*    
*se ha de volver a pisar.*  

<br>

*And looking back, one sees the path one will never tread again*. Read flatly, the line places the road behind the walker, receding, lost to distance. The corpus will not read it flatly, because What Is the Past? established that the past is not behind and not elsewhere. The Now has no sides for a road to sit beside it the way a hallway sits beside a room; and the only past there is has already been folded, entire, into the Now that is. The question the line forces is therefore sharper than it first sounds: if the *senda* is not a place behind, why can it never be trodden again?

Not because it lies far off, out of reach by distance. Because it is closed. *What Is the Past?* named the operator of pastness exactly: closure is the cessation of reception at a crossing — the exhaustion of what that particular gathering had still to receive. The road walked is a confluence that has stopped receiving; to tread it again would be to reopen a crossing that has shut, which is not difficult but impossible, in the way that no decimal subdivision reopens the interval it has already completed. Machado's *nunca se ha de volver a pisar* is the poet's word for closure. The impossibility he records is not remoteness. It is that the crossing is finished, and a finished crossing is not far away — it is done.

<br>

## IV. The Road Has Entered the Walker

But closure removes nothing. A confluence closes the way a chord finishes sounding, not the way a document is shredded; what was gathered remains gathered, and only the gathering-in stops. What was walked, then, is not gone. It is condensed — carried, as trace, in the one who walked it. Closure is therefore not causal irrelevance: the closed crossing can receive no new act of becoming, but the trace it left is carried, operative, into every crossing that follows. And here is the inversion the poem invites and does not itself take.

The traveler turns — *al volver la vista atrás* — and believes he is looking at the road. He is not. The road is no longer an object out there to be looked at; it has become the shape of the one who looks. *The Angle of Reception* named this without the poem: the past is not what I look back upon; it is the shape through which I look. You do not consult your childhood, *What Is the Past?* said; you are inclined by it. The *senda* is not the object of the backward glance but the curvature of the glance itself. The traveler cannot find the road behind him because he is seeing by means of it; the backward glance mislocates what has become the shape of the glance. That it can never be trodden again does not follow from this, but from the prior fact of closure: the crossing is no longer open. The two facts meet in one consequence — there is no longer a road back there to tread, only a walker shaped like his walking.

<br>

## V. The Line Machado Did Not Need to Write

This permits a completion of the poem — offered, with care, in the register the poem declines. Machado writes *al andar se hace camino*, in walking the road is made, and stops where a poet rightly stops. The topology adds the half-line the image already carries and the poem had no need to state:

> *Al andar se hace camino,* ***and the road, once made, enters the one who walks on.***  

The footprint is not only pressed into the ground. Something answering to the footprint is pressed into the walker. The guard must be stated plainly, lest this read as a correction of Machado: it is not. A poem shows; it is under no obligation to state the metaphysics of what it shows, and the restraint is part of its truth. The added line is not better poetry — it is worse poetry and a piece of metaphysics, set down only to make explicit, for this corpus's purposes, the condensation the image already holds.

And the completion marks a difference the sea-image will press later. The *huella* on the path disperses — the rain and the years take it, and nothing out there preserves it. The *huella* in the walker does not disperse, and for the reason *What Is the Past?* gave: what was genuinely lived is not lost, not because it is filed somewhere safe behind the traveler, but because it was never left standing anywhere on its own to be lost. It is carried in the one thing still being sustained — the walker at his present step.

<br>

## VI. Every Step Has Two Components

If the path is made at the crossing, each step is a condensation, and the corpus has already said what a condensation is made of. [*The Weight of the Present*](https://oscargaitan.org/metaphysics/philosophical%20theology/ontology/catholic%20philosophy/thomism/2026/07/02/the-weight-of-the-present.html) and [*To Become Otherwise*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophy-of-time/philosophical-anthropology/philosophy-of-religion/2026/10/10/to-become-otherwise-en.html) distinguished its two principles: inheritance, which carries forward everything the walker has become by having walked; and reception, which takes up what is newly given at this step and could not have arisen from the road behind alone. These two do not displace the three co-determinants named at the first crossing but regroup them: inheritance names the traces the walker brings; reception names the event in which the world's uninvited given is taken up — or refused — by the will. The three were never three principles: inheritance is one principle; reception is the other, with the given as that which reception takes up or refuses. Both are required, and the poem needs both to mean what it says. Without inheritance there is no walker — only a first step repeated, a traveler minted new at every footfall, which is not a journey but a series of unrelated beginnings; this is replacement, which [*The Infinite Interior*](https://oscargaitan.org/ontology/philosophy-of-time/2026/05/05/the-infinite-interior.html) spent itself denying. Without reception there is no road-making at all — only the old road prolonged by rule, the next step a theorem of the last, which is fate, the horizon collapsed into the traces.

So *se hace camino al andar* is true, and double. The path is made in the Now; and it is made of two things, only one of which is the walker's own past. The other is given to him. Its highest case, this corpus has held from the first, is grace — the arrival of what could never have come from what has been walked alone. *To Become Otherwise* fixed the shape of the creature's part in it: not to manufacture what it receives, but to hold the hand open at the crossing, and to be constituted otherwise by what is placed there while remaining, without interruption, the same one who received it. Otherwise, and not another. That phrase will carry the rest of the essay.

<br>

## VII. The Two Sayings

Set the poem now beside Matthew 9:5. A paralytic is brought to Christ at Capernaum, and before He heals the body He forgives the sins, and to the scribes who murmur He puts a question: *Whether is easier, to say, Thy sins are forgiven thee: or to say, Arise, and walk?* The Gospel's own answer is that He speaks the visible word, the raising of the body, to prove His authority over the invisible one. The pericope is about authority, and this essay claims nothing against that reading and does not pretend to exhaust it.

A caution of method is owed here — the same two-register caution this corpus has made before. The distinction the essay is about to draw, between a status reversed in one Now and a structure walked over many, is a formal distinction and stands on its own. Its mapping onto these two sentences of Christ is an application, and the application is theological, supplied openly and not deduced from the structure. A reader who construes the sayings otherwise will reach other verdicts by the same method, which is the method working and not failing.

One thing more is owed, and it sharpens the argument rather than softening it. In the pericope itself, *Arise and walk* is not yet the figure this essay will make of it: the man is a paralytic, and his walking is a healing of the body, restored whole in one Now. The essay deliberately lifts the imperative *walk* out of that healed body, where it names an instantaneous restoration, and carries it into the moral path, where walking is the slow labor the rest of the essay describes. That the pericope pairs a word of forgiveness with a word of walking is the occasion; the content of the walking, once transposed, is supplied from elsewhere in the corpus, not read off the miracle.

<br>

## VIII. Status and the Road Behind

With that marked, read the two sayings in the corpus's key. *Thy sins are forgiven thee* touches what *The River That Returns to Its Course* and [*The Topology of Absolution*](https://oscargaitan.org/catholic-theology/ontology/philosophy-of-time/2026/06/15/the-topology-of-absolution.html) called status: guilt which, in the vocabulary of this corpus, is a relational boundary between the self and its ground and not a trace in the constitution of the self. A boundary can be lifted whole, in one Now, because guilt is that severed relation and not the disorder it leaves behind — which is why absolution does not wait on the slow interior, and why the thief on the cross, whose remaining earthly life offered no ordinary span in which such a channel could have been worn, received what no riverbed of his could have generated. But the lifting of guilt is not the whole of what grace does to the person, and the distinction must not be heard as though the sacrament were merely juridical. Absolution remits the guilt in one Now; it also restores the man to grace and strengthens him for the walk — while the *reliquiae peccati*, the disorder the deed has worn into him, it heals not at a stroke but along the slow path, as *The Angle of Reception*, and Aquinas before it, insist. The forgiveness of guilt is instantaneous; the transformation of the person is not exhausted by that instantaneous act. What the lifting of guilt does not do is unmake a single footprint of the road the walker took to reach it. That is the first saying — real and complete as the reversal of status, and not yet the end of the Gospel sentence.

<br>

## IX. Arise and Walk

Because the second saying is also spoken. *Arise, and walk.* The relation is restored; now the restored one must walk — and walking, as this essay has established, is the making of the path through reception, inheritance and gift meeting at each crossing. The forgiven traveler is not handed a new road already carved in the direction of the good. He is handed the next step, and the step after it, each to be received. *The Angle of Reception* said exactly why the road behind is not thereby re-laid: absolution lifts the boundary but does not regrind the lens, so the same traveler meets the same world through the same curvature his walking carved, and the promotion still arrives pre-bent, the neighbor still arrives half-accused — until, crossing by crossing, the angle is re-formed. *The River That Returns to Its Course* said exactly how the re-forming is done: not from the bank, by a word that commands the river to turn, but down in the bed, grace deepening, with the walker's own repeated presence, the channel that will one day be the deeper one. The repeated walking does not manufacture the grace; it is the creature's participation in a grace already given — the opened hand of *To Become Otherwise*, not a work that earns what is placed in it.

So *Arise and walk* is the sending of the same man, with his road intact beneath him, into the one labor forgiveness does not perform in his place. And relapse, on this reading, is not the failure of the forgiveness. It is a micro-abdication at one crossing of the ongoing walk — a single Now, tired or wounded or distracted, in which the deliberate step did not appear in time, and the carved road, undisplaced, ran where it always ran. The forgiven man who finds himself again on the old path has not lost what was restored. He has discovered how many more steps he must take before the new channel becomes the one the water more readily takes.

<br>

## X. Grace Gives No New Past

Now the sentence the essay exists to state, and the distinction it rests on. The temptation, hearing *Thy sins are forgiven thee*, is to say that grace changes the past. This corpus cannot say it. *The Ownerless Past* established, against every counterfeit, that the deed has withdrawn into its own completion and is beyond editing: what has happened, happened, and nothing — not omnipotence, because a past unmade is not a feat withheld but a contradiction — will make it not have happened. The road walked was walked. The footprints are where they fell. Forgiveness does not reach back and un-walk it, and the corpus would sooner lose the comfort than falsify the metaphysics.

What forgiveness does is stranger and larger. It does not give the traveler another past; it gives the traveler another next step. The road behind is left exactly as it was, every footprint counted, and the relation from which the next footprint is received is restored — so that the same road, borne by the same walker, inclines toward a step it could not have inclined toward an hour before. Grace changes not the past, but what the inherited past is permitted to become at the next crossing. The word permitted must be heard narrowly, lest it reach too far: what has been was, and its having-been is untouched. What changes is not what the past was but what the inherited past is still able to become within the next act of the person — its structural determinacy over the coming crossing, not its settled content.

Mark the precise contrast, because the whole severity of the claim is in it. *The Ownerless Past* described an operation that works on exactly this seam and works it the opposite way. The counterfeiter substitutes the trace: he leaves the deed untouched and unreachable, and overwrites the record, so that the walker is made, in the only register others can reach, to have walked a road he never walked. Grace is that operation's structural counter-image — its answer in the opposite direction. The counterfeit falsifies the trace and leaves the relation dead; grace leaves the trace entirely truthful — the deed admitted, the road owned, the footprints uncancelled — and restores the relation. The counterfeit gives a false past and no next step worth taking. Grace gives the true past, unedited, and a true next step. Forgiveness is not the substitution of the road. It is the restoration of the one who walked it, to walk on.

Scripture carries a grammar of walking that marks the same distinction from another side. In Genesis 24:40 Abraham speaks, in one English rendering, of the Lord before whom he has walked; others keep the present form — before whom I walk, in whose sight I walk. The variance need not be made to carry an exegetical argument it was never framed to bear. What both forms presuppose is enough: the one who has walked before God is the one who stands before Him now. The steps already walked are over, and they are not a road Abraham could return to and tread again; yet neither are they absent from the one whose walking they were. The words before whom I have walked still belong truthfully to the present man because the past has closed without becoming ownerless — the deed finished, the trace truthful, the walker still its bearer. The path is finished; the walker who bears it remains before the One before whom he walked.

The past remains constitutive without remaining determinative: it belongs to the person who walks, without possessing the next crossing in advance. This is the sense in which the apostle can forget what lies behind and strain forward to what lies ahead. The forgetting is not the erasure of the closed past, which no one can perform and the corpus will not pretend to; it is what *What Is the Past?* found in Isaiah's remember not the former things — a command addressed not to the trace but to the grip, not to constitution but to the backward fixation of memory that keeps returning to a closed crossing as though it were still open. To forget what lies behind is to refuse the closed road as the measure of the open horizon. Paul presses forward precisely as the same one who has come through what lies behind — his road condensed in him, unedited, and no longer permitted to set the direction of the next step.

<br>

## XI. Estelas en la mar

The poem's last image is the hardest, because at first it seems to contradict the corpus.

<br>

*Caminante, son tus huellas*      
*el camino, y nada más.*    


<br> 

There is no road, only wakes on the sea. A wake is the purest case of the un-re-walkable: the water closes behind the hull and keeps no print, the *senda* on the sea is gone the instant it is cut, and the boat is only ever at its present bow, cutting water it has never cut. Read one way this is the asymptote of *What Is the Future?*, exactly: the open sea ahead is received only by being made into the Now of the bow; the horizon recedes at the rate of every advance and is reached at no point, because to reach it is to have cut it.

But the sea seems to say more than the corpus allows — that the past is lost, dispersed, kept nowhere. Here the two images of the poem must be held together rather than played against each other. On the water, yes: the wake disperses, and there is no *senda* out there to return to — which is only the point already won, that the past is not a place behind. But the voyage is not kept on the water; it is kept in the one who sailed it, in the heading he now holds. *Estelas en la mar* is true of the road-as-place: gone, un-re-walkable, no territory behind — and answered by the corpus's claim that what was genuinely sailed is not lost, because it was never left standing on the water to be lost. The wake vanishes. The voyage is condensed in the voyager. And the only water that can be cut is the water at the bow — which is to say the next step, which is to say the Now.

<br>

## XII. A Formal Statement

Everything above compresses into one statement, offered here as the claim the essay exists to make. The path is neither laid out ahead nor left standing behind. It is made at the crossing, of inheritance and reception, and what is made condenses into the one who walks, becoming the shape through which the next stretch of world is received. The road already walked cannot be trodden again — not because it lies far behind, but because it is closed, and has been condensed into the walker. Forgiveness lifts the boundary between the walker and his ground in a single Now, leaving every footprint of the road intact and unedited, and sends the same traveler to walk otherwise; it does not give him another past but another next step, because the deed is beyond revision and only the next reception is presently open. *Arise and walk* is therefore not the abolition of the path already walked, nor the gift of a new one already carved, but the restoration of the relation from which the same traveler, bearing the same road, may receive — and so walk — otherwise.

<br>

## XIII. Coda

The traveler turns, *al volver la vista atrás*, to look at the road he has walked, and finds no road. He finds himself: the *senda* folded into the one standing at the bow, the whole walked distance condensed into the shape of his looking. He is not told, and was never going to be told, that he has been given another road to have walked. The footprints are where they fell; the wake is cut; nothing will make the walked distance other than it was. He is told the one thing that leaves the past true and the next step open. *Arise, and walk.* And he walks onto the next water, which has never been walked and cannot be walked twice, and which closes behind him — not into a road he could return to, but into the shape of the one who goes on.
