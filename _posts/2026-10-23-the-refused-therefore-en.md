## Note on Sources and Method

This essay is a revision — and in one respect a retraction — of an earlier essay, [*Eating Stones*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophy%20of%20religion/philosophical%20theology/metaphysics/christian%20philosophy/ontology/2026/06/29/eating-stones.html), written in June of 2026, before the October sequence on change and persistence had given the corpus the instruments this question requires. The earlier essay reached a conclusion I still hold and reached it by a mechanism I no longer can. It argued that to command a stone to become bread would be to set the form of bread over a reality that remains stone — a mask laid upon a surviving subject. That picture cannot survive [*Idem, an alius?*](https://oscargaitan.org/metaphysics/ontology/numerical-identity/2026/10/22/idem-an-alius-en.html), and the first task below is to retire it honestly. What takes its place is not a weaker claim but a more exact one, and it is drawn entirely from tools the corpus forged after June.

The essay presupposes rather than re-derives four results. From [*Quid est mutatio?*](https://oscargaitan.org/metaphysics/ontology/philosophy-of-time/2026/10/18/quid-est-mutatio-en.html) it takes the definition of change as the becoming-otherwise of one numerically continuous subject — the same, otherwise — held apart from replacement, A → B, in which numerical identity does not cross the arrow; and it takes that essay's deliberate bracketing of coming-to-be and passing-away, the case where the question is precisely whether the same subject stands on both sides of the transition. From *Idem, an alius?* it takes the structure of substitution — replacement offered as the continuation of a subject — though, as the body will show, the wilderness proposal is not an instance of that structure but a neighbor of it. From [*The Infinite Interior*](https://oscargaitan.org/ontology/philosophy-of-time/2026/05/05/the-infinite-interior.html) it takes continuity as an unbroken interior and the history by which a continuant is the one it is — carefully distinguished, below, from the nature by which a thing is the kind it is. From [*To Become Otherwise*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophy-of-time/philosophical-anthropology/philosophy-of-religion/2026/10/10/to-become-otherwise-en.html) it takes reception, the operation on which the final sections turn.

Aristotle and Aquinas are engaged here as company on the road — on act and potency, on generation and corruption, and on the reach of omnipotence — and not as authorities from which the argument descends. On one point the debt must be named as a divergence rather than a borrowing: in the Summa (III, q. 41, a. 4) Aquinas locates the disorder of the first temptation in the needless working of a miracle for bodily provision, and does not say that changing the stones would falsify the being of the stone; indeed he grants that Christ, had He done it, would not for that reason have sinned. This essay follows him on divine power, on act and potency, and on the moral structure of the temptation, and parts from him only in asking a further question his treatment there does not raise — whether the topology permits a distinction, between the becoming of a subject and the replacement of one substance by another, that governs how the command should be read. Where the argument reaches the Son before the Father, it marks, as the corpus always marks, the line between a philosophical proposition and a doctrinal reading, and claims only the weaker and surer form.

<br>

## Abstract

The first temptation in the wilderness has been read as a test of hunger, of obedience, of the right use of power. An earlier essay, *Eating Stones*, added a metaphysical reading and spoiled it with a false mechanism: that the commanded bread would be a mask over a stone that remained. But honest replacement is no counterfeit — the table does not lie about the tree — and if the stone genuinely ceased and bread genuinely came to be, no mask would remain to accuse. The argument therefore relocates, and narrows. The power at issue is real: God does not lack the strength to end a stone and bring bread into being. But to bring bread into being is not what the command asks; it asks that these stones become it — and where the stone, as this substantial kind, has no natural potency to become bread as one of its own determinations, the event cannot be the alteration of that stone-subject but only the generation of another substance in its place. So the metaphysics yields not that God could not, but that the proposed act is not the becoming of this subject, however the verb may dress it. That result does its real work one step further on. Satan's premise is true — you are the Son — and the power it ascribes is real; the counterfeit is in the «therefore»: the demand that the Son enact who He is through an act performed to prove His Sonship, on a condition supplied from outside the filial relation. The refusal is at once metaphysical and filial, and the two are one: because the act cannot be the becoming of the stone, nothing about the stone compels it, and its whole weight falls on the «therefore» the Son will not take from the Tempter's mouth. He remained hungry.

<br>

## I. The Earlier Reading, and Why It Must Be Retired

The June essay built its case on a distinction between transformation and substitution. Transformation was a continuous traversal, the interior carried across; substitution was the deletion of one meaning and the insertion of another beneath an unchanged surface — a stone wearing the mask of bread. To command the stone, it concluded, would be to perform the second while calling it the first: the form of bread set over a reality that remains stone, so that what Christ received would still be stone in its meaning, and He would be eating stones.

Read with the instruments of October, that mechanism fails, and it fails from within. The essay could not hold its own description steady. In a single sentence it said that the stone's whole history would be wiped and rewritten — which is annihilation — and then that the result would be a substitution, the form of bread set over a reality that remains stone — which is residue. These are not two phrasings of one event. Either the stone ceases, in which case nothing remains to wear a mask, or the stone remains, in which case nothing has been wiped. The essay convicted the act of being a mask over residue while reserving the right to describe it as a wiping, and the conviction depended on the very residue it had elsewhere denied.

*Idem, an alius?* is the essay that makes this visible, because it drew the line the June essay lacked. There it is laid down that replacement as such is not a lie: honest replacement, openly declared, is no counterfeit, for the table that comes from the tree does not present itself as a later phase of the tree. If, then, the commanded stone genuinely ceased to be and genuine bread came to be in its place, there would be no stone left within, no mask, no surviving subject made to profess a vocation not its own. The whole machinery of the June argument — interior overwritten, appearance imposed, creature made to lie — requires a subject that stays to do the lying. Grant honest cessation and the machinery has nothing to grip. The earlier mechanism is therefore retired: not because its prose aged, but because the corpus has since discovered the distinction that dissolves it.

<br>

## II. What the Bracket Left Open

To retire the mechanism is not to concede the point, and it is here that haste does its damage. One is tempted to reason: *Quid est mutatio?* expressly set aside coming-to-be and passing-away; *Idem* expressly cleared honest replacement of the charge of counterfeit; therefore the stone ceasing and bread arising is an honest coming-to-be, and the temptation, if it was one at all, lay only in the relation — in the if you are the Son — and not in the thing proposed. The reasoning overshoots at its first step, and the overshoot is instructive.

Quid did not license coming-to-be; it bracketed it. To set a question aside is to decline to answer it, not to answer it in the permissive direction. The sentence reads that the essay does not attempt a theory of coming-to-be or passing-away, where the question is precisely whether the same subject stands on both sides of the transition. The open question is the problem, not a dispensation from it. What the later corpus offers the wilderness is not a verdict of innocence but a sharper question, and the sections that follow put it: not whether the verb says become — it does, and says no more — but what it would mean for these stones, these subjects, to become bread, and what rests on the answer.

<br>

## III. The Grammar, and What It Cannot Carry

Return to what is said, in the words in which it is said. Matthew: εἰπὲ ἵνα οἱ λίθοι οὗτοι ἄρτοι γένωνται — say that these stones become loaves. Luke, in the singular: εἰπὲ τῷ λίθῳ τούτῳ ἵνα γένηται ἄρτος — tell this stone that it become bread. The verb is from γίνομαι, and the grammar places a weight it is tempting to seize: the stone is the subject of the verb of becoming. One could argue from this that Satan demands idem, aliter — the same stone, otherwise — where only alius, deinde is possible, and that the counterfeit therefore lies in the verb itself.

The temptation must be refused, for γίνομαι will not bear it. Its range is broad — become, come to be, happen — and Scripture uses it for a becoming the corpus honors without hesitation: at Cana the steward tastes τὸ ὕδωρ οἶνον γεγενημένον, the water become wine, the same verb in the same construction. If X-becomes-Y carried the indictment, it would convict Cana along with the stones. So the grammar settles nothing by itself. What it gives is a clue and no more: Satan's words are cast as a becoming of these stones, not as a request that bread be created in their place — he frames a replacement in the idiom of an alteration. Whether that framing hides anything, and what, is a question the verb cannot answer and the metaphysics must. It is to the metaphysics, and not the Greek, that the next sections turn.

<br>

## IV. The Stone Without the Natural Potency of Bread

Why can the command not be obeyed as what it says — a genuine becoming of these stones? Because mutatio, in the strict sense this corpus has fixed, requires one numerically continuous subject of whom the earlier and the later determinations are both true; potency enters afterward, to explain how the later determination becomes actual in that subject. And the stone, as this substantial kind, has no natural potency to become bread as one of its own determinations. The water warms, the tree grows, the person turns: in each, a determination becomes actual in a subject that stands, continuous, on both sides of it. Bread is no such determination of stone. There is no phase of a stone, however deep in its interior or far along its course, at which bread is simply the next thing it is coming to be. The arc of a stone's becoming does not run toward bread, and no continuous stone-subject stands on the far side of these stones became bread to have become it.

It may be answered — with Aquinas, who holds that divine power reaches the passive potentiality of matter and can move it immediately to a form the ordinary causes would otherwise produce — that at the floor of things one matter underlies both, and that God can reconfigure it from the one to the other without waiting on nature. Grant it entirely; it does not touch the claim, because that reconfiguration is not change. The tradition engaged here as company — Aristotle on generation and corruption, and Aquinas after him — keeps generation-and-corruption apart from alteration on exactly this point: the coming-to-be of one substance and the passing-away of another is not the motion of one continuant but the replacement of one substantial form by another, the terminus at which change ends rather than a stretch it crosses. The matter that underlies the swap is no this, no subject that was otherwise and is otherwise; it persists only as the indeterminate ground of the exchange, not as the one of whom the before and the after are both truly said. So even granting the deepest continuity the physics allows and the fullest reach of divine power over matter, these stones becoming bread names no mutatio. It names a generation and a corruption — A → B — and nothing in it is the becoming of this subject. The question was never what God can do; it is what it would mean to say that these stones became bread, and the answer is that the saying predicates of the stone a becoming that belongs to no subject the stone ever was.

<br>

## V. History and Nature: What the Interior Fixes

The June essay reached for *The Infinite Interior* here, and reached wrongly, in a way that must be corrected rather than repeated. It wrote as though the history by which bread ordinarily comes to be — soil and seed, rain and harvest, the grinding, the leaven, the heat — were constitutive of breadhood itself, so that a loaf produced without that history would not be bread. That cannot be right, and the paragraph to follow shows what it would cost. What *The Infinite Interior* fixes is not the essence but the career: the unbroken interior is what makes the later phase of a thing continuous with its earlier, the history by which this subject is the one it is. It individuates a continuant; it does not legislate what kind a thing must be, nor forbid that a thing of some kind be brought to be otherwise than nature brings it.

Set that straight and the real point stands clear of the objection that sank the old one. Nothing here forbids God to create bread — real bread, whole in its nature, as creation gives a thing both being and kind; freshly made bread is no less bread for never having passed through harvest, and the June essay already granted that the multiplied loaves, each genuinely bread, falsify nothing. Provenance is not the question. The question is to whom the becoming belongs. For bread to come to be where the stones were is one thing; for these stones to have become it is another, and only the second is commanded. No replacement becomes the continuation of the stone's career by receiving the name bread — a loaf set in the stone's place, however real as bread, is not the stone grown otherwise but another thing begun. Satan did not ask for bread to be created. He asked that a becoming be spoken over stones that owns no subject on the far side of it.

<br>

## VI. Cana, and the Line the Metaphysics Will Not Draw

This is the moment the June essay's exemption of the true miracles comes due, and it cannot be paid in the coin it reached for. That essay drew the line at process, calling Cana an accelerated traversal to keep water-into-wine clear of a rule against becoming at a word. The line was misdrawn, and the metaphysics just set out will not redraw it in the same place. For Cana is, in the very grammar this essay has declined to lean on, a becoming — the water become wine — and it is, at the level of substance, as much a production of a new kind from underlying matter as the bread would be. If these stones became bread names a generation and not an alteration, then, strictly, so does the water became wine. The multiplied loaves are an increase of what is already bread; the rock at Horeb stays rock and yields real water, claiming nothing; but Cana will not be sorted onto the safe side of a purely metaphysical line, because there is no purely metaphysical line that keeps it there.

The honest conclusion is that the difference between the miracle the Lord works and the act the Tempter proposes is not, in the end, a difference in the metaphysics of the becoming. Both would be genuine productions; both can be described as a becoming of X into Y. What the metaphysics establishes is narrower, and still necessary: that the proposed act is not the alteration of the stone-subject, so that nothing about the stone compels or explains it, and the whole weight of the proposal rests elsewhere than on the stone. Where it rests is the question the last sections take up. The line between Cana and the wilderness is not in the stone or the water. It is in the «therefore».

<br>

## VII. Fittingness, Not Impossibility

It must be said plainly, against the June essay's own overstatement, that none of this is a limit on the power of God. The earlier essay let slip that for Christ to make the stone bread would itself have been a contradiction of divine truth, and that is too strong. A contradiction is not a feat God is too weak to perform; it is no object of power at all, and the omnipotence the tradition confesses is in no way diminished by its not extending to what cannot be. But a stone ending and bread beginning is no contradiction. God could end these stones; God could bring bread to be. The only impossibility in the neighborhood is narrow and conceptual: that one and the same act be both a replacement and the alteration of the subject it replaces. That alone cannot be — not because God is weak before it, but because it is nothing, a description that eats itself.

So the register is fittingness, not impossibility, and the June essay should have kept it there throughout. But fittingness attaches to the act under its description, not to the bare rearrangement of matter — and the description that makes this act unfitting is not bread made from stone but Sonship proved by bread made from stone on a condition set by the Tempter. The corpus has a name for signs pressed to say what they have lost the standing to say: [*The Inverse Miracle*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophy%20of%20religion/philosophical%20theology/metaphysics/christian%20philosophy/ontology/2026/06/30/the-inverse-miracle.html) described the sacramental sign made to signify while its bond to the signified is cut, and *Idem* generalized it to every sign of continuity marshalled where the continuity is absent. The unfittingness here is of that family, but its seat is not the loaf. It is the inference the loaf would be made to serve — and that inference is the subject of the sections that remain.

<br>

## VIII. "If You Are the Son of God"

Now the essay reaches its center, the point toward which the metaphysics was only clearing the ground. Satan's premise is not idle: if you are the Son of God. The conditional is often read as a taunt of doubt; but whatever Satan's own certainty, the temptation takes its force from the Sonship it presupposes, not from any question about it — for an ordinary man cannot be asked to command stones. The conditional is not a doubt but a lever, and its full shape is: you are the Son; «therefore» use what you are to provide for yourself, here, on the condition I set. The attack is on the «therefore». The premise is true and the power is real — that is what makes the attack formidable — and neither the truth of the one nor the reality of the other yields the conclusion. It takes a true identity and a real power and tries to make them the premise of an act performed on the Tempter's terms.

This is where *To Become Otherwise* bears on the wilderness, and it must be said with care, on the doctrinal and not the philosophical side of the line. The Son is eternally begotten, not constituted by any reception; in the Incarnation, however, He possesses a true human will, and according to that human will freely obeys the mission given by the Father — willing humanly, in unbroken harmony with His divine will, what He wills divinely with the Father. Satan proposes the counterfeit of that obedience: not the taking-up of what the Father gives, but self-provision on a condition supplied from outside the filial relation. And here the metaphysics returns, not as a rival to this reading but as its ground. The act Satan names cannot be the stone's mutatio — a becoming owned by the continuous stone-subject; it can only be a production the Son would perform to prove His Sonship. So the «therefore» asks the Son to manifest who He is through an act that answers to the Tempter's condition rather than the Father's gift — to make His identity the servant of a premise set outside it. He refuses, not because He lacks the power the premise correctly ascribes, but because the one who is the Son does not take the manner of His Sonship from the mouth that tempts Him. The Church reads the temptation just here — not whether Christ possesses divine power, but how the Son enacts His mission in relation to the Father — and this essay, marking its line as doctrine and not demonstration, claims only the weaker and surer form: that the refusal of the stones and the refusal of the «therefore» are one refusal, because the stones were never the point.

<br>

## IX. Not by Bread Alone

The June essay's title named a literal thing: Christ eating stone within a borrowed form. That meaning is gone with the mechanism — were real bread brought to be, He would eat bread. What the old image was reaching for survives only as this: to eat that loaf would be to take, as food, the fruit of an act performed to prove His Sonship on the Tempter's terms — to be nourished by the very «therefore» He came to refuse. The temptation's object was never the meal, but the condition the meal was to be had on.

So His answer has its exact force. Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceeds from the mouth of God. Bread is real bread, and He does not deny He could have it; every word that proceeds from the mouth of God points instead to the Father whose mission the incarnate Word receives and fulfills, and whose gift He will not exchange for the Tempter's condition. To live by that Word is to refuse every sustenance had by making what He is the servant of another's command. The refusal and the reply are one: He will not make His bread the proof of His Sonship at another's word, and He will not eat what that word would set before Him.

<br>

## X. The «Therefore» the Son Refused

The temptation in the wilderness was not, at its root, the lure of a mask over a surviving stone, nor even a demand for an act God could not perform. It was the demand that the Son take the manner of His Sonship from the Tempter's mouth — that a true identity and a real power be made to yield a «therefore» the Father never spoke. He refused, and the refusal was not the finding of a limit but the keeping of a sonship. He did not deny that He could end a stone or bring bread to be. He denied that what He is could be turned into the servant of a condition set outside the Father's gift.

He was hungry, and He remained hungry, and the hunger changed nothing, because the hunger was never the premise at issue. The power was His, and the bread could have been real; what could not be made real was the Tempter's conclusion — that because He is the Son, He must prove it here, on this condition, at this word. The voice would have been the Son's own voice, and the deed a real deed — and He would not lend either to a sentence whose conclusion was not the Father's.

The premise was true; the power was real; the inference was the Tempter's.

Filius Dei es — non ideo.
