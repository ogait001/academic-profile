## Note on Sources and Method

This essay asks what a creature's freedom may do with a constitutive actuality of itself whose source is not itself. It does not re-derive the results it stands on. From [*Who Was Before Me, or Myself?*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophical-theology/philosophy-of-mind/thomas-aquinas/2026/10/17/who-was-before-me-or-myself-en.html) it takes the distinction between derivation and traversal, and the self that has no earlier self behind it. From [*Does Time Need Me, or Do I Need Time?*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophy-of-time/ontology/2026/04/10/does-time-need-me-or-do-i-need-time.html) and [*What Sustains the Now?*](https://oscargaitan.org/metaphysics/philosophical-theology/philosophy-of-god/2026/09/18/what-sustains-the-now-en.html) it takes the Non-Derivative Ground and constitutive dependence — that the subject is held in being by what its own becoming cannot supply. From [*The Weight of the Present*](https://oscargaitan.org/metaphysics/philosophical%20theology/ontology/catholic%20philosophy/thomism/2026/07/02/the-weight-of-the-present.html) and [*Whose Condensation Am I?*](https://oscargaitan.org/phenomenology/christian-theology/philosophical-anthropology/2026/08/07/whose-condensation-am-i-en.html) it takes condensation and confluence, the body received as what one is and not authored as what one prefers. From [*To Become Otherwise*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophy-of-time/philosophical-anthropology/philosophy-of-religion/2026/10/10/to-become-otherwise-en.html) it takes reception, the operation by which a subject takes up what is given and is otherwise without becoming another. From [*Do I Have a Say?*](https://oscargaitan.org/metaphysics/philosophical-anthropology/ethics/2026/10/07/do-i-have-a-say-en.html) it takes, formally, the body as what I am rather than what I hold, and the will as answerability rather than dominion. From [*The Refused «Therefore»*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophical-theology/metaphysics/ontology/2026/10/23/the-refused-therefore-en.html) it takes the distinction between the career of a subject and the nature of its kind. And from [*Against You Alone*](https://oscargaitan.org/catholic-theology/philosophy-of-time/ontology/2026/05/08/against-you-alone.html) it takes non serviam, the final refusal, of which the posture named here is a particular mode. It presupposes throughout, with the whole corpus, the metaphysical realism and natural teleology already operative there — that things have natures, and that a nature carries an order of flourishing proper to it — which the essay uses and does not re-prove.

One methodological limit governs the whole, and it is the mirror of the discipline the corpus keeps elsewhere. The essay closes a metaphysical question — whether the creature can become the Ground or author of the nature it receives — and it withholds, on the same principle that forbids the proof to outrun its premises, every judgment of persons: it does not read any individual's interior life as the posture it describes, does not pronounce on culpability, and does not adjudicate the pastoral or the clinical particulars. Its object throughout is the logic of a proposed resolution, never the heart of anyone who might be thought to hold it. Catholic theology supplies that very distinction — an objective disorder of relation and personal culpability are not the same thing — and the essay keeps it without apology, because the structure it analyzes is a structure, not a diagnosis.

First-person testimony keeps the office an earlier inquiry gave it. That a person may experience the sexed body as alien, painfully and involuntarily and without having chosen the conflict, is real, and is owed its full hearing; nothing here treats that experience as illusion or as itself the posture the essay names. What is examined is not the experience but a particular account of what may be done in answer to it — and whether that account asks the will to occupy an office no creature holds.

Where the argument meets the teaching of the Church — that 'being man' and 'being woman' are good realities willed by God, that each person is called to acknowledge and accept his or her sexual identity, and that creation is prior to our decisions and must be received as a gift — it marks, as the corpus always marks, the line between a philosophical proposition and a doctrinal reading, and claims only the weaker and surer form. The epigraph follows the traditional Vulgate and Clementine Psalter, «et non ipsi nos»; the Masoretic text preserves the well-known ketiv/qere here, its written reading agreeing with 'and not we ourselves' while the marginal reading gives 'and we are his,' which the Nova Vulgata follows with «et ipsius sumus». The essay leans on the sense the traditional Latin preserves, and nothing in the argument hangs on the variant. A prior study of the adjacent question, *Mas, femina, quid?*, remains unpublished and is not built upon here; the present essay begins from different and, the author judges, surer premises.

## Abstract

The ordinary form of the question is downstream and misleads: may I alter what I did not make? The prior question is what the will's relation to a constitutive given is at all, when the source of that given is not the will. This essay answers from premises the corpus has already earned. I do not ground myself; I encounter myself first as received actuality; my freedom is therefore exercised within what is received, not prior to it as a rival act of making. The governing opposition is accordingly not change against no-change — for the creature alters what it did not author constantly and rightly, healing a bone it did not make, disciplining appetites it did not give itself — but reception against repudiation.

Stewardship is not authorship. The will may act upon the given to serve its good; what it cannot do is become the Ground from which the given takes its nature. To receive is not to remain untouched; it is to refuse to mistake one's agency for authorship of the reality upon which that agency acts. Repudiation is the act that crosses this line: it places the given itself under the will's judgment, so that what I am must receive its truth from me. That inversion is the attempt of the derivative to occupy the office of the Non-Derivative, and the essay names it Non accipio me — the refusal to receive oneself as given — a particular mode of the older non serviam. The doctrine of the Fall supplies its structural precedent: not that nature rebelled, for nature has no will, but that the creature's rebellion wounds the harmonious reception of self and world.

The result is closed where the topology can close it and bounded where it cannot. The metaphysical conclusion is firm: no creaturely act makes the received nature take its terms from the will; the will answers, it does not found. From this a criterion for action upon the body follows — whether the act serves the good and order of the nature received, or requires the will to become the source of a contrary account of it — and the essay states the criterion and its clear poles while declining to sort the genuinely hard cases from the armchair, leaving those to prudence, to medicine, and to pastoral care. And throughout, the metaphysical closure is held strictly apart from any judgment of persons, which the essay does not make.

<br>

## I. The Question Behind the Question

The question usually arrives in a form that has already gone wrong. May I alter what I did not make? — and at once the dispute is about alteration, about what the will may do to a thing, as though the thing were simply there, an object in the hand, and the only question were the reach of the hand. But that framing has smuggled in the whole matter under dispute. It treats the self as material first and received second, when the order of being is the reverse. Before the will can be asked what it may do to its own nature, a prior question must be answered: what is the will's relation to that nature at all, given that the will did not supply it?

The corpus has a habit of finding that the hard question lies one step upstream of the loud one, and it does again here. The loud question — change or no change — cannot be primary, because the creature alters what it did not author at every hour of its life, and rightly. It sets a bone it did not make. It disciplines appetites it did not give itself. It educates capacities it received unformed. If 'I did not author it' entailed 'I may not touch it,' medicine and asceticism and education would all stand condemned together, which is absurd. So the inference from unauthored to inviolable is too broad, and the essay does not make it. The real line falls elsewhere, and finding where is the whole work.

The prior question, then, is not about the permission to act but about the standing of the one who acts. I arrive at my own freedom already constituted — embodied, sexed, placed, inheriting a nature I did not convene. The question is what freedom is, exercised by one who is, in this way, given to himself before he is anything to himself. That is a question the corpus is equipped to answer without borrowing a single premise from the controversy the answer will eventually touch.

<br>

## II. I Do Not Ground Myself

Begin where the corpus has stood from the beginning. I cannot ground my own next instant. The actuality by which I am here at all, now, is not supplied by me; it is held open by what *Does Time Need Me?* and *What Sustains the Now?* called the non-derivative Ground, the I AM WHO I AM that is not a member of the series it sustains. This is not a claim about my origin only, as though I were made once and then left to myself; it is a claim about every moment of my continuance, which is constitutive dependence and not a debt long since paid. I do not, at any instant, author the being by which I am.

From this the essay takes one consequence and no more than one. If I do not supply the actuality by which I am, then I do not meet myself, first, as something I have made or am making. I meet myself as received — as a given actuality that is already the case when my freedom turns to it. *Who Was Before Me, or Myself?* fixed that there is no earlier self standing behind the present one, authoring it; there is only a beginning given. *The Weight of the Present* and *Whose Condensation Am I?* fixed that the body in which I am this one is confluence, inheritance and reception gathered into a constitution I did not convene, constitutive of me rather than owned by me. Put together: the subject does not precede, as a bare will, the nature it then selects. The nature is given with the subject, and the will wakes inside it.

So freedom is real, and the essay will not diminish it; but its place must be stated exactly. Freedom is not a faculty that meets the self as raw material and confers a form upon it. It is the response of one who is already formed enough to respond — the uninherited answer at the crossing, as the corpus has always called it, which is answerability and not authorship. My freedom operates within reception. It does not operate prior to it, as a rival act of creation, because there is no vantage prior to my own given actuality from which I could create the one doing the creating: the self-author would have to be there already to author himself, and that he is already there is exactly what he did not author.

<br>

## III. Stewardship Is Not Authorship

Here the decisive distinction must be drawn, because everything turns on keeping two things apart that the loud question runs together. To act upon what one has received is one thing; to become the source of what that received thing is, is another. The first is stewardship, and it is wide. The second is authorship, and it belongs, over a nature, to no creature at all.

*Do I Have a Say?* drew the formal shape of this and the present essay inherits it, formally and not morally. There the body was fixed as what I am and not what I hold — constitutive of the embodied person rather than a chattel an interior self owns and operates — and the one thing genuinely mine was named as the response of the will, mine analogically, as answerability rather than dominion. Carry that here. Because the body is what I am, my relation to it is not the relation of an owner to a possession he may dispose of as he pleases; it is the relation of a steward to a reality entrusted — analogically, for no interior proprietor stands over the body here; the embodied person is entrusted to himself, under the One from whom he has his being — which he may serve, tend, heal, and govern, and for which he answers. Stewardship is genuine authority. It is simply not the authority to determine what the entrusted thing is.

This is why the inference from unauthored to inviolable fails: I did not author my body, and I may still act upon it, widely and for its good; what I may not do is make my act the source from which my body's nature takes its terms. The distinction is not change against no-change. It is answering against authoring. The will may answer the given with every resource stewardship allows; it cannot become the Ground of the given, and the whole of what follows is the drawing-out of that single impossibility.

<br>

## IV. Reception and Its Counterfeit

The opposition the essay has been approaching can now be named in its proper terms. It is not change against stasis; it is reception against repudiation. Both are acts of a will upon a given it did not make. They are distinguished not by whether the given is touched — both touch it — but by the standing each accords to what is given.

Reception acts upon the given in order to serve its good. It presupposes that the given has a nature and an order of its own, and it works within that order, toward it. And this presupposes, in turn, what the corpus's realism has always required and does not derive anew here: that a nature is not a bare descriptive regularity but carries an intelligible order of flourishing proper to the kind of thing it is — for otherwise stewardship would have nothing to serve and healing nothing to heal toward. The essay takes that order as a prior, as it takes the Non-Derivative, and on it rests the distinction, drawn below, between the good of a received nature and every contingent state in which that nature may be found. This is the structure of healing: medicine does not invent the body's form, it presupposes it, and sets the bone toward the wholeness the bone's own nature already specifies. It is the structure of discipline, which does not invent the good of the appetites but orders them toward it. It is the structure of every genuine cultivation, which draws out of a received nature what that nature was already for. In all of these the will is answering — serving a good it did not legislate, within a reality whose terms it receives.

And one distinction must be made explicit here, or the account invites an obvious objection. Not everything factually received is thereby normative: a wound, a disease, a malformation is given, yet does not belong to the good toward which the received nature is ordered. Reception is fidelity not to every present state but to the good and order of the nature whose present state may itself call for healing — which is why to heal is not to repudiate. Healing serves the nature's own good against a condition that afflicts it; repudiation sets the will against that good itself. The one word nature tends to fuse things the argument must keep apart: the nature common to the kind; the constitutive determinations by which this embodied person is the one he is; the teleological good toward which that nature tends; the contingent present states, afflictions among them, in which the nature may be found; and the self-interpretation by which the person understands all of these. Reception is owed to the first three; the fourth may rightly be acted upon; and the fifth is the site at which the counterfeit can arise if interpretation is made constitutive of what it interprets.

Repudiation has the opposite structure, and the opposition is exact. It acts upon the given not to serve the nature received but because that nature has itself been placed under the judgment of the will. The given is no longer the measure the act serves; it is the defendant the act overrules. And the moment the will sets itself up as the tribunal before which the received nature must justify what it is — the moment what I am must receive its truth from me — the will has claimed the one office it cannot hold. It has tried to make the derivative the source of the actuality it receives. So repudiation is a narrow thing, and three conditions must hold together before an act is one: the reality in question must be genuinely constitutive, not a pathology or an accident; a contrary account of it must be given normative priority over the reality itself; and the act must require the given to conform to that account rather than the account to the given. Where these do not all hold there is no repudiation — error, confusion, suffering, resentment, and mere mistaken interpretation are not it, and must not be called it. That is the counterfeit of reception, and it has a form the whole corpus has been describing under other names: a sign of self-possession laid over a self that was given, an answer put where only an answer was ever possible and authorship never was. Its liturgical opposite is the Fiat — fiat mihi secundum verbum tuum, let it be done to me according to your word — the reception in which the creature's freedom is wholly real and wholly a receiving. Its own name, the one this essay gives it, is Non accipio me.

<br>

## V. The Criterion, and Where It Stops

A criterion for action upon the body follows from the distinction, and it must be stated with its boundary in the same breath, or it becomes exactly the armchair tribunal the essay has just refused to anyone. The criterion is this: an act upon the received nature is reception where it serves the good and order that nature already specifies, and it crosses into repudiation where it requires the will to become the source of a contrary account of what that nature is. The poles are clear. Setting a fracture, treating a disease, correcting a genuine malformation toward the body's own form — these serve the nature received; they answer it. An act whose intelligibility depends on the given nature's having first been overruled, so that the will supplies the account the body is then made to conform to, has the structure of repudiation. The teaching of the Church supplies a doctrinal analogue to this distinction when it distinguishes interventions addressing genuine abnormalities from sex-change interventions, while grounding that distinction in the natural order of the human person and in creation received as gift.

But the criterion sorts the poles, not every case between them, and the honesty the corpus demands everywhere requires saying so here most of all. There are developments — the atypical courses of sexual formation medicine classifies in more than one way among them — where what the body's own order specifies is itself genuinely difficult to read, and where an intervention's standing cannot be settled by a formula applied from a distance. The essay states the structure; it does not pretend the structure reads off, unaided, the right description of every clinical reality, and it will not turn the persons in the hard cases into illustrations of a thesis. Those cases belong to prudence, to medicine answerable to the body's own good, and to pastoral care, and the metaphysics that clears the way is not a substitute for any of them. What the argument establishes is the criterion and its direction. What it declines is the pretense that a criterion discharges the work of discernment.

<br>

## VI. Non accipio me

Name the posture precisely, because its precision is what keeps it from becoming an accusation. Non accipio me — I do not receive myself — is not the presence of a wish, nor the suffering of a conflict, nor the mere fact that something in one's own given nature is experienced as a burden. It is a determinate metaphysical posture: the refusal to receive one's own constitutive actuality as given, and the corresponding attempt to make that actuality answerable to the will for its terms. It is the creature undertaking to be the one before whom its own nature must be justified — which is to undertake the office of the Non-Derivative, author of what things are. The derivative cannot hold that office. Not because it is forbidden to reach for it, though it is, but because the reach is empty: the receiver of being is not the kind of thing that can be the source of the being it receives, and no act of will converts the one into the other.

The distinction that makes this precise is epistemological, and it is the hinge of the whole essay. Interpretation may be authored; its truth-condition is not thereby authored. A person genuinely interprets herself — understands, revises, and sometimes misunderstands what she is — and none of that is the posture; self-interpretation is not the counterfeit but the ordinary work of a mind meeting its own given. The counterfeit does not begin when the creature interprets the given, misunderstands it, suffers under it, or wishes it otherwise. It begins when the creature's interpretation is installed as the source from which the constitutive given must take its truth — when an account is treated as making the reality so, merely because it is willed or affirmed. To understand oneself authors an interpretation; it does not author the thing interpreted, and the inversion is the attempt to collapse that difference.

Everything the method note promised must now be kept, and kept firmly, because this is the section most easily abused. The posture is a structure, and the essay describes the structure. It does not claim to find the structure in any particular person, and it emphatically does not identify it with the experience of conflict between a person and the sexed body — an experience that may be wholly involuntary, long-borne, and unchosen, and that the essay has already granted is real and owed its hearing. Objective structure and personal culpability are different questions, and Catholic theology itself keeps them apart — imputability for an act can be diminished or even removed by ignorance, duress, fear, habit, and other psychological and social factors the tradition names — and the first is all this essay treats. To read a person's heart off a metaphysical diagram would be to commit, in the register of judgment, the very inversion the essay condemns in the register of being — to put the will where only reception belongs, this time the will of the one who would judge. The topology can describe what Non accipio me is. It cannot, and does not, tell anyone where to find it.

<br>

## VII. The Fall, and the Wounded Reception

The Fall supplies no missing premise to the metaphysical argument; that argument is already complete. What the Fall explains is how a creature ordered toward reception can nevertheless become divided against that reception. The posture has a precedent, and naming it rightly matters, because it is easy to name it wrongly. It is sometimes said that after the Fall nature rebelled against man. That cannot be said strictly, for nature has no will with which to rebel. What the tradition teaches is more exact and more to the point: that the creature's own rebellion against the Creator wounded the harmonious reception of himself and of the created order. The Catechism describes the original harmonies shattered — the governance of the body by the spirit wounded, the relation of the sexes disordered, and the harmony with creation broken, so that visible creation has become 'alien and hostile' to man. Human nature is not thereby made evil; it is good, and wounded.

And the primordial temptation has exactly the shape the present essay has been tracing. It was not, at root, to change some particular thing. It was to be 'like God,' the Catechism says, but 'without God, before God, and not in accordance with God' — to hold the office of the source while remaining a creature, to have the terms of one's own good from oneself. That is the generic form of which Non accipio me is a species: the derivative reaching for the office of the Non-Derivative. non serviam, in *Against You Alone*, named the final refusal of the creature's place before God; Non accipio me is that refusal turned inward upon the creature's own given nature — not I will not serve the One who is, but I will not receive the one I am.

From this a last proposition follows, and it must be stated with the same care as the sixth section, because it is the one most liable to be misheard. If the harmony of reception is wounded, then post-lapsarian interiority — the inner sense of how things stand with oneself — cannot by itself be the infallible tribunal over created actuality. This is not a claim that any particular experience is false, nor that sincerity is worth little; it is a claim about what sincerity can and cannot establish. An interior conviction, however deep and however involuntary, settles the reality and the weight of the experience; it does not thereby settle the nature of the actuality the experience is about, because the faculty that reports the conviction is itself among the things the Fall left wounded, and a wounded faculty is a real witness but not an infallible judge. The essay asks interiority to be heard and not to be sovereign — which is only reception again, applied to the inner life: the self received there too, and not authored.

<br>

## VIII. The Will That Answers

The argument reaches its end, and unlike its unpublished predecessor on the adjacent question, it ends closed where the earlier one had to end open — because it never undertook to reconstruct what sexual difference positively is, but only to fix what a creature may do with any constitutive given it did not author, and that it had the primitives to settle. The metaphysical result is firm: the will may answer the given — receive it, serve it, heal it, govern it, suffer it, cultivate it toward its own good — but it cannot become the Ground of the given. No creaturely act makes the nature one has received take its terms from the will that received it, because authorship over a nature is the office of the one who gives being, and the receiver of being does not occupy it by willing to. But it is the general office-question the topology closes, and the limit must be marked as plainly as the result. That sexual difference is itself one such constitutive given — a determination of the received nature and not a construction laid upon it — is not something these primitives establish on their own; it is precisely the determination the unpublished study could not positively reconstruct, and it is the Church that supplies it, teaching that man and woman are good as willed, that each is called to accept the identity received, and that creation is prior to us and must be received as gift. The two accomplishments are therefore distinct and must not be run together: the topology furnishes the metaphysical grammar — the creature cannot author the nature it receives — and the Church identifies the sexual reality to which that grammar applies. The door the loud question wanted left ajar is shut; but it is shut by grammar and application together, and the argument claims for its own primitives only the grammar.

What is closed is a metaphysical office-question, and nothing beyond it is closed by this essay. It pronounces no verdict on any person, measures no one's culpability, and settles none of the pastoral or clinical discernment that the fifth section placed, deliberately, outside its reach. Those who stand in the hardest relation to their own given nature are not thereby shown to be holding the posture the sixth section described; the essay has been at pains to withhold exactly that identification. What it offers them is not a sentence but the same thing it offers everyone: the proposition that the self is received before it is answered, and that this is not a diminishment of freedom but the condition of a dignity no self-making could confer — for a thing one had authored would be, at last, only one's own, while a thing received is given, and the giver is the measure of the gift.

So the title is a refusal, and the essay's last word is the refusal of the refusal. Ipse fecit nos, et non ipsi nos: he made us, and not we ourselves. The creature's freedom is not the authorship of what it is but the answer it returns to what it has been given to be — and the whole height of that freedom is shown not in Non accipio me but in its opposite, the Fiat of the one who received the greatest word ever given to a creature and said, let it be done to me according to it. To receive oneself is not to be passive before oneself. It is to stand, as the only kind of being a creature can be, at the one place it was given, and to answer there — which is the whole of what a creaturely will is for, and a freedom it has precisely because it receives the being it does not supply.

Accipio me; non facio me.
