## Abstract

Two earlier essays in this corpus took positions on the cogito. [*The Am That Remains*](https://oscargaitan.org/ontology/philosophy-of-time/catholic-theology/2026/04/20/the-am-that-remains.html) reversed it: I do not exist because I think; I think because I am. [*The Conserved Cogito*](https://oscargaitan.org/rene-descartes/metaphysical-grounding/divine-attributes/2026/10/15/the-conserved-cogito-en.html) read it as the place where a creature's self-possession and its dependence coincide without rivalry. Neither asked what stands between the two verbs. This essay asks it. It begins from an observation that turns out to prove nothing — that thinking never seems to stop — and from the temptation the observation carries, which is to conclude that to be and to think are one. It refuses that conclusion, and with it a claim the first essay made, that knowing is what the soul is. In its place it proposes, with Augustine and Thomas Aquinas as guides, that the thinking subject is present to itself before any particular thought and without needing one; that this presence is neither a hidden act of thinking nor an identity of the soul with its knowing, but the soul's own being as it stands open to its intellect; and that, given what this corpus has already argued about received existence, it is received together with existence and not achieved. That last step is offered as an application of earlier commitments and not as a new proof. The one who says I is the person, not the soul alone. The and of the title is therefore neither an identity nor an inference. It joins what is received to the operation in which a response can begin: I am given to myself, and I think. How a standing condition of this kind belongs to the one who traverses is recorded as a debt and left unpaid. A note on origins states which claims of the earlier essays are no longer held, and why those essays are left as they were written.

<br>

## A Note on Origins and Terms

This essay has three origins in the corpus, and it does not agree with all of them.

*The Am That Remains* (April 2026) argued that Descartes's therefore pointed the wrong way, that the soul underlies its thinking as a river underlies its current, and that death removes a chain of interference — senses, passions, discursive thought — and leaves the soul knowing itself entire. [*The Mercy of Time*](https://oscargaitan.org/catholic-theology/ontology/philosophy-of-time/2026/05/18/the-mercy-of-time.html) (May 2026) proposed that the self is composed of soul and will, distinguished soul from spirit as two poles of one personal being, and described the soul as a merciful opacity that keeps a human life open to return. *The Conserved Cogito* (October 2026) read Descartes as an ally on the matter of dependence, and distinguished the act of thinking, which is the creature's, from the actuality in which there is an act at all, which is given.

Those essays are published and I have left them as they were written. A corpus that silently repairs its earlier pages hides the path by which its later ones were reached, and the path is part of what a reader is owed. They stand as origins: a map of where the inquiry has been, contradictions included. But a map without a legend misleads, so the corrections are entered here, by name.

Four claims of the earlier essays are no longer held.

First, that knowing is what the soul is when everything temporal has been removed (*The Am That Remains*, III). Section III below gives the reason: the claim identifies a creature's being with its act of understanding, and that identity belongs to God alone.

Second, that the therefore was the error (*The Am That Remains*, I). The therefore is sound as far as it reaches. It establishes certainty; it does not establish ground. *The Conserved Cogito* already read Descartes this way without saying that it was revising the earlier judgment. It was.

Third, that death frees the soul into its own proper mode, the body having been a vehicle and its deliveries an interference (*The Am That Remains*, IV, V, and VIII). Sections V and VI give the reasons.

Fourth, that the self is the soul, an identification the first essay assumed throughout and its last page states. *The Mercy of Time* said a month later that to call the self the soul is too simple. It was right, though the present essay does not follow its remedy.

A word on terms. The word soul has carried two different meanings in these pages. First it named the knower, buried beneath the chain of interference. Later it named the temporal, burden-bearing pole of the person, the opacity itself, while what had once been called the soul came closer to what would later be called the spirit. Here the word is used in neither way. Soul means what Augustine and Aquinas mean by it: the one soul of a human being, the form of the body, which subsists when the body dies, and whose powers include intellect and will. Will, accordingly, is treated here as a power of the soul and not as a second principle beside it. Spirit is not used as a working term. The distinction between soul and spirit proposed in *The Mercy of Time* is not argued against here, and stays where it was proposed; but the two schemes are not both in force. An account on which one soul has intellect and will as its powers cannot also treat the will as a principle beside the soul, or the soul as only the temporal pole of the person, and where the two differ the present essay follows the first. The one who says I is called the person.

What is kept from the earlier essays is named where it is used. The movement of this one is from an observation, through a refusal, to a narrower question: what it is for someone to be present to himself when he is not thinking about himself at all.

As before, what follows is offered as philosophical proposition and not as doctrinal pronouncement. Where it reports the teaching of the Church it says so. Where it reads Augustine and Thomas it reads them as a student, and the readings are open to correction by those who know them better.

## I. The Observation That Cannot Testify

The inquiry began with something noticed, not something argued. Thinking does not seem to stop. A distracting thought can be dismissed, but another takes its place; and in sleep, where nothing is being dismissed or chosen, the dreams come anyway. From there the step is short. If thought never ceases and I never cease, perhaps what I call my thoughts and what I call my soul are one thing, and I am and I think say the same.

The impression is an understandable one, and that is the trouble with it. From the inside, the only instrument I have for detecting my own thought is thought. Whenever I look to see whether I am thinking, I am, because the looking is a case of it. I could never come upon an interval in which thinking was absent and report, from inside that interval, that it was so. The finding is guaranteed by the method, and a finding the method guarantees is not evidence. It would come out the same whether thinking were continuous or full of gaps. Introspection, then, cannot establish that thought is uninterrupted.

Dreams add less than they appear to. They show that something like thought can run without my direction. They do not show that it runs all night, still less that it runs under the surgeon's drug or in those hours of sleep from which nothing is ever recovered. Thought is used in these opening pages in its ordinary broad sense, for whatever passes through the mind; a dream may be more the work of imagination than of intellect. The stricter sense, the act of understanding, comes in with Section III.

So the observation has to give up its place as a premise. What it leaves behind is a question, and the question is better than the premise was. Before turning to it, the short step has to be looked at, because it has been taken before, and by the philosopher this corpus has twice set itself to answer. Descartes held that the mind is a thinking substance and that thought is its essential attribute; pressed by Gassendi, he accepted the consequence that it always thinks, in the womb and in the deepest sleep, though it does not remember. Locke answered that this was a doctrine and not an experience. Thinking, he said, is to the soul what motion is to a body, something it does and not what it is, and a man asleep without dreams gives no sign of doing it. If I conclude from the restlessness of my own mind that to be and to think are the same, I have not gone past Descartes. I have arrived at the res cogitans by another door, and *The Conserved Cogito* said in so many words that the topology does not go there.

There is a second reason to refuse the step, and it is internal to the corpus. *The Am That Remains* rests on one sentence: I am whether I think or not. If being and thinking are the same, that sentence is false.

## II. Three Sentences

It helps to set three sentences side by side.

I think, therefore I am. This is a sentence about certainty. It says that the one who thinks cannot coherently doubt that he exists, since the doubting is his. It moves from what is most evident to me toward what must be the case for anything to be evident to me. It does not say that thinking makes me exist, and Descartes did not take it to; in the Third Meditation he asks what keeps the thinker in being from one moment to the next, and finds the answer outside the thinker.

I think because I am. This is a sentence about being. It says that there must be someone for there to be someone's thought, that the subject is the condition of the act and not its product. It moves in the opposite direction from the first, from what is first in reality toward what follows from it.

The two do not contradict each other, because they put different things in order. What is first for my knowing need not be first in being; I learn of the fire from the smoke without supposing that smoke causes fire. *The Am That Remains* opened by calling the therefore an error. The error was never in the therefore. It lay in a reception of Descartes that asked the sentence to carry more than it carries, and made the certainty of the thinker into the ground of the thinker. The reversal that essay proposed can stand. Its accusation cannot.

I think and I am. This is the sentence the present essay is named for, and at first it looks like the weakest of the three, a conjunction where the others have an argument. If it meant only that both things are true, it would not be worth an essay. It is worth one because of what the other two leave unasked. The first tells me how I am sure of myself. The second tells me which of the two comes first. Neither tells me how the one who is and the one who thinks are the same one to himself. Two questions are folded together there, and they should be separated. One is what makes a thought mine. The other is how I can know it as mine without having to find that out. The first has a short answer, which this essay assumes and does not argue: a thought is mine because it is an operation of my intellect, as a step is mine because my legs take it, whether I meant to take it or stumbled. Belonging in this sense is attribution and nothing more. A thought can be mine, as occurring in my intellectual life, without my having chosen it and without my assenting to what it says. Authorship and endorsement are further matters, and they involve the will. The second is the question of this essay. For a thought to be recognized as mine without inference, I must already be available to myself before the recognizing; and what that availability is, when no recognizing is going on, is what has to be explained. That is the question of self-presence. It is a question about what is the case, and not about how things feel. How things feel raised it. Only an account of what a thinking subject is can answer it.

## III. What the Earlier Essay Claimed Too Much

*The Am That Remains* gave an answer to this question before the question had been asked. The answer was that the soul's knowing is not something it has but something it is: "knowing is what the soul is when everything temporal has been removed." Under the noise of sensation, passion, and reasoning the soul was said to be "knowing all the while," and death was said to remove the noise.

I wrote that sentence meaning it literally, as an identity between the soul's being and an act of knowing. It cannot be kept.

The same essay had used the image of the river and its current to say that an activity can cease while its subject remains. Actual knowing, the understanding of something here and now, is an activity. If the image holds for discursive thought, it holds for any act of understanding whatever, and the essay cannot say in its first section that the subject is not its activity and in its third that the subject is its highest activity. The two sections give two accounts of the soul. They were never reconciled, because the word thinking was allowed to mean reasoning in the one place and something wider in the other.

Thomas Aquinas is exact on the point, and against me. The soul's essence is not its power to understand, and the power is not its act (Summa Theologiae I, q. 77, a. 1). He says the same of the angels, whose understanding is not discursive at all: an angel's act of understanding is not its substance, and neither is its power (I, q. 54, aa. 1–3). Only in God are to be and to understand the same. The earlier essay appealed to the angels to describe what the separated soul is freed into, and in doing so it claimed for a human soul after death what Thomas declines to say even of an angel.

Something true was being reached for, and it should be stated plainly so that it is not lost with the overstatement. The soul does not stop being an intellectual subject when its bodily operations stop. And it does not have to assemble its acquaintance with itself out of experience, as it assembles its knowledge of the world. Those two things stand. What does not stand is the passage from them to the claim that the soul's existing consists in an act of knowing that never pauses.

That leaves a gap exactly where the essay had placed its strongest claim. If the subject is not its thinking, and is not a hidden and uninterrupted knowing either, then in what sense is it there to itself in the hours when it is not thinking of itself, and in the hours when it is not thinking at all?

## IV. Present, Not Absent

Consider ordinary cases before the authorities. A woman works for an hour on a column of figures, and in that hour she does not once think of herself. Then the telephone rings and she says, I'll get it. She did not have to look for the one who would get it. Nothing in the hour had been about her, and yet when the moment came she was not news to herself. Or take waking. No one wakes and then works out who it is that has woken. The first thought of the morning may be about the weather or the hour; it arrives already belonging to someone who does not need to be introduced.

These cases show a difference between two things that the word self-knowledge runs together. There is thinking about myself, which is an act, begins and ends, and is rather rare. And there is the standing fact that I am available to myself, on which the act draws when it occurs, and which does not begin when the act begins. The cases show that the difference is real. They do not show what the standing fact consists in, and for that the authorities are needed.

Augustine drew this line and gave it names. In the tenth book of De Trinitate he observes that the mind, told to know itself, does not go looking for itself as for something absent; it is asked to attend to what is already there. It is one thing, he says, for the mind not to know itself and another for it not to think of itself: a man who knows grammar does not cease to know it while his mind is on medicine (X.5.7). He returns to the point in the fourteenth book, where the mind is said to remember, understand, and love itself even when its attention is wholly elsewhere. Nosse is one thing and cogitare another. The mind always knows itself in the first sense, and only sometimes in the second.

Aquinas takes Augustine's always and asks what kind of always it is. His answer, as I read him, is that it is habitual and not actual: the mind does not always actually understand itself, but it is always in a condition to do so (I, q. 93, a. 7, ad 4). In the disputed question on the mind he is more precise, and the precision matters for this essay more than any other single text. There are, he says, two ways in which the soul knows that it is. Actually, it perceives that it exists by perceiving its own acts: a man notices that he has a soul, that he lives, that he is, in noticing that he senses and understands. Habitually, it knows itself through its own essence, because its essence is present to it, and from that presence it is able to pass into the act of perceiving itself. For this, he adds, no habit is required; the essence of the soul, present to the mind, is enough (De Veritate, q. 10, a. 8). The Summa says the same more briefly: for the mind to perceive that it is, its own presence suffices, since that presence is the principle of the act in which it perceives itself (I, q. 87, a. 1).

Three things follow, and each corrects something.

The first is that self-presence is not an act, and so it is not a state of consciousness. It is not a faint cogito running underneath the explicit ones, and it is not a dim awareness of oneself that persists through sleep. Nothing is being performed in the woman at her figures by which she keeps herself in view. Three things have to be held apart: the soul's presence to itself, which is a fact about what it is; the capacity for actual self-knowledge, which that presence founds; and conscious awareness of oneself, which is an act and comes and goes. The first does not entail the third. This removes the temptation of the opening observation at its root. What abides is not thought, so its abiding gives no reason to say that thought never stops.

The second is that self-presence is not an extra piece of equipment either. It is not a habit acquired, and not a faculty beside the intellect. The soul's being, as that being stands open to the soul's own power of understanding, is the whole of it; and by stands open I mean only what Thomas means, that the essence is there as the principle from which an act of self-knowledge can arise. I do not mean that the soul is already aware of itself in some lower key. So self-presence adds no constituent to the soul. The term names the soul's essence considered as present to its own intellect, and it is needed only to mark that standing fact off from actual self-knowledge. How essence and power are related beyond this is a question Thomas treats at length, and this essay leans on his distinction without adding to it. This is where the earlier essay's intuition was pointing. It said that the soul's knowledge of itself is uncovered and not built, and that much is right: there is nothing to build. What it got wrong was to call the unbuilt thing an act of knowing.

The third is a limit. In this life the soul's presence to itself yields actual knowledge only through its acts, and its acts in this life depend on what the senses supply. Thomas compares the human intellect, in the order of intelligible things, to bare potentiality; it becomes intelligible to itself by being brought into act by something other than itself (I, q. 87, a. 1). So I am always present to myself, in the sense just given, and never transparent to myself. That I am, I know with a certainty nothing shakes whenever I turn to it; I do not hold that certainty as a continuous possession, since the turning is an act. What I am, I learn slowly, by inference, and often wrongly. My acts leave traces, and through them I learn something of myself. Presence is given; its interpretation is work.

What this gives the question of Section II is limited, and should be stated at its limit. Self-presence does not itself recognize anything. It is what makes it possible for a thought to be recognized as mine without inference when recognition occurs, because the one who recognizes does not first have to be found. Whether it is all that such recognition requires, I do not claim.

## V. Who Says I

So far the essay has spoken of the soul and of the subject as if they were the same. They are not quite the same, and the first essay's last page, which speaks of "the self — which was never the thinking," took for granted that the self that remains is the soul. The question has to be put directly. Who is it that says I am?

Thomas's answer has two parts, and they have to be taken together. The soul is not the man (I, q. 75, a. 4). Elsewhere he says outright that the soul of Peter is not Peter, and, commenting on Paul, that my soul is not I; and he denies that the separated soul is a person, since it remains a part of human nature even when it subsists alone (I, q. 29, a. 1, ad 5). Yet the soul has the act of existing in its own right and shares that one existence with the body, so that the whole human being is by the soul's being; and when the body dies, the same existence remains in the soul (I, q. 76, a. 1, ad 5). After death, then, the soul is not a different thing from the one who lived. It is that same one's own subsisting principle, with his knowledge, his history, and his will in its final direction, and it is incomplete.

The faith confirms this from another side. At death, the Church teaches, each one is judged, and the soul receives at once what its life has made it ready to receive. That is doctrine and not my proposition. Now the one who is judged must be the one who acted. If the soul that stands condemned were another than the human being who sinned, it could fairly say: he did it, not I. No one supposes it can say that. There is no second party to blame. This does not explain the continuity between the one who lived and the soul that is judged. It requires it, and any account of the soul has to meet the requirement. Thomas's account meets it, because there are not two existences for accountability to pass between.

But the requirement is for one subject, one who, before death and after. It is not a requirement that the soul be the whole of what that subject is, and the argument from judgment, when pressed, asks for more than the soul. The acts that are judged were done by an embodied human being, with hands and a tongue. If the one who acted must be the one who answers, then the whole one must answer, and this is among the reasons the tradition gives for the resurrection of the body. The survival of the soul is not that resurrection. An account on which I simply am my soul makes the resurrection an addition to me, where the faith speaks of it as the restoring of me.

This is why the body cannot be what *The Am That Remains* called it, a vehicle set down at the end. No one stands in that relation to what he is. And it is why the chain of interference has to be described again. Senses, passions, and the slow work of reasoning are how a human being knows anything, himself included. They are the human way of being an intellect, and not a fog lying over some other way. *The Mercy of Time* had already seen this from another side. It called the opacity of temporal life a mercy and not a defect: because we do not know all at once, we do not choose all at once, and what is not chosen all at once can be chosen again. That essay placed the opacity in the soul. I would now place it in the embodied condition as such. The insight is kept; its location is corrected.

So the first essay's identification is withdrawn, and what replaces it is deliberately less than a theory. The one who says I is the person. In this life he says it as a whole human being, by his soul. Between death and resurrection there is no second speaker and no new one: the soul that subsists is his, exists by the existence that was his, and answers for what he did. Whether that soul is then to be called the person, or is that of the person which remains, is disputed among those who follow Thomas, and this essay does not decide it. It holds only what the foregoing requires: one I, not two, and a soul that is not the whole of it.

One consequence should be stated, because the essay moves between the two terms. The presence described in Section IV belongs immediately to the intellectual soul, since it is the soul's essence that is present to the soul's intellect. It belongs to the person through that soul, which is the form of the body and not a second someone residing in it. The soul is not another subject whose self-presence is afterwards credited to the person. It is the form through which the person exists and understands, and so the soul's presence to its own intellect is the principle by which the person is present to himself. Thomas says that it is more correct to say the man understands by his soul than that the soul understands, as it is the man who sees, by his eyes (I, q. 75, a. 2, ad 2). By the same reasoning, though the application is mine and not his, it is the human being who is present to himself, by the soul's presence to itself. When this essay says that the person is given to himself, that is the sense intended.

## VI. What Death Uncovers

If the first essay's picture of death is withdrawn, something has to be said in its place, and Thomas's question on the knowledge of the separated soul says it with more restraint than I showed.

He does not describe separation as a release into the soul's proper mode. To be united to a body belongs to the soul's nature, and to understand by turning to what the senses and the imagination provide is its natural way of understanding. To be without the body is, in the words of that question, beside its nature, praeter naturam; elsewhere he uses the harder word, against. The separated soul understands in another manner, by likenesses it receives from above, after the manner of the separate substances. But because its intellect is the weakest of intellects, what it receives in that manner it holds, by its natural power, only generally and confusedly (I, q. 89, a. 1). This is not the knowing-entire that the earlier essay attributed to the separated soul.

Two further things in the same question cut against the earlier essay. What was learned in life remains: the knowledge acquired here, and the use of it, are not lost with the body (I, q. 89, aa. 5–6), and Thomas allows a memory that belongs to the intellect itself (I, q. 79, a. 6). So the contrast I drew between direct apprehension and memory was too sharp; the separated soul is not without its past. And the natural knowledge of the separated soul is not the vision of God. When Paul says, then I shall know even as I am known, the tradition hears a promise of that vision, which no created intellect reaches by its own nature, in the body or out of it. The earlier essay let the one slide into the other. They have to be kept apart. What the soul can know by being a soul is one thing. What it is given to see is another, and is grace.

Yet one sentence in that question gives back, in a truer form, what the first essay wanted. The separated soul, Thomas says, understands itself through itself (I, q. 89, a. 2). In this life the soul's presence to itself becomes actual knowledge only by way of its acts and of what the senses give those acts to work on. Separated, it no longer turns to the senses, and it is itself an intelligible thing, present to its own intellect. What was presence becomes sight. The phrase needs its limit. It names a change in the manner of knowing, from knowing oneself through one's acts to knowing oneself directly. It does not say that the soul then knows itself exhaustively, or sees the whole of its history at once, and it is not the vision of God.

This is what "uncovered" should have meant. It is not that an act of knowing had been running all along beneath the noise and is at last heard. No hidden act of self-knowing had been running beneath the others. It is that what had always been there to be known, the soul to itself, and had been known only in glimpses and through its works, is now known directly. The change is from being able to being so. Nor is it, as the first essay had it, a liberation without loss. The one who knows himself in this way is not a whole human being. He is in a condition his nature was not made for, knowing himself apart from the embodied way of knowing that is natural to him, though not apart from what he learned and became by it.

One further thing must be distinguished. The Church teaches that death ends the time in which grace can be accepted or refused (Catechism, 1021). *The Mercy of Time* proposed a reason: that what was open in us was open because we lived and chose in succession. That proposal stands or falls on its own. Nothing in the present section supports it, since the separated soul's knowledge of itself has just been described as direct and not as complete, and I do not offer that knowledge as the cause of the soul's fixity. What the soul finds when it knows itself, whether joy or ruin, is not decided by the knowing. It was decided at the crossings.

## VII. The Topology's Part

Augustine and Thomas have supplied the terms. If the essay stopped here it would be a report on them and a correction of myself. The topology owes something of its own, and it concerns the relation between this self-presence and what *The Conserved Cogito* called the given.

That essay made a cut along one axis. In any act of thinking, the act — this determinate turning of a mind — is the creature's own; the actuality in which there is an act at all is received. The present inquiry has cut along another axis: the subject, its power, its standing presence to itself, its acts. The two cuts are not rivals, but the first does not do the work of the second. That essay called the cogito the summit of creaturely self-possession and said nothing of what the summit stands on. It can now be said. The cogito is an act. The self-presence it draws on is not a second and quieter cogito, and it is not an operation the creature keeps up. So the question that essay asked of the act can be asked again one level down. Whose is the self-presence, and where does it come from?

The proposition I offer is that it is received, and received in the same giving by which the person is. Thomas says that the soul's essence is present to it; he does not say what follows, and the step is mine. The argument is not self-contained. Its conclusion depends on the account of creaturely dependence developed in [*The Ground That Knows*](https://oscargaitan.org/philosophical%20theology/metaphysics/ontology/2026/07/27/the-ground-that-knows.html) and *The Conserved Cogito*. Without that account, the first step below would establish only that self-presence is not produced by reflection; it would not establish that it is received. There are three steps.

The first step is that self-presence is not produced by the subject. Suppose it were: suppose I became present to myself by an act of turning toward myself. To turn toward myself, and not merely toward some object that happens to be me, I would have to know already which one to turn toward. I would need the very acquaintance the act was supposed to supply. An act of reflection can find me only because I was not lost to begin with. That is the philosophical form of Augustine's remark that the mind does not seek itself as absent. Reflection presupposes more than that I exist; it presupposes that I am available to be recognized as myself, and so it cannot be what first makes me so. That is not an independent refutation of every account on which self-recognition arises within the reflexive act itself. It follows from the account of habitual self-presence adopted in Section IV: the reflection does not produce the presence that makes it possible.

The second step is that what is not produced by the subject is received. By itself this does not follow. A feature may belong to a thing simply because of the kind of thing it is, without being achieved and without being a separate gift. The step holds only on a premise brought from outside this essay: that the creature's actuality is received at every instant, and received all the way down, so that nothing in the creature's being exists independently of the giving, though the creature's powers and operations remain its own. This is the premise taken from the two earlier essays.

The third step is that it is received together with existence, and not as a second thing added. This follows from Section IV, if Section IV is right. If self-presence required a habit, or an act, or a faculty of its own, it could be given separately and later. If it is nothing over and above the soul's being as that being is open to its own intellect, then there is nothing separate to give. To be created an intellectual subject is to be handed to oneself. To say that I am given to myself is not to describe an experience of being given, nor an act by which I take possession of myself. It is to say that an intellectual subject receives its existence according to a nature whose essence is present to its own intellect. The gift is one: the existence of a subject of this kind. By the same giving I mean one creative giving, the same by which the creature is made and by which it is kept in being. I do not mean that essence, existence, and self-presence are one and the same. They remain distinguishable, as a creature's nature and its act of existing remain distinguishable though it never has the one without the other. Existence is received according to the nature it is the existence of; and for a nature of this kind, to exist is to be present to itself. The first half of that sentence is Thomas's; the second is my reading of Section IV.

The result is therefore conditional. If creaturely existence is received, and if self-presence is nothing other than the being of an intellectual subject as available to itself, then self-presence is received with existence. On those conditions, the argument reaches three levels. The actuality of each act is given. The being of the one who acts is given. And the presence of that one to himself, from which any saying of I begins, is given with the being. At none of these depths does the giving take anything from the creature. *The Conserved Cogito* found that the act is no less mine for being held in being. Self-presence is the plainer case: there is no one it could belong to but me, and I did nothing to get it.

What does this add to what Thomas already says? Less than a new doctrine. It carries the argument of *The Conserved Cogito* one level down. The picture that essay wrote against treated the cogito as the one thing the self holds without owing it. A subtler form of the same picture would concede the act and retreat to what the act stands on: my thinking may be held in being, but my being there for myself, at least, is my own doing. On the conditions stated, it is not. The root of self-possession is not self-originated either.

This is what the and holds together. I am: that is what is given, and it includes my being present to myself. I think: that is the operation, and it is mine. Not every thought is an answer. This essay began from thoughts that arrive unbidden, and a dream or an intrusive thought is an operation of my mind without being anything I chose to do with myself. But in thinking I can apprehend and judge what I was given, and through the will, which is another power of the same soul, I can turn toward it or away. Neither operation constitutes the one whose operations they are. So the and joins neither a premise to a conclusion nor a thing to itself. It joins what is received to the operation in which a response can begin. I am given to myself, and I think.

Two consequences for the topology should be marked, one a gain, the other a debt.

The gain is that the priority in question is not a priority in time. Self-presence does not precede my first thought as one event precedes another, because it is not an event. At every Now in which I think, it is there as that from which the thinking can be known as mine. If there is a Now in which I do not think, as there seems to be in sleep without dreams or under the surgeon's drug, the person is still held in being and is still the one who will wake as himself. Much goes on in him then; what is not required is any continuing act of reflection by which he keeps hold of himself. The subject's presence to itself is not repeatedly produced or extinguished by its changing acts of understanding. Its exercise and its clarity vary, across a day and across a life.

The debt is this. The topology has tended to describe what is true of a continuant in terms of what occurs at the crossing: reception, turning, condensation. Self-presence, as described here, is true of the continuant and does not occur. It stands; it does not happen. Nor is it what the corpus calls structural inertia, or condensation. Those are what acts leave behind, the past carried in the one who crosses. Self-presence is not left behind by any act, since it is prior to the first: prior in the order of what an act presupposes, and not as an earlier stretch of time. The figure has a place for what is carried. It has not yet said how something that was never an act and never an event belongs to the one who traverses. I record that as owed, and do not pay it here.

## VIII. I Think and I Am

The opening observation was that thinking never stops. It did not survive as evidence, and what it was offered as evidence for was false. But it was a true perception of something, and the essay can now say of what. What does not stop is not the thinking. It is the one to whom every thought, when it comes, arrives as his own. The thoughts are many and successive, and whether they are continuous is neither established nor required for the continuity of the one who thinks. The one who is there for them remains the same through their succession without being made up of it, and he mistook his own continuity for theirs.

The boundaries of the claim should be drawn before it is left. The essay holds that the soul does not always think, and that it is not its knowing. Its claim that self-presence is received is conditional on what earlier essays argued about received existence. It has not settled how the separated soul stands to the person, nor paid the debt named in the last section. It has not replaced the essays it corrects. They remain where they are, as the first drawings of something that needed more than one.

What it has proposed is small enough to state in a breath. I do not exist because I think. I am not my thinking, and I am not a thinking that never ends. I am a person, given being and, in the same giving, given to myself; and from there, when I think, the thought can be known as mine. Descartes's sentence tells me how I can be sure that I am. The reversal tells me which comes first. The conjunction tells me what the two words have to do with each other: the first names what I received, and the second names the operation in which I can begin to do something with it.

I am, and I did not make that so. I think, and that is mine. The and between them marks one part of a creature's situation, and perhaps the first: to have been handed oneself, and to be able to answer.
