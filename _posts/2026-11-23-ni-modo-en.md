## A Note on Method

What follows is offered as philosophical proposition, not doctrinal pronouncement; its readings of Scripture serve as illustrations of an argument rather than as exegesis. The following are granted here rather than argued: that the necessary source conceded at the outset is someone and not merely something; that the distinction between an originating cause and a sustaining ground, developed elsewhere in this corpus, is real; and, from that same prior work, that the creature's actuality remains dependent upon its source at every moment it is had. The essay does not set out to demonstrate that continuing dependence here; it asks what the concession that there must have been someone has, and has not, recognized.

<br>

## I. The Concession

There is a way of arriving at God that sounds less like discovery than like surrender. Ask someone how they know that God exists, and the answer often comes with a small shrug: Something must have made all this. There is no other way. Ni modo.

Ni modo is one of the phrases a language keeps for the moment a person stops resisting. It is not despair, and it is not quite peace. It is the sound of someone laying down an argument he cannot win — there is no alternative, so I accept it. Turned toward God, it names a real terminus of thought. The mind follows the demand for an explanation as far as it will go, finds that it cannot rest in anything that would only need explaining in turn, and concedes: there must have been someone.

I am not going to question whether that conclusion is true. Let us grant it. The inference to a source that does not depend on what it originates may be the opening of a perfectly serious metaphysics. The question here is narrower, and I think sharper: what, exactly, has been conceded? A mind can reach the end of an argument and still not have understood what stands at the end of it. Ni modo gives us the human weather of the concession — the reluctance, the shrug, the relief. It does not tell us whether the one who shrugs has grasped what he has agreed to.

One reading I want to set aside at once. It is tempting to hear ni modo as a confession of weak faith: assent given grudgingly, and therefore given poorly. That does not hold, and this essay will not lean on it. A person may say there must have been someone with resignation, or with wonder, or with gratitude, or with a conviction deeper than any proof. The manner of arrival tells us nothing reliable about the quality of what arrives. So the essay's concern is not the one who concedes but the thing conceded — and what, in conceding it, he may have left unasked.

The difficulty is not in the mood. It is in the tense.

<br>

## II. The Hidden Assumption

There must have been someone.

Listen to the verb. Must have been sets the needed act in a finished past. It pictures an origin that happened, an act that concluded, and a world then handed over to carry on by itself. The Creator is invoked then — at the beginning, to start things — and the creature, once started, continues on what it was given.

I do not say that everyone who speaks this way holds that picture. The grammar permits the picture; it does not force it. A person can use the past tense and believe without hesitation that God holds the world in being at every instant. The quarrel is not with the speaker but with the conception the tense can conceal: the quiet assumption that existence, once granted, becomes a possession — something the creature now has, rather than something it is still being given.

That assumption is the whole matter. If existence were an inheritance — received once, owned thereafter — then the Creator would indeed belong to the past, and the past tense would be exactly right. But if existence is not owned, if the creature stands at this moment in the very dependence it stood in at the first, then the past tense has misfiled the most important fact about it. It has put at the beginning what belongs to the present.

See what has happened. The one who says there must have been someone has recognized a necessity. He has not yet asked what that necessity means now. These are different recognitions, and the distance between them is the subject of everything that follows.

<br>

## III. The Ground and the Ladder

It helps to pull apart what is usually run together. There is, first, a fact; and there is, second, a set of ways a creature may stand toward that fact. They are not rungs of one scale. The first is the floor the scale stands on.

The fact — call it the ground — is that the creature is not the source of its own existence: it has its existence as something received, not as something it supplies itself. This is no achievement of the mind. It is true of the philosopher and the stone alike, true before anyone reflects and true of those who never do. Call this the creature's ontological dependence in its barest form: that it is not the author of its own being.

Resting on that ground, and only for a creature that can reflect, there is a ladder of response. The first is compelled assent: acknowledging that a Creator must exist, the mind conceding the necessity. The second is metaphysical recognition: understanding what the concession leaves open — whether the actuality the creature now has, once received, is thereafter independently possessed, or remains at each moment dependent upon its source. The third is recognition in faith: the creature's assent to God who reveals Himself, an assent of trust and obedience, moved by grace, and not resting upon the creature's power to articulate the metaphysical grounds of its own existence.

These three mark distinctions in what may be recognized, not stages every mind must pass through in turn. Faith may be present where metaphysical understanding has never been reached. Here is the proposition the essay turns on. The creature cannot choose whether its existence depends upon its Creator. But recognizing that dependence, understanding it, and responding to the Creator are not the same thing. The dependence is real without the creature's consent; recognition, understanding, and response do not follow automatically from it.

It is worth seeing two ways the response can fall short, for they fail at different places, and neither is a simple refusal. The ni modo formulation stops at the first: the concession that a Creator must exist, with the question of what the dependence means left unasked — not, necessarily, refused, only not yet taken up. The other case is more disturbing. The letter of James says that even the demons believe, and shudder; they have gone far past the bare concession, and their knowledge is vivid enough to make them tremble. Yet it does not become the trusting assent by which a creature turns toward God. Recognition, however vivid, does not by itself become charity. The demons do not occupy a rung of this ladder; their case illuminates by contrast the distance that can remain between knowing and responding in faith.

This is also where I would be fair to the one who asks for evidence. When Thomas refused to believe the Resurrection until he saw, Christ answered by presenting Himself and inviting him to believe, yet blessed those who would believe without seeing. The episode shows that sensory confirmation is not a universal condition of faith; it does not settle the separate question of whether metaphysical understanding must precede belief.

<br>

## IV. The Believer Who Does Not Ask

Set beside the philosopher a different figure: the soul who has believed, loved, prayed, and forgiven for a lifetime, and who has never once asked what esse means, or whether its own actuality is received anew at each moment. Is something missing from such a faith?

Nothing that faith requires. And this is where the essay must guard itself most carefully, because all of the above could be misheard as making metaphysical insight a condition of standing rightly before God. It is not. Hold the three apart: ontological dependence, metaphysical recognition, recognition in faith. The humble believer may wholly lack the second and wholly possess the third. His faith is not an absence of intellect — it is the intellect's assent to God who reveals, moved by grace; what it lacks is only the articulation of the metaphysical grounds. The philosopher's understanding is a good; it is not a requirement; and it is never the price of faith.

The reason lies in the ground. A creature does not begin to depend upon God at the moment it recognizes God. Its dependence was never produced by its awareness of it. The believer who could not explain how his existence is received depends upon God no less than the one who can — no less for his silence, and the philosopher no more for his speech. Awareness does not constitute the dependence. At best it perceives it.

So this is not a rung in the ladder but a railing along it, set there to keep the ladder from being read as a ranking of souls. What the humble believer lacks is the articulation. What he has is the reality itself — the dependence, and the One upon whom it rests — that the articulation would only labor to describe.

<br>

## V. The Question That Will Not Close

Now the tense returns, and it can no longer be heard innocently.

If my existence was received — given to me, not generated by me — what does it mean that its actuality remains dependent upon its source at every moment it is had? The question concerns whether what was received can thereafter be independently possessed, or whether its actuality remains dependent upon the source from which it is received: a question about the present, not about a vanished beginning.

I will not demonstrate that continuing dependence here. Demonstrating it would require taking up the metaphysics of existential inertia — whether a thing, once actual, continues without requiring a further sustaining cause, or whether its actuality remains dependent upon that cause at every moment it is had. That inquiry has its own work, and its own place to do it. What belongs here is only that the question is real, that it was always real, and that there must have been someone was never enough to put it to rest — not because the conclusion is false, because the initial concession leaves the present unrecognized.

<br>

## VI. Ni modo

There must have been someone.

That is where we began; it is true, and it is not where the matter ends. The sentence looks backward, toward an origin, and files the whole question under the past. What it never asks is what the necessity it concedes should mean for my existence now.

I do not begin to depend upon God when I recognize Him. My existence was never independent of Him while I failed to recognize it. The dependence I had pictured as belonging only to the beginning is the condition of my existence now.

And here the sentence must finally give way, not to resignation before the possibility that someone, long ago, must have made me, but to something the past tense could not hold. Whether someone must have been there is only the beginning of the question. The deeper question is whether I am, even now, receiving what I cannot give myself.
